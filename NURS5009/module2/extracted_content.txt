Introduction
-
-
Early childhood development is a multidimensional journey, with each phase marked by unique physiological and anatomical characteristics. Understanding these intricacies is vital for all healthcare professionals, especially nurses. Recognising the variations in physiology and anatomy between children and adults enables us to offer more effective and personalised care to our paediatric patients. This topic will explore the stages of child development and their key milestones. We will conclude with an interactive activity exploring the essential anatomical and physiological differences between children and adults, enhancing our ability to provide comprehensive person-centred care.
Stages of child development
Early childhood development is multidimensional, and includes physical, social, emotional and mental aspects of a child's wellbeing. Healthy development generally takes place in a series of predictable and common stages: As they grow older and their brain matures, children learn increasingly complex skills and become progressively more independent.
Although individual children develop at different rates, and with different parenting and early educational cultural and contextual differences, all children benefit from adequate and enriching environments in which they can survive, thrive and develop to their fullest potential (UNICEF, 2023). The Convention on the Rights of the Child clearly highlights the importance of early child development, stating that a child has a right to develop to “the maximum extent possible” (article 6).
At each stage of development, children gain skills in four main domains. These are Communication and speech, physical, social and emotional, and cognitive. All of these areas of development interlink at every stage.
You will recall that in Module 1, each stage of childhood development was presented in detail, including:
- Early childhood (0-5 years),
- Middle childhood (6-12 years)
- Adolescence (13-18 years)
To recap: early childhood is time of rapid change, broken down into developmental stages of the newborn (0-3 months), the infant (3-12 months), the toddler (1-3 years) the preschooler (3-4 years) and the school aged child (4-5 years). Middle childhood involves the consolidation of new skills and large developments with friendships, language, logic, knowledge and understanding. Adolescence is a period of rapid and significant physical and cognitive development. It is also a important in the development of ethical and moral principles, and is a time of separating from parents.

===== FILE: 08 - Developmental milestones.epub =====
Developmental milestones
-
-
What are developmental milestones?
Child development sets the foundation for lifelong learning, behaviour, and health. The experiences children have across childhood shape the brain and the child's capacity to learn, to get along with others, and to respond to daily stresses and challenges.
Children learn more during their first 5 years than at any other time of their lives. Tracking how they reach 'milestones' is one way that nurses and health professionals assess whether children are developing as expected.
Developmental milestones are essential abilities – things such as moving, seeing, hearing, communicating and interacting with other people, that babies and young children achieve as they begin to engage with the world.
Child and family health nurses and doctors check whether babies and children are reaching some of the following milestones:
- physical: these milestones include large body movements, such as sitting and walking, and small body movements like controlling their hands, eating and sleeping, reflexes, vision and other physical abilities
- social: how they react to other people around them, including through play and communication
- emotional: how well they are bonding with other people, whether they are happy or not
- cognitive: their learning, how their senses are working, and how they are engaging with the world
- Language: these include hearing, making sounds, and understanding sounds
When should a child meet developmental milestones?
Babies grow and develop rapidly - especially in the first 12 months of life. As noted, babies will reach milestones at different times. If a baby misses a milestone altogether, this may indicate that their development is delayed.
The earlier the developmental delay is detected, the earlier the correct intervention can be started to promote the best developmental outcomes for the child.
What are some of the problems that can occur with early child development?
Even when babies are slow to reach a certain milestone, in most cases, it will only be a temporary delay. Signs that a baby may not be developing normally include:
- they don't seem to hear or see properly (they may not focus, follow things with their eyes or respond to sounds)
- they are still holding their fingers in a tight fist at 3 months
- they aren't moving both arms or both legs
- they can't hold their head up by 3 to 4 months
- they are unhappy or unsettled most of the time at about 4 months
- they aren't reaching for objects by 6 months
- they don't sit well by 10 months
- they aren't babbling or using sounds by 10 months
- they don't want to stand up by 12 months
- they don't seem to understand anything you say by 18 months
- they don't seem interested in the world around them

Family-centred care
Family and patient centered care is an essential part of healthcare for all consumers, particularly our paediatric patients. Children and adolescents exist within a distinct social environment compared to adults, and their primary sources of assistance and voice are often their families and parents. Collaborating closely with the child and their family is of utmost significance, and taking into account their viewpoints and contributions is crucial when making clinical decisions.
Research suggests that engaging in healthcare decision-making and being actively involved in one's own health results in improved outcomes and higher levels of care.

===== FILE: 17 - Charter on the Rights of Children and Young People in Healthcare Services.epub =====
Charter on the Rights of Children and Young People in Healthcare Services
-
-
The Charter on the Rights of Children and Young people in Healthcare Services has eleven principles. These principles are an element of family / patient centered as it is well known that building effective partnerships contributes to a safe and high quality healthcare system. The charter provides a shared understanding among healthcare providers, patients, consumers, families, and caretakers regarding the rights of individuals of all ages who are receiving healthcare in Australia.
Every child and young person has a right to:
1. To consider their best interests as the primary concern of all involved in their care
2. To express their views, and to be heard and taken seriously
3. To the highest attainable standard of healthcare
4. To respect themselves as a whole person, as well as respect for their family and the family’s individual characteristics, beliefs, culture and contexts.
5. To be nurtured by their parents and family, and to have family relationships supported by the service in which the child or young person is receiving healthcare
6. To provide information in a form that is understandable to them
7. To participate in decision making and, as appropriate to their capabilities, to make decisions about their care
8. To be kept safe from all forms of harm
9. To have their privacy respected
10. To participate in education, play, creative activities and recreation, even if this is difficult due to their illness or disability
11. To ensure continuity of healthcare, including well-planned care that takes them beyond the paediatric context.

Effects of hospitalisation
The way children and adolescents handle hospitalisation can be influenced by various elements such as their developmental stage, separation from their caretaker, exposure to new people and situations, and being in an unfamiliar environment.
There are several methods to reduce the adverse consequences of hospitalisation and offer support to children and young people to help them comprehend and manage their situation better. Family-centred and patient-centred care is an essential element in reducing the negative effects of hospitalisation.
