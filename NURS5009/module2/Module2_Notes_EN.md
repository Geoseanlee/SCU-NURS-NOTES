## 1. Conception, Pregnancy and the Newborn

### Conception and pregnancy

Child development begins at conception and continues through adolescence. Pregnancy
provides the biological foundation for later growth and development, so nurses should
consider maternal health, fetal development, family context and access to support.

**Study prompts:**
- When is a delivery considered full-term?
- What are the three stages of labour?
- What are the defining features of each stage?

### Stages of labour

| Stage | Main events |
|---|---|
| **First stage** | Labour begins with regular contractions and ends when the cervix is fully dilated. It includes early/latent and active labour. |
| **Second stage** | Full cervical dilation to birth of the baby. The birthing person pushes and the baby is delivered. |
| **Third stage** | Birth of the baby to delivery of the placenta and membranes. |

**Nursing priorities:** monitor the birthing person and fetus according to local policy,
support comfort and informed participation, maintain privacy and dignity, recognise
abnormal findings, and escalate concerns promptly.

### Newborn care

Newborn care includes supporting feeding, warmth, bonding, elimination, safe sleep and
ongoing assessment. Care should be adapted to the newborn's physiological immaturity and
to the family's knowledge, culture and preferences.

**Breastfeeding education:**
- Support early parent-infant contact and responsive feeding.
- Observe attachment and positioning and provide practical, non-judgemental support.
- Assess feeding effectiveness, hydration and output.
- Refer concerns about feeding, weight or supply according to local pathways.

**Sleep safety:**
- Place the infant on their back for every sleep.
- Use a firm, flat, separate sleep surface with no loose bedding, pillows or soft toys.
- Keep the infant's face and head uncovered and avoid overheating.
- Discuss smoke-free environments and safe room-sharing with families.

## 2. The First 2000 Days of Life

The first 2000 days, from conception through the early years, are a critical period for
brain development, physical growth, learning, relationships and lifelong health. Early
experiences shape the child's capacity to learn, regulate emotions, form relationships and
respond to stress.

### Factors that support healthy development
- Safe, stable and nurturing relationships
- Responsive interaction, talking, reading and play
- Adequate nutrition, sleep and physical activity
- Preventive healthcare and immunisation
- Stable housing and access to education and community services
- Protection from violence, neglect, discrimination and environmental hazards

### Nursing implications

Nurses should use every contact to assess the child and family holistically, reinforce
protective factors, identify social or developmental risk, provide anticipatory guidance
and connect families with appropriate services. Development is influenced by parenting,
culture, education and context; children do not all develop at exactly the same rate.

## 3. Child Development

Child development is multidimensional. Physical, cognitive, language, social and
emotional domains are interrelated and should be considered together rather than assessed
in isolation.

### Broad developmental stages

| Stage | Approximate age | General focus |
|---|---:|---|
| Newborn | 0–3 months | Adaptation to life outside the uterus; attachment and regulation |
| Infant | 3–12 months | Rapid motor, sensory, communication and social development |
| Toddler | 1–3 years | Mobility, autonomy, language and exploratory play |
| Preschool child | 3–4 years | Imagination, language, self-care and social play |
| School-aged child | 4–5 years and onward | Consolidation of skills, friendships, logic and learning |
| Middle childhood | 6–12 years | Competence, peer relationships and increasing independence |
| Adolescence | 13–18 years | Puberty, identity, abstract thinking and separation from parents |

Age ranges are guides, not rigid deadlines. Individual, cultural and contextual
differences must be considered. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child recognises a
child's right to develop to the maximum extent possible.

### Developmental domains

| Domain | What may be assessed |
|---|---|
| **Physical / motor** | Gross motor movement, fine motor control, posture, vision, reflexes, eating and sleep |
| **Language and communication** | Hearing, sounds, words, comprehension and ability to express needs |
| **Cognitive** | Learning, problem-solving, memory, attention and engagement with the environment |
| **Social** | Interaction, play, relationships and response to other people |
| **Emotional** | Attachment, emotional expression, regulation and general wellbeing |

Developmental theories introduced earlier in the course include Freud, Erikson, Piaget
and Kohlberg. These theories can help organise observations, but they do not replace
individual assessment or clinical judgement.

## 4. Developmental Milestones and Delay

### What are developmental milestones?

Milestones are abilities that babies and young children commonly develop as they learn to
move, see, hear, communicate, interact and participate in daily life. Child and family
health nurses use milestones, health history, observation and family report to assess
whether development is progressing as expected.

Children develop at different rates. Missing a milestone altogether, losing a previously
acquired skill, or showing concerns across more than one domain warrants further
assessment. Early recognition allows earlier intervention and better outcomes.

### Possible indicators of developmental concern

- Does not appear to hear or see properly, focus, follow objects or respond to sounds
- Keeps fingers tightly fisted at about 3 months
- Does not move both arms or both legs
- Cannot hold the head up by 3–4 months
- Is persistently unhappy or unsettled at about 4 months
- Does not reach for objects by about 6 months
- Does not sit well by about 10 months
- Is not babbling or making sounds by about 10 months
- Does not try to stand by about 12 months
- Does not appear to understand speech by about 18 months
- Shows little interest in the surrounding world

These are prompts for assessment, not diagnoses. Consider prematurity, hearing or vision,
illness, neurodevelopmental variation, family concerns and the child's context. Do not
wait for a concern to become severe: document, discuss with the RN/appropriate clinician
and refer according to local policy.

### Practical developmental assessment

1. Review history, pregnancy and birth information, health and family concerns.
2. Observe the child during play and routine interaction.
3. Assess across all developmental domains.
4. Ask open questions and take the family's observations seriously.
5. Compare with an appropriate developmental guide, allowing for individual variation.
6. Document findings clearly and arrange follow-up or referral when indicated.

## 5. Children Are Not Small Adults

Children have age-dependent anatomical, physiological, developmental and communication
characteristics. Assessment and treatment must therefore be adapted to the child's age,
size, developmental level and family context.

### Key nursing implications

- Use paediatric equipment and age-appropriate reference ranges.
- Weight is important for medication and fluid calculations; verify the current weight.
- A small airway can become compromised quickly, and respiratory deterioration may be
  subtle or rapid.
- Infants and young children have limited physiological reserves and may deteriorate
  quickly.
- Temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation vary
  with age and clinical condition.
- Observe work of breathing, colour, behaviour, feeding and interaction, not only numbers.
- Use a calm, child-friendly approach; involve the caregiver and explain procedures simply.
- Minimise distress while maintaining accurate assessment and safety.

> Always use the current local paediatric guideline or observation chart for normal ranges,
> escalation criteria, medication doses and resuscitation procedures.

## 6. Vaccination and Immunisation

### Key concepts

- **Vaccination** is the administration of a vaccine to stimulate an immune response.
- **Immunisation** is the process by which a person becomes protected against a disease,
  usually through vaccination.
- **Herd immunity** occurs when enough people in a population are immune that transmission
  is reduced, indirectly protecting people who cannot be vaccinated or who respond poorly.
- **Basic reproductive number (R₀)** describes the average number of people one infected
  person would infect in a fully susceptible population. The higher the R₀, the higher the
  population immunity needed to interrupt transmission.
- A **zero-dose child** has not received any routine vaccine doses; under-vaccination
  means that some recommended doses have been missed.

### Why childhood vaccination matters

Vaccination reduces preventable illness, complications, hospitalisation and death. It also
protects infants, immunocompromised people and other community members. Disruptions such
as COVID-19 can interrupt routine services and campaigns, increasing missed vaccinations.

### Barriers and vaccine hesitancy

Potential barriers include access and transport, cost, service availability, language and
health literacy, lack of reminders, competing priorities, previous adverse experiences,
fear, misinformation, distrust and cultural or social factors.

### Role of the registered nurse

- Listen respectfully and ask what the parent is worried about.
- Acknowledge concerns without judgement or shaming.
- Provide clear, evidence-based information in understandable language.
- Check understanding and correct misinformation appropriately.
- Discuss benefits, common expected reactions and when to seek help.
- Check the child's records and identify missed opportunities.
- Address practical barriers and use interpreters or other supports when needed.
- Follow current Australian immunisation guidance, screening, consent and documentation
  requirements.

2. How can a nurse help reduce those barriers?
3. How did COVID-19 affect vaccination campaigns, and what could improve access?

## 7. Family-Centred Care and Hospitalisation

Family-centred care recognises the child and family as partners in healthcare. Parents and
caregivers are often the child's primary source of support and knowledge, while the child
or young person should be involved to the extent of their developing capacity.

### Core principles

- Treat the child and family with dignity and respect.
- Share information openly and in a form the child and family can understand.
- Invite family knowledge, preferences and participation.
- Include the child in decisions at an age- and developmentally appropriate level.
- Work collaboratively and support continuity of care.
- Respect culture, beliefs, family structure and individual context.

### Effects of hospitalisation

Children and adolescents may respond to hospitalisation according to developmental stage,
separation from caregivers, unfamiliar people and surroundings, painful procedures, loss
of control and disruption of routine. Possible responses include fear, anxiety,
regression, sleep or feeding changes, withdrawal, anger and distress.

### Reducing distress

- Prepare the child honestly using simple, age-appropriate explanations.
- Offer choices where possible to restore a sense of control.
- Keep caregivers involved and maintain familiar routines.
- Use play, distraction, comfort and therapeutic communication.
- Cluster care where appropriate and minimise unnecessary procedures.
- Assess pain and emotional distress; document and respond to both.

## 8. Communication and Assessment of the Paediatric Patient

### Communication principles

- Establish rapport before beginning an assessment.
- Speak directly to the child as well as the caregiver.
- Use the child's preferred name and developmentally appropriate language.
- Get down to the child's level, use a calm voice and allow time for a response.
- Explain what will happen before touching or examining the child.
- Use play, demonstration and visual supports when useful.
- Observe non-verbal communication, behaviour, interaction, colour, posture and work of
  breathing.
- Use a qualified interpreter when language support is required; do not rely on children
  to interpret.

### Assessment approach

Begin with observation from a distance, particularly when the child is distressed. Assess
the least invasive elements first and leave the most distressing examination until last
when clinically safe. Include the caregiver's account, the child's voice and objective
findings. Reassess after an intervention and escalate deterioration promptly.

## 9. Charter on the Rights of Children and Young People in Healthcare Services

Every child and young person has the right:

1. To have their best interests as the primary concern.
2. To express their views and have them heard and taken seriously.
3. To the highest attainable standard of healthcare.
4. To be respected as a whole person, including their family, culture, beliefs and context.
5. To be nurtured by parents and family, with family relationships supported.
6. To receive information in a form they can understand.
7. To participate in decisions and, where appropriate, make decisions about their care.
8. To be kept safe from all forms of harm.
9. To have privacy respected.
10. To participate in education, play, creative activities and recreation where possible.
11. To have continuity of healthcare, including planned transition beyond paediatric care.

These rights support safe, high-quality partnerships between children, families and health
professionals. They should guide communication, consent, privacy, advocacy and discharge
planning.

## 10. REACH: Responding to Parental Concerns

### Reflective activity: Meet Noah

Watch **Meet Noah** and reflect on:

- How you would feel as Noah's nurse while caring for him and his family
- What you can do to self-care and wind down after your shift
- How you might cope with the emotional impact of caring for a sick child

Video channel: https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC4Zaux92-w_C5xP2ITKIoBQ

An important component of family-centred care is not only being able to express concerns
and be involved in decision-making, but also being heard. Parental concerns can be
overlooked because of bias, communication breakdowns, busy departments and high workload.

REACH is a system developed by the Clinical Excellence Commission, in collaboration with
local health districts and consumers, to assist patients, caregivers and families in
raising concerns with staff about significant changes in a patient's condition.

The system acronym stands for:

- **Recognize**
- **Engage**
- **Act**
- **Call**
- **Help is on its way**

The system is based on the surf life-saving analogy, which promotes recognising and
appropriately treating deteriorating patients by signalling for help.

REACH encourages partnership between patients, caregivers, families and healthcare teams
when recognising and escalating deterioration. Families should initially engage with the
medical team. If concerns persist, they can escalate by requesting a clinical review,
which should occur within 30 minutes. If they remain concerned after the review, an
independent review or rapid response can be activated by calling the facility's designated
REACH number. REACH enables consumers to "speak up for safety" and provides reassurance
that help is on the way.

Watch the Clinical Excellence Commission **Day Family** video explaining the story behind
the REACH system (5:47): https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3gxsCgOpThQ

### Similar escalation systems: Ryan's Rule

Similar systems operate across Australia. **Ryan's Rule** was introduced in Queensland
after the death of three-year-old Ryan Saunders in 2007. Ryan died from invasive group A
streptococcal infection. His parents had raised concerns several times but felt that their
concerns were not acted on.

Ryan's Rule provides patients of any age, families, carers and guardians with another way
to obtain help when a patient's condition is getting worse or not improving as expected.
It is for clinical deterioration, not general complaints.

The three steps are:

1. Speak to the nurse or doctor and explain the concern.
2. If concerns persist, ask to speak with the nurse in charge of the shift or the doctor
   on duty.
3. If concerns remain after steps 1 and 2, request a Ryan's Rule clinical review by
   calling **13 HEALTH (13 43 25 84)**, or ask a nurse or Aboriginal and Torres Strait
   Islander Support Person to call on your behalf.

Families and carers should use the process when the patient looks worse, is not improving
as expected, or shows behaviour that is not normal for them. General complaints should be
directed through the hospital's usual complaints process.

## 12. Terminology Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Developmental milestone | An ability commonly acquired during development |
| Developmental delay | Development that is slower than expected or not progressing in one or more domains |
| Gross motor | Large-muscle movement such as sitting, standing and walking |
| Fine motor | Small-muscle control such as grasping and manipulating objects |
| Family-centred care | Care that treats the child and family as partners |
| Immunisation | Protection against disease, usually through vaccination |
| Vaccination | Giving a vaccine to stimulate an immune response |
| Herd immunity | Population-level protection when sufficient people are immune |
| R₀ | Average number of secondary infections from one case in a fully susceptible population |
| Zero-dose child | A child who has received no routine vaccine doses |
| Vaccine hesitancy | Delay in acceptance or refusal despite available vaccination services |
| Paediatric | Relating to the care of children and young people |
| Developmentally appropriate | Matched to the child's age, abilities and understanding |
| Advocacy | Acting to protect a person's rights, safety and expressed interests |
| Escalation | Communicating concern and obtaining a higher level of clinical review |
