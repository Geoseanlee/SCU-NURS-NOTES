Mental health and mental illness
Our understanding of wellness and illness has evolved from a diagnostic-focused approach in the mid-twentieth century to a contemporary person-focused definition of mental health. Defining mental health and mental illness becomes challenging due to the dynamic nature of these concepts and their cultural and societal influences. As a result of our enhanced understanding, more people are receiving treatment for mental illness than in the past.
The World Health Organization defines health holistically, considering physical, mental, and social well-being. However, mental health and mental illness lack a universally accepted definition, with cultural and societal variations playing a significant role. Mental illness encompasses conditions affecting mood, behaviour, and thinking, such as depression, schizophrenia, anxiety disorders, and addictive disorders, often causing distress and impaired functioning.
The modern approach to treating mental illness adopts an eclectic stance, incorporating concepts and strategies from various sources. Holistic perspectives acknowledge that mental health is shaped by a multitude of components and factors, contributing to its dynamic nature. Psychosocial interventions in mental health nursing
-
-
While many nurses may not specialise in mental health nursing, it is highly likely that they will provide nursing care for individuals experiencing mental health issues. Intervention plays a crucial role in mental health nursing, and psychosocial interventions are nursing activities aimed at enhancing the client's social and psychological functioning. These interventions improve social skills, interpersonal relationships, and communication. Nurses frequently employ psychosocial interventions to address client's needs and achieve desired outcomes in various practice settings, extending beyond mental health.

===== FILE: 08 - Introduction to neurodiversity.epub =====
Introduction to neurodiversity
-
-
Neurodiversity is the acknowledgment that people possess varying information and processing styles, resulting in diverse ways of thinking. This concept is closely tied to the understanding that autism represents a distinct cognitive perspective and a unique experience of being in the world. Wendy Lawson, an autism advocate, has termed autism as a disability. Tony Attwood and Carol Grey explored this notion earlier during their work on the discovery of Asperger's syndrome, concluding that while recognising differences is crucial, autism is more than a case of different abilities; it is a disorder causing subjective distress and impaired social and occupational functioning. This understanding that autism, while neurodiverse, constitutes a disability is embedded in the DSM criteria for diagnosis. Dutch philosopher Soren Kierkegaard, in older writings, questioned whether extremely restrictive and repetitive behaviours could be adaptive. Through a playful process, he concluded that such behaviour represents impairment (Cashin, 2017). These writings pre-date the construct of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and contribute to understanding neurodiversity. This paper is worth reading to see how texts that pre-dated the construct of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) discussed behaviours and thinking that would clearly fit into the construct of autism and were steps towards understanding neurodiversity. The book 'Repetition' was written by Kierkegaard in 1843 (Kierkegaard, 1843/1983).
Understanding disabilities is a core responsibility for nurses, aligned with international conventions focused on discrimination and inclusion. As we will discuss later, there are strong imperatives arising from international conventions to which Australia is a signatory to, and national and state laws, related to discrimination and inclusion. Perhaps even more significantly understanding diverse thinking gives insight into typical thinking, this insight is very important as understanding how people think and process information is central to person-centred care.
What is autism?
Throughout your career all registered nurses in all settings will care for someone with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). By increasing your understanding of ASD and making small adjustments to your practice you can help improve the health outcomes of this cohort of patients.
What is intellectual disability?
Unlike autism, intellectual disability does not represent a different style of thinking. Rather the diversity occurs in the volume of information that can be processed and the speed at which it is processed. In intellectual diversity, the degree of difference in processing is signified in the diagnosis which ranges from mild to severe.

===== FILE: 12 - Reasonable adjustments in person-centred care practice.epub =====
Reasonable adjustments in person-centred care practice
-
-
What are needed adjustments?
Unlike autism, Intellectual Disability does not represent a different style of thinking. Rather, the diversity occurs in the volume of information that can be processed and the speed at which it is processed. In Intellectual Diversity, the degree of difference in processing is signified in the diagnosis which ranges from mild to severe. Over the last century there has been a revolution in how society views the rights and citizenship of people with developmental disabilities (including Intellectual Disability and Autism). Well into the 1900s, Western societies' prevailing view could be maternalistic or paternalistic. People with developmental disabilities were infantilised and the mission of care was predominantly one of maintenance (maternalistic?). Earlier in the 20th century, when people with developmental disabilities did not live with family, they were usually separated in large institutional settings, often alongside people with mental illness (paternalistic?). This view has shifted to a recognition that people with developmental disabilities are citizens who like all citizens have rights and responsibilities. The lens shifted from one of caring for alone, to one of recognition of the potential inherent in all humans.
Good life for individuals with developmental disabilities
The right to full inclusion has developed even further to the right to have a good life. Having a good life includes such things as meaningful social participation, the chance to have friends and form relationships, and the right to have a secure place to call home over which one has a sense of control. Consistent with the underpinning tenants of inclusion, a good life also includes meaningful engagement in study or employment. Inclusion and the right to a good life as concepts both are founded on the acknowledged need for reasonable adjustments to be made. Developmental disabilities have morphed from being conceptualised as problems that need treatment to a natural part of the neurodiversity of the human species. Because people with developmental disabilities have divergent neurodevelopment, they think, and as a result behave, differently. This can be a different thinking style, as in the case of autism, or a difference in the speed of processing and the amount of information that can be processed at any one time, as in intellectual disability. Both require adjustments to be made to systems designed in society for typical thinkers (those without a developmental disability).
Challenges in advancing health equity for people with developmental disabilities
Society has not just recognised neurodiversity and the right to inclusion, and the need for the necessary adjustments to be made to allow this. However, international conventions have been made that enshrine these principles. National, state and territory legislation and policy exist that are based on these principles in general systems and specifically related to healthcare. Developmental disabilities are overtly named in the Australian NMBA Standards for Practice for the Registered Nurse (2016) as a context of practice. The standards are founded upon person-centred care. Person-centred care means adjusting care as needed for each individual. Unfortunately, even though gains have been made and our vision refined, and the lifespan for people with developmental disabilities is approaching the general population norm in western countries, general health outcomes for this group are some of the poorest of any group. In Australia, it has been identified that people with developmental disabilities are at higher risk of premature mortality and greater risk of morbidity than peers without a developmental disability. There is much work to be done to change the health outcomes, and Registered Nurses are central to this process.


===== FILE: 13 - Positive behaviour support.epub =====
Positive behaviour support
-
-
Two common approaches to constructing support or intervention in nursing are deficit-based and strengths-based approaches:
- The deficit-based approach centres on impairments and limitations, focusing on what an individual cannot do. In contrast, the strengths-based approach emphasises a person's capabilities, utilising their strengths to establish new functional routines.
- Positive Behaviour Support aligns with a strengths-based nursing focus, recognising that understanding, interpreting, and modifying behaviour necessitates considering the whole person. This holistic exercise harmonises well with nursing practice.
Behaviour, excluding autonomic reflex actions, is rooted in cognition. A person's senses can influence behaviour, representing actions to achieve desired or necessary outcomes. Like driving a car, habits become automatic over time, operating without conscious effort.
Understanding that behaviour is thought-driven, especially before becoming habitual, connects with the earlier domains. For individuals with hyper or hypo sensitivities, distorted sensory information can disrupt meaning-making efforts and become a distressing source.
Meaning-making involves expressive and receptive communication, and individuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) or autism spectrum disorder (ASD) may exhibit a deceptive imbalance between the two. Positive behaviour support operates on the principle that all behaviour serves a function, leading to the exploration of supporting positive behaviour and developing functional routines.
This process entails working collaboratively to re-engineer routines and find more adaptive ways to achieve desired outcomes. Despite behaviour having a function, some behaviours may be efficient but not adaptive, leading to undesirable consequences. Strong emotions like anxiety, confusion, and frustration can influence behaviour expression, impacting the meaning-making process.
Maladaptive behaviour, typically labelled as "bad behaviour," may elicit negative responses.

===== FILE: 15 - Introduction (2).epub =====
Introduction
-
-
Parenting, while often filled with joy, can also be a source of significant stress. Research consistently shows that parenting a child with an Intellectual Disability is more stressful than parenting neurotypical children, and parenting a child with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) can be even more challenging.
The metaphor of the vortex has been used to describe the experience of parenting a child with autism (Cashin, 2004), especially for neurotypical parents. Neurotypical parents, who typically have behavioural and thinking flexibility, may find themselves struggling to adapt to the unique needs of a child with ASD. This struggle can reduce flexibility and spontaneity, causing family activities and routines to become overly regulated. Unfortunately, this attempt at adaptation through overregulation may, in turn, hinder the family's ability to adapt effectively, creating a vortex effect where autism dominates the family system.
As a result of this overregulation and loss of flexibility, parents often experience heightened stress and anxiety. These challenges are compounded by the complexities of navigating various systems to meet their child's needs.
Nurses can play a crucial role in supporting parents by mindfully working with them to navigate and alleviate stress in the context of raising a child with ASD, and the main focus of this topic is to explore the experience of parenting children with ID and/or ASD.

===== FILE: 16 - The developmental trajectory in Autism Spectrum Disorder.epub =====
The developmental trajectory in Autism Spectrum Disorder
-
-
ASD a lifelong disorder
In the DSM IV-TR and earlier versions, autism and the related diagnostic constructs were clustered under disorders appearing in childhood. Many viewed autism as an issue for children and adolescents, even though it was acknowledged that autism was a pervasive developmental disorder. Research expenditure internationally has been informed by this view, with historically less than 2% of ASD research funds being invested into research with adults by the Office of Autism Research Coordination in the National Institute of Mental Health. While this has shifted a little in recent times, the disparity in targets of research funding remains (Cervantes et al., 2021). In terms of physical health, people with ASD appear to be at higher risk of all-cause mortality than the general population. While the average lifespan for people with intellectual disability is normalising, we do not know the average lifespan for people with ASD. Outcome studies in adults indicate that people with ASD have significant challenges and experience disadvantages in the domains of physical and mental health, independent living, relationships, educational attainment and employment. In 2012, it was noted that it had not been demonstrated that the positive effects of early intervention translate into adulthood benefits. It had not been demonstrated that early intervention impacted the poor outcomes experienced by adults with ASD, and this remains the case (Howlin & Moss, 2012). This finding is counterintuitive for some who have enthusiastically championed the belief that early intervention will reduce the expression of ASD and reduce the need for intervention across the lifespan.
The developmental trajectory
In this topic, we explore the developmental trajectory of those with ASD across the lifespan. We look at the change in the ways the associated behaviours are manifested and the common accompanying challenges across the lifespan. Of course, there is no fixed trajectory, and every individual will encounter unique challenges and opportunities along the way. ASD does not define who a person is. This is emphasised in person-first language as discussed in the reading in activity 1.1, and even those who advocate for the use of identity-based language often acknowledge that the use of the term autistic does not mitigate the fact that the person referred to is an individual (Shakes & Cashin, 2018). Even though there is no fixed trajectory, the idea of a trajectory (or even the metaphor of a tour itinerary) is a useful way to think about the transitions encountered between, and problems that are common within, different life periods. If we use the itinerary metaphor, this could be thought of as the common stops on the tour.
The trajectory

The authors of the small-scale study that did identify a more extended time from the onset of labour to delivery wondered in writing whether resistance to change begins this early. ===== FILE: 17 - Assessment of intelligence and functional assessment in Intellectual Disability and ASD.epub =====
Assessment of intelligence and functional assessment in Intellectual Disability and ASD
-
-
Both intelligence and functional assessments play crucial roles in the diagnostic process of Intellectual Disability. In this section, we will delve into the significance of evaluating both intelligence (Intelligence Quotient - IQ) and functional capacity.
Psychologists traditionally conduct psychometric testing, which includes intelligence tests. While an intelligence test provides a full-scale IQ, it also yields a set of sub-scores. The full-scale IQ score aids in determining the severity of Intellectual Disability. However, understanding an individual's profile across the sub-scores is equally valuable in identifying their strengths and areas of relative challenge.
For instance, if someone exhibits higher scores in visual reasoning than verbal reasoning, it suggests the potential benefit of using easy-to-read resources, simplifying language, providing concise verbal instructions, and visually augmenting instructions. Interpreting the IQ test results can be exceptionally beneficial for nurses in developing strengths-based intervention and support plans for those they are assisting.
Watch: IQ Testing - Understanding the process
As can be see in the video intelligence testing is a social process. Testability refers to a person’s ability/willingness to participate in an IQ test. People who have impaired social communication may underperform or not perform at all in an IQ test. Outcomes depend on engagement with the testor and the test. The test outcomes are indicative and not definitive. Functional assessment refers to a person’s adaptive functioning or behaviours. Adaptive functioning refers to age appropriate things one needs to do to successfully navigate their world and care for themselves. This includes the need to cope with changes and unexpected events.
