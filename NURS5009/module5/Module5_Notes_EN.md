## Healthy Ageing, Diversity and Social Context in Older Adulthood

> This module explores healthy ageing, age-friendly environments, cultural and social diversity,
> person-centred care, social prescribing, and how nursing care can support older adults to live
> well and participate meaningfully in society.

## 1. Healthy Ageing

Healthy ageing is defined by the WHO as the process of developing and maintaining functional
ability that enables wellbeing in older age.

### Functional ability includes:
- ability to meet basic needs
- ability to learn, grow and make decisions
- ability to be mobile and physically active
- ability to participate socially and emotionally

### Important point

Healthy ageing is not only about longevity or being free from disease. It is about enabling
older adults to live with dignity, autonomy and purpose.

---

## 2. Determinants of Healthy Ageing

A person’s ageing experience is shaped by many factors, including:
- education and income
- housing and social security
- family and community support
- social participation and inclusion
- physical environment
- healthcare access and quality
- culture, spirituality and identity

These are social determinants of health and they strongly shape ageing outcomes.

---

## 3. Age-Friendly Environments

Age-friendly environments support mobility, safety, dignity and participation. They reduce
barriers and allow older adults to remain connected to community and purpose.

### Features of an age-friendly environment
- safe and accessible housing
- safe public spaces
- supportive transport and mobility aids
- community inclusion
- social participation opportunities
- responsive healthcare systems

---

## 4. Diversity in Ageing

Older adults are not a homogeneous group. Ageing experience differs according to:
- culture and ethnicity
- gender and sexuality
- disability or neurodiversity
- social class and historical context
- migration and trauma history
- spirituality and identity

### Nursing implication

Culturally safe care requires respect, curiosity and humility. Listening to individual stories
helps reduce assumptions and supports equitable care.

---

## 5. Person-Centred Care in Older Age

Older adults should be partners in their own care. Person-centred care recognises each person’s
values, goals, preferences, and lived experience.

### Core features
- Dignity and respect
- Shared decision-making
- Communication and trust
- Family and carer involvement where appropriate
- Holistic care that considers social, emotional and spiritual dimensions

---

## 6. Social Prescribing

Social prescribing is a holistic approach that connects people with non-medical support to
improve wellbeing.

### Examples
- community groups
- exercise programs
- social activities
- volunteering opportunities
- mental health support
- culturally safe services

### Why it matters

Social connection and purpose are powerful determinants of health and wellbeing in older age.

---

## 7. Ageism and Bias

Ageism affects how older adults are seen and treated. It may influence care quality, health
outcomes and self-image.

### Avoiding ageism in practice
- challenge stereotypes
- avoid infantilising language
- listen to older adults’ own views
- value lived experience and expertise
- promote autonomy and inclusion

---

## 8. Nursing Practice Considerations

Nurses caring for older adults should:
- assess the person in context, not just the diagnosis
- consider home environment, mobility and lived experience
- screen for social isolation, abuse, neglect or poverty
- recognise the importance of identity, culture and spirituality
- collaborate with multidisciplinary teams and community services

---

## 9. Key Takeaways

- Ageing is a social, biological and cultural process.
- Healthy ageing is broader than disease prevention.
- Person-centred, culturally safe care is essential.
- Social and environmental factors strongly shape older adult outcomes.
- Nurses have a central role in reducing ageism and promoting dignity and wellbeing.
