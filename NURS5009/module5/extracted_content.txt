===== FILE: 01 - Module overview.epub =====
Module overview
-
-
This module introduces the older adult and the ageing experience, exploring how biological, social, and environmental factors interact to influence health and well-being. You will examine the diverse ageing population, consider the challenges and opportunities associated with ageing, and reflect on your own beliefs, values, and assumptions about older people and how these shape nursing care. The module deepens understanding of healthy ageing and the barriers created by the social determinants of health, such as housing, income, education, social connection, and access to healthcare. Empathy and ethical care underpin this learning: empathy involves understanding another person’s perspective and lived experience, while ethical care requires acting as their moral agent - being honest, caring, and ensuring no harm comes to the person in your care. As you move through each topic, you will be encouraged to consider how these principles support dignity, autonomy, and inclusion for older adults, while challenging stereotypes and reflecting on how ageism and bias can influence the therapeutic relationship and impact health outcomes.
Module purpose
The purpose of this module is to introduce the ageing process, the diversity of the ageing experience, and the nursing considerations that promote healthy ageing. The module highlights how the social determinants of health influence ageing, well-being, and access to equitable, person-centred care.
By the end of this module, you will be able to:
- Describe the basic concepts of ageing, including healthy ageing and common misconceptions.
- Reflect on ageism and personal biases and how these influence care for older adults.
- Recognise the diversity of ageing experiences shaped by culture, identity, and social context.

===== FILE: 03 - Overview.epub =====
Overview
-
-
Global ageing is accelerating, with life expectancy reaching 73.3 years in 2024 and the proportion of people aged over 60 projected to almost double from 12% in 2015 to 22% by 2050 (World Health Organisation [WHO], 2018). In Australia, the proportion of adults aged 65 and over has grown from 8.3% in 1970 to 16% in 2020, and is projected to reach up to 23% by 2066 (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW], 2021). These demographic changes highlight the growing importance of understanding ageing as a global and societal issue.
Learning focus
This topic introduces the science of ageing, exploring how ageing affects individuals, communities, and health systems. It examines the concept of healthy ageing and the factors that contribute to positive experiences of growing older. The topic also considers ageism and how stereotypes, bias, and social attitudes can shape the experiences of older people.
1.1. The science of ageing
During the 1970s, researchers identified telomeres, the stretches of DNA that protect both ends of chromosomes, as an important factor in human aging. Human cells are thought to divide about 50 to 70 times over a lifetime. With each replication, telomeres shorten, allowing chromosomes to stay intact. Over time, the telomeres shorten until the cell is no longer able to replicate, resulting in cell death. For example, losing the ability of the cells to divide could hamper the immune system's capacity to respond to particular pathogens, which may underlie the slower wound healing in the elderly (Bernoth & Montayre, 2022).

Activity 1.1 Normal ageing process
Normal aging is a continuous process, and its effects are influenced by factors such as genetics, health, lifestyle, stressors, and environmental conditions. Recognising normal aging empowers nurses with the knowledge and skills essential for delivering tailored, compassionate, and effective care to older adults and collaborating with them in adapting to physiological changes. However, older adults exhibit considerable variability in their levels of functional ability. While these physiological changes are not fundamentally pathological, they may render older adults more susceptible to common clinical conditions and challenge their sense of self-identity. Some individuals undergo a spectrum of these changes, while others experience only a subset.

1.2
-
-
The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Healthy Ageing “as the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing in older age”.
Educational and economic status, health behaviours, and social participation and inclusion are factors that support healthy ageing. Healthy Ageing is about creating the environments and opportunities that enable people to be and do what they value most in life. Being free of disease or disability is not a requirement for Healthy Ageing as many older adults have one or more chronic health conditions that, when well controlled, have little influence on their wellbeing. Healthy aging helps reduce the pressure on health care and social services. Seniors make important contributions to their families, their communities, the economy, and their nation. Older people who stay healthy, active and independent can continue to contribute their skills, knowledge, and experience to society.
Active Ageing is a term used to describe the maintenance of positive subjective well-being, good physical, social and mental health and continued involvement in one’s family, peer group and community throughout the aging process. The World Health Organization defines Active Ageing as “the process of optimising opportunities for health, participation and security in order to enhance quality of life as people age.”
The WHO and the UN have led and coordinated international efforts to develop guiding frameworks and principles relevant to nursing care of the older person, notably following the release of the World Health Organization (WHO) World Report on Aging and Health in 2015 and the Framework for Action on Aging and Health in 2016. In May 2016, an international consensus was reached, key priorities for action are based on the following components:
- active ageing
- a ‘determinants of health’ approach
- age-friendly environments
- training of care providers and health professionals.
This global momentum has led to the declaration of 2020-2030 as the UN "Decade of Healthy Aging," marking a concerted effort to address the challenges and opportunities associated with an aging world population.

===== FILE: 06 - 1.3.epub =====
1.3
-
-
Recently there has been significant research into ageism and several high-profile campaigns, including campaigns by the World Health Organization, to increase awareness of ageism and its effects on individuals. Regardless of these efforts to reduce the discrimination against many older people, ageism remains a socially acceptable form of discrimination. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines ageism as:
“Ageism refers to the stereotypes (how we think), prejudice (how we feel), and discrimination (how we act) towards others or oneself based on age” (World Health Organization, 2025).
Ageism is expressed through negative perceptions and stereotypes of older people. These attitudes are usually developed through social environmental influencers. Ageism not only includes an individual’s attitudes and beliefs but also encompasses, discriminatory behaviours towards older people and formalised policies and practices e.g. the language used to describe older people in government reports as “a growing burden” or “a social problem.” Ageism does not only include those attitudes of younger people towards older people. Older people can also be ageist towards themselves through a process of internalisation and acceptance of ageist stereotypes. As a result, they believe they are a burden on society and their families and that they are unable to contribute in any meaningful way, are uninteresting, unattractive, and senile. The WHO found that older people who hold negative attitudes and beliefs about their own ageing had poorer health outcomes when recovering from illness and live on average 7.5 years less than those with positive attitudes towards ageing (World Health Organization, 2021). Why do you think this may be? Do you think that this poor self-view may lead to poor self-care, less engagement?

===== FILE: 07 - 1.4.epub =====
1.4
-
-
The WHO has identified many of the flow-on effects of ageism. These include poorer health and wellbeing, decreased quality of life, reduced employment opportunities, the link to learned helplessness and the reduction in life expectancy. Another negative impact of ageism on the older person is the quality of health care they receive. This is particularly important to you all as future nurses. Research has found that ageist attitudes towards older people can reduce the amount of information shared regarding their care and the treatment and diagnostic options made available to the older person (Burnes et al., 2019). Ageism can lead to patronising behaviour from the health care professional, exclusion, and a general feeling of “being invisible.”
The following short comment paper highlights the findings from the WHO Global report on Ageing: Mikton, C., de la Fuente-Núñez, V., Officer, A., & Krug, E. (2021). Ageism: A social determinant of health that has come of age. The Lancet, 397(10282), 1333–1334. https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(21)00524-9/fulltext
The recent release of the Final Report from the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety described an aged care sector that is failing our elderly population. This report, along with evidence provided at the hearings, exposed most care being provided in this space as suboptimal at best. The report highlights the systemic flaws of the sector whose purpose is to provide care directly to older Australians, but also the barriers older people face when accessing care both in the community and the acute care setting. This report acknowledges the societal influence that the ageist mindset of Australians has on the acceptance of poor care. This ageism based on stereotypes provides limited space for research (Elliott et al., 2017), leading to the development of deficient social policies that do not address the needs of this vulnerable population group, resulting in poorer clinical outcomes, dehumanised care, neglect, and abuse (Band-Winterstein, 2015; Kagan & Melendez‐Torres, 2015). In her overview titled Care, Dignity and Respect in the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety - Final Report (vol.1), Commissioner Lynelle Briggs wrote
“…I fear that society as a whole undervalues older people and their contribution. The acceptance of poorer service provision in aged care reflects an undervaluing of the worth of older people, assumptions and stereotypes about older people and their capabilities, and ageism towards them. This must change.” (p.26 )
The following excerpt from the Royal Commission Hearing is an example of dehumanised care that is a result of ageist policies that have reduced staffing numbers in aged care and led to staff delivering care that is uncaring. This excerpt, from a resident who had stage four metastatic bone cancer, details to the Royal Commission how he was left by carers for extended periods (30-90 minutes) on a mobile toilet while unable to stand or get off:
“When neglected like that, I feel I have been dehumanised, left as a carcass in an aged care abattoir ready to be processed like a slab of meat in a sausage-processing factory… My spine and pelvic regions are riddled with bony metastases. The longer I am left sitting there… pain dominates my whole existence. Every second of every minute seems like an eternity. No one seems to get this” (Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety, 2021).

As a health professional, consideration of an older person’s living arrangement is fundamental to all stages of the clinical reasoning cycle, including assessment, planning and evaluation of care. For example, when considering the discharge of an older person from a tertiary setting back into the community, we must ensure they are returning to a safe living situation. However, this is not always the case, so we must also consider those returning to suboptimal living arrangements.
In Australia, unfortunately, many older people are currently living as the homeless. Homelessness is defined as any situation where someone does not have suitable accommodation, lives in a dwelling that is inadequate, lives in a dwelling that has no tenure or a dwelling that does not allow space for social relatedness. In 2016, older people represented 16% of the total homeless population in Australia, an increase from 14% in 2011 (AIHW, 2019). Those identified at higher risk in the older population include Veterans, Indigenous peoples and those from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds. Additionally, the latest 2021 census reveals that women over 55 are the fastest-growing cohort of homeless people in Australia (Australian Human Rights Commission, 2019). Can you think of why this might be the case?

In nursing, one of the fundamental concepts is providing ‘safe care’ to our patients in many ways. This constitutes many different focuses, from legal safety to ethical safety to safety in clinical practice. But what about cultural safety? Where does that fit in?
In defining culture, it is important to consider anyone who differs from our own belief systems, values and upbringing. The term cultural safety was coined by New Zealand Maori nurse Irihapeti Ramsden after seeing the discrimination the Indigenous peoples of New Zealand faced. Cultural safety looks to be regardful of one’s culture and providing care regarding individual differences. It would be fair to suggest that, in this case, older people are a culture within themselves. Older people are considered a vulnerable/minority group; therefore, they need to be approached with a culturally safe approach.

2.5
-
-
Human sexuality is a natural part of life, yet it is often misunderstood or overlooked in older adults. Despite common misconceptions, touch, intimacy, and connection remain just as important in later life as they are for younger people (Portellos et al., 2023; Bigras et al., 2024). The need for companionship and affection does not fade with age, but social stigma and assumptions can make it harder for older people to express these needs (Slack & Aziz, 2020).
Common myths about sexuality and ageing include:
- Sex is no longer important as people age.
- Residents in aged care should not have sexual relationships.
- Sex is dangerous or inappropriate for older adults.
- Older people are not interested in intimacy or affection.
Recognising and challenging these myths helps promote dignity, autonomy, and person-centred care for older adults.
2.6. Gender and sexual identity in the older person
Equal opportunity and freedom from discrimination are fundamental human rights that belong to all people, regardless of age, sexual orientation, or gender identity. Many lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex, and other diverse sexual orientations, gender identities, and sex characteristics (LGBTI+) older adults have lived through periods of significant discrimination, social stigma, and prejudice. Often being labelled as criminals, sinners, or mentally ill. These experiences have profoundly impacted their lives, disrupting family relationships, limiting career opportunities, and affecting financial security in later life. As a result, some older LGBTQI+ individuals remain apprehensive about engaging with health professionals and services (McMullen-Roach et al., 2025).
In Australia, a 2015 report found that many older adults chose to keep their LGBTQI+ status private when entering aged care facilities due to fear of discrimination (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care, 2019). This concealment often led to ‘hiding’ their sexuality, reducing opportunities for authentic self-expression and diminishing their sense of safety and belonging (Australian Human Rights, 2015).
Despite progress toward inclusion, comprehensive data on LGBTI communities in Australia remains limited. The 2021 Census, for example, did not collect information about sexual orientation due to privacy concerns and respondent comfort. Consequently, LGBTI older adults are often rendered invisible within ageing and health service demographics, meaning that the unique challenges they face frequently go unrecognised and unaddressed. Additionally, a 2017 survey found that less than a quarter of 1094 facilities surveyed had access to policies around intimacy and sexual health to guide health workers (McAuliffe et al. 2020). This resulted in staff being uneducated and unprepared to support older individuals with gender-diverse needs.

2.8
-
-
Spirituality is a broad concept that encompasses many perspectives. In general, it includes a sense of connection to something bigger than ourselves, and typically involves a search for meaning in life.
There are many definitions of Spirituality. One comprehensive definition of Spirituality is offered by Puchalski et al (2014), who says that ‘Spirituality is a dynamic and intrinsic aspect of humanity through which persons seek ultimate meaning, purpose, and transcendence, and experience relationship to self, family, others, community, society, nature, and the significant or sacred. Spirituality is expressed through beliefs, values, traditions, and practices.” (Puchalski, et al., 2014).
A person’s spirituality may be independent of their religious belief, affiliation, or practice (palliAGED, 2022). Coming to terms with death is a key developmental challenge of old age. Erikson, a prominent psychosocial theorist, names his final stage of psychosocial development, Stage 8, “Integrity vs. Despair”, wherein a person generally begins to focus more on reflecting on the meaning of their life, as they are more so confronted with their mortality. Given this, providing spiritual care to older persons is an essential component of delivering holistic care to our patients. In fact, acknowledging and supporting a person’s spirituality has been shown to reduce the distress that many people experience when they are ill or dying (palliAGED, 2022)
In 2019, the new Aged Care Quality Standards (ACQS) were implemented, in which the notion of spiritual care became fully embedded into Australian regulations. The National Guidelines document provides a foundational understanding of spirituality, spiritual care and the importance of respect in all contexts (Meaningful Ageing Australia, 2022).
Source: Queensland Health https://www.health.qld.gov.au/cpcre/indig_pc Artwork: ‘End of Life Journey’ by Uncle Michael Connolly, Munda-gutta Kulliwari.
Spiritual care encompasses sensitive and respectful interactions, effective patient communication, and activities such as storytelling, reminiscing, mindfulness and meditation, prayer, therapeutic life review, or dignity therapy. palliAGED (2022)
Nursing actions for providing spiritual care to older adults
Use touch and eye contact that is appropriate, and a welcoming and unhurried approach to conversation.
Listen so that the person feels heard and valued.
Be sensitive to and respectful of the spiritual and religious needs of people in your care and their families. Allow for privacy and uninterrupted time for spiritual practice.
Offer opportunities for a connection with external spiritual practices.
Provide opportunities for discussion with chaplaincy staff, spiritual care practitioners or faith representatives. Facilitate opportunity for re-connection.
Offer activities and roles that give people purpose. palliAGED (2022)
“Spiritual care is often associated with support during times of crisis, ill-health, loss and grief and palliative care and it has much to offer in these areas. However, it is equally important in bringing meaning, purpose, and wholeness to assist a person in flourishing, growing, and realising their potential during their final journey of ageing. This reinforces the concept that spirituality is part of everyday living and hence all those who have contact with older people can share in the spiritual journey” (Meaningful Ageing Australia, 2016).

Social isolation and loneliness are increasingly recognised as significant health and wellbeing concerns among older adults. Both are strongly associated with poorer physical and mental health outcomes and can significantly reduce life expectancy (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2021). These issues are particularly concerning in ageing populations, where loss of social networks, bereavement, retirement, and declining mobility can all contribute to reduced social connection.
The World Health Organisation (2021) describes these terms as:
Social isolation is the state of having a smaller number of social contacts, which may contribute to loneliness.
Loneliness is the pain we feel when our social connections do not meet our needs.
The risk factors for social isolation in older people may relate to the individual or wider structural factors. Read the following document from the Australian Institute of Family Studies and consider the nurse's role in identifying and preventing social isolation.

As discussed in the previous podcast, social prescribing has emerged as an effective way to reduce social isolation and loneliness in older adults by connecting them with meaningful activities that promote healthy ageing. Used primarily in primary healthcare settings, social prescribing encourages nurses to identify and support adults at risk of loneliness or disconnection, linking them to local resources and programs that enhance wellbeing.
According to a scoping review by Sadio et al. (2024), social prescriptions typically fall within one of eight main domains:
- Arts
- Personal development
- Social interaction
- Physical activity
- Gardening
- Cultural activities
- Religious or spiritual activities
- Technological activities
This approach aligns strongly with the principles of person-centred care, recognising that each individual’s social, cultural, and emotional needs are unique. By tailoring recommendations to a person’s interests, values, and abilities, nurses can foster a sense of belonging, purpose, and empowerment, which are essential components of holistic health and wellbeing (Sadio et al., 2024; Freak-Poli et al., 2025).

It is common to expect a decline in the functioning of our sensory organs as we age. The brain requires a certain level of stimulation to register sensations like sound, light, or smell, and this threshold tends to increase with age. These sensory changes impact physical health and affect one's ability to function optimally, influencing overall quality of life. For healthcare professionals, awareness of age-related sensory changes is crucial in devising effective care plans. It is essential to note that age alone does not guarantee a sensory deficit; individuals age differently.
Age-related hearing loss, commonly referred to as presbycusis, typically affects both ears, often resulting in a decline in hearing high-frequency sounds and difficulties distinguishing between different sounds. Additionally, many older individuals experience tinnitus, a persistent ringing or other abnormal noise in the ear. Aging-related changes in vision encompass presbyopia, glaucoma, dry eyes, age-related macular degeneration, cataracts, and temporal arteritis. Skin tears
A skin tear is a traumatic wound that occurs when friction or shearing forces cause the skin to separate from the underlying tissue. These injuries often result from accidental bumps, manual handling, or falls. To ensure consistency in assessment, documentation, and communication, skin tears are classified using a staging system (Victoria Department of Health, 2024).
The STAR Skin Tear Classification System, developed by Curtin University of Technology, is one of the most widely used frameworks.

Pressure injuries
Pressure injuries are known by many names, as stated above; however, for this module, they will be referred to as pressure injuries, as this is the term used in clinical practice in Australia. Pressure injuries are defined as localised damage to the skin or underlying soft tissue, which can be open or intact and graded from stage 1 to stage 4 and beyond.
Pressure injuries have the potential to cause major harm to individuals, impacting their quality of life in the form of pain, distress, mood and sleep disturbances, along with increased susceptibility to infection, other complications and prolonged hospitalisation. This increased hospital stay can also impact the patient's family and increase costs to the healthcare facility, not only due to the extended length of stay but also the increased complexity of care requirements.

Pain is complex, subjective and depends on many differing variables. Pain experienced can alter from one day to the next based on your stress levels, the environment – including visual and auditory feedback, the language used by health professionals associated with the pain and your expectations.

===== FILE: 31 - 3.7.epub =====
3.7
-
-
Older adults experience higher rates of Dementia and Delirium than their younger counterparts, with a high risk of Depression. It is essential to recognise that these conditions are not a normal part of ageing, but rather reflect underlying health, social, or biological factors that necessitate assessment and appropriate care.
Key figures
- Dementia: Around 425,000 Australians were living with dementia in 2024, with projections suggesting this number will reach 1.1 million in 2065 if current trends continue (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2023).
- Delirium: Between 10–18% of Australians aged 65 years and older experience delirium at the time of hospital admission, and a further 2–8% develop delirium during their stay (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, 2021).
- Depression: Mental health conditions, including depression, are also prevalent among older adults, with 7.9% of men and 11.1% of women aged 65 years and older reporting a mental health condition (excluding dementia) within the previous 12 months (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, 2022).
The relationship between dementia, delirium, and depression is bidirectional, as each condition can increase vulnerability to or exacerbate the others. For example, dementia heightens the risk of delirium, while depression may accelerate cognitive decline or delay recovery following delirium (Mukku et al., 2021; Fong & Inouye, 2022). These conditions often present with overlapping symptoms, making accurate assessment and differentiation essential in nursing care. In some cases, delirium may occur superimposed on dementia, a particularly complex presentation that increases morbidity, length of hospital stay, and the risk of further cognitive decline (Morandi et al., 2017).

3.10
-

Adults with cognitive impairment may exhibit responsive or changed behaviours such as anxiety, agitation, or apathy, which can become more noticeable in certain situations. These behaviours are often triggered by illness, medication effects, unfamiliar environments, or the loss of social supports (Abbott et al., 2022; Lourida et al., 2020).
For nurses, these changes can make care more complex. Behaviours such as aggression, repetitive questioning, or refusal of care often reflect stress or unmet needs, rather than intentional resistance. Recognising these actions as forms of communication enables nurses to look beyond the behaviour and identify the underlying cause - such as pain, fear, or confusion.
Providing person-centred care involves adjusting the environment, communicating calmly, maintaining familiar routines, and offering reassurance to reduce distress. Understanding how hospitalisation affects people with cognitive impairment supports nurses in delivering safer, more compassionate, and responsive care.

3.11
-
-
Nonpharmacological interventions are essential in supporting adults with cognitive impairment. By meeting a person’s biological, psychological, and social needs, these approaches can reduce distress and responsive behaviours and help prevent the use of chemical and physical restraints (ACSQHC, 2013; Davis et al., 2023).
Under Australian law, restrictive practices refer to any intervention that limits a person’s rights or freedom of movement. The Aged Care Act 1997 and Quality of Care Principles 2014 (Part 4A) require that restrictive practices are used only as a last resort, in the least restrictive form, and with informed consent. A behaviour support plan must also be in place for anyone where such practices are considered.
There are five types of restrictive practices:
- Environmental restraint
- Mechanical restraint
- Physical restraint
- Seclusion
- Chemical restraint
Using person-centred, non-pharmacological strategies such as maintaining routines, modifying the environment, involving families, and providing reassurance can help nurses address unmet needs, reduce distress, and avoid the need for restrictive practices, while promoting dignity and safety in care.
