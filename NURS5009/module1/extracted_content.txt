Introduction
-

The terms "health," "wellness," and "illness" are often used interchangeably to describe an individual's overall well-being. It's essential to recognise that these terms imply that this understanding can differ from one person to another, influenced by various factors.
One proposed approach to comprehending health and well-being beyond physical health is the "6 Dimensions of Health". These dimensions encompass different facets of an individual's life and overall wellness. These six components are interconnected and overlap, highlighting the intricate nature of well-being. Wellness entails addressing all aspects of physical, psychological, social, emotional, spiritual, and cultural dimensions to promote a comprehensive sense of well-being.

Cultural Well-being
Culture is the pattern of life, beliefs and customs of whole or subsections of populations. It is a way of life in a society, including codes of manners, dress, language, rituals, norms of behaviour and systems of beliefs. It is important to understand that culture is a human construction and not biologically determined, and it continually faces challenges and transformations.
Often, we perceive the world through the lens of our own cultural group, which can lead to the tendency to evaluate other cultural groups in relation to our own. In order to understand the differences between cultures and the people themselves, we need to be culturally aware, particularly when our values and beliefs differ.
Cultural wellbeing involves the capacity to not only practice and uphold your own cultural experiences, beliefs, traditions, and values but also to embrace and celebrate the diverse experiences and identities of others. Being culturally aware allows you to acknowledge and respect the impact and intersection of varying aspects of diversity among yourself and others. This can encompass shared values, beliefs, customs, behaviours, and identity, fostering a sense of harmony and respect within a diverse society.
Travis Illness-Wellness Continuum
The Illness-Wellness Continuum, often credited to Dr Travis, offers a dynamic and comprehensive perspective of health, challenging the simplistic notion of being either "sick" or "well."
In this model, two arrows converge at a neutral point, signifying a balanced state. To the right of this neutral point, we observe an ascent towards greater health and well-being, driven by three essential factors: (1) awareness, (2) education, and (3) growth. Conversely, movement to the left of this neutral point indicates a gradual decline in health.
This continuum embraces the complexity of health as a multidimensional spectrum. It acknowledges that individuals can traverse it in both directions, influenced by various factors and choices. It emphasises the possibility of experiencing physical illness while simultaneously pursuing wellness or being physically healthy while adopting an illness-oriented mindset.
Source: Travis, J. W., & Ryan, R. S. (2004). The wellness workbook: How to achieve enduring health and vitality. Celestial Arts.

===== FILE: 03 - Social construction of health and illness.epub =====
Social construction of health and illness
-
-
The social construction of health and illness considers how societal and economic factors impact the well-being of the individuals we care for. Many chronic health conditions can be traced back to the social and economic disadvantages different societal groups face. Elements such as poverty, educational opportunities, cultural backgrounds, race, gender, and more have a profound influence on health outcomes and access to healthcare services. Individual choices do not solely determine these outcomes and access but are shaped by society's broader impact on health. In essence, the concept of health as a social construct involves examining how an individual's environment and circumstances affect their health status and recognising that numerous determinants are beyond an individual's control, often due to their specific context.
Similarly, the social construction of illness involves attributing meaning to illnesses that may not always correlate with their underlying causes. Society's perspective on individuals who are unwell, as well as the personal experiences of those affected by the illness, plays a significant role in shaping how illness is perceived. According to Conrad and Barker (2010), the experience of illness is deeply embedded in cultural contexts and may not consistently align with the objective medical diagnosis of a disease. Often, illness is constructed based on individual experiences, understanding, and coping mechanisms. It's essential to understand that medical professionals do not solely determine the concept of illness but is also influenced by societal and cultural factors, illustrating the intricate interplay between health, culture, and individual experiences.

Introduction
-

Social determinants of health encompass material and psychosocial factors contributing to disparities in health experiences and the impact of diseases and illnesses (Braveman, Egerter, & Williams, 2011).
Individuals with limited assets, inadequate childhood education, precarious employment, substandard living conditions, or low incomes face disadvantages. The cumulative effect of these disadvantages results in physiological and psychological stress, suffering, and a reduced likelihood of attaining a fulfilling and healthy life. Consequently, individuals in disadvantaged social and economic circumstances are more prone to experiencing poorer health throughout their lifetimes.
Activity: Watch 'Health as a social construct'
Are individuals solely responsible for their health, or are we collectively responsible for each other's health?
The concept of responsibility for one's health is a complex and debated issue in society. It can be viewed from two perspectives:
- Individual Responsibility: Some argue that individuals are primarily responsible for their own health. They emphasise personal choices, lifestyle, and behaviours such as diet, exercise, smoking, and alcohol consumption as key determinants of health. This perspective places a significant burden on individuals to make healthy choices and take actions to maintain their well-being.
- Collective Responsibility: On the other hand, there is a growing recognition that health is not solely an individual matter but is influenced by broader social, economic, and environmental factors. In this view, society as a whole shares responsibility for the health of its members. Factors like access to healthcare, social determinants of health (including income, education, and housing), and the overall quality of the healthcare system play crucial roles in determining health outcomes. Advocates for collective responsibility argue for policies and interventions that address these systemic factors to improve public health.
The idea of the social construction of health considers the socio-cultural aspects that impact an individual. We tend to discount that someone’s poverty, low education, belonging to a minority ethnic group, or being homeless can affect one’s sense of well-being. Health equity and equality
Inequity pertains to avoidable and unjust disparities that emerge due to factors like inadequate governance, corruption, or cultural exclusion. Conversely, inequality involves an uneven allocation of health opportunities and resources, often attributed to genetics or other inherent factors, as well as limited or insufficient resources. For instance, we have access to Medicare healthcare services in Australia. Nevertheless, the distribution of these benefits is not uniform due to varying accessibility, literacy levels, and other determinants.

Introduction
-
-
The last topic in this module considers the health consumer narrative. These narratives are personal stories and cultural, social, and contextual accounts that shape our perception of health. Consumer narratives, also known as patient narratives, significantly shape our understanding of health.
There is a trend towards using narratives, stories, and storytelling as educational tools for patients, students, and healthcare professionals. The effectiveness of storytelling as a means of communication is supported by evidence from various fields, including nursing, social science, and psychology. An appeal of storytelling is its ability to present information couched within a personal account that engages the reader and validates their own experiences.
