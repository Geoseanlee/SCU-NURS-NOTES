## 1. Health, Wellness and Illness

Health is not only the absence of disease. It is a broader state that includes physical,
psychological, social, emotional, spiritual and cultural wellbeing.

### The Six Dimensions of Health

| Dimension | Description |
|---|---|
| Physical | Body health, energy, sleep, exercise, nutrition |
| Psychological | Mental wellbeing, coping, resilience, cognition |
| Social | Relationships, support systems, connectedness |
| Emotional | Ability to recognise, express and manage emotions |
| Spiritual | Meaning, purpose, values, beliefs, inner connection |
| Cultural | Identity, belonging, traditions, cultural safety and respect |

These dimensions are interconnected. A person can be physically well but emotionally strained,
or socially connected but spiritually disconnected. Nursing care should therefore be holistic.

### Important idea

The terms “health,” “wellness,” and “illness” are socially and personally interpreted and
can vary between individuals. What counts as a “healthy life” depends on context, culture,
resources and lived experience.

---

## 2. Illness–Wellness Continuum

The Illness–Wellness Continuum, often associated with Dr John Travis, describes health as a
continuum rather than a simple binary of “well” or “ill.”

### Core idea
- The midpoint represents a neutral or balanced state.
- Moving to the right reflects growth, awareness, education and wellbeing.
- Moving to the left reflects decline, illness or reduced wellbeing.

### Why it matters in nursing

It reminds us that:
- A person may be unwell physically but still engaged in wellness behaviours.
- Someone may appear healthy but still hold an illness-oriented mindset or poor coping patterns.
- Health is dynamic and can change over time.
- Nurses should focus on what supports recovery, resilience and self-management.

**Three drivers of movement to the right:**
1. Awareness
2. Education
3. Growth

## 3. Social Construction of Health and Illness

The social construction of health and illness explores how society, culture and social systems
shape how we define and experience health problems.

### Key points

- Many chronic conditions are linked to social and economic disadvantage.
- Poverty, education, culture, gender, race, housing, work and access to services influence
  health outcomes.
- Individual behaviour is shaped by the environment and social conditions in which people live.
- Illness is not only a biological event; it is also interpreted through culture, meaning and
  social expectation.

### Example

Someone may experience illness differently because of:
- the way their community views that condition
- their access to diagnosis and treatment
- previous experiences of discrimination or stigma
- cultural beliefs about symptoms, treatment or help-seeking

This means nurses should avoid reducing patient experience to only diagnosis. We must also
attend to social context, meaning and lived experience.

## 4. Social Determinants of Health (SDoH)

The social determinants of health are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live,
work and age. These conditions influence health outcomes and health inequity.

### Examples of SDoH
- Socioeconomic status
- Education and literacy
- Employment and working conditions
- Housing and food security
- Access to transport and healthcare
- Social support and community networks
- Cultural inclusion and discrimination
- Environmental conditions

### Health equity vs health equality

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Equality | Providing the same resources or opportunities to everyone |
| Equity | Providing different levels of support to account for different needs and starting points |

A fair health system seeks equity, not simply equal access for all people. People may need
different support to achieve the same health outcome.

---

## 5. Health Inequality and Health Inequity

### Health inequality

Uneven distribution of health outcomes, opportunities or resources.

### Health inequity

Health inequity refers to avoidable, unfair and unjust differences in health outcomes due to
social, institutional or structural disadvantage.

Examples include:
- unequal access to preventive care
- poorer outcomes for marginalised communities
- reduced opportunities because of poverty, exclusion or discrimination

### Why this matters

Nurses are not only treating illness — they are also working in systems where social and
structural factors create health disparities. Care is stronger when nurses recognise the impact
of inequity and advocate for fairer, more inclusive health systems.

## 6. Vulnerable Population Groups

Some populations are more likely to experience poorer health because of intersecting factors
such as disadvantage, discrimination, trauma or poor access to services.

### Examples of vulnerable groups
- Families with complex health and social care needs
- People requiring mental health support
- People who are homeless or at risk of homelessness
- People experiencing domestic or family violence
- Refugees and asylum seekers
- People with disability
- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples
- LGBTIQ people
- People exiting prison

### Inclusion health and “the causes of the causes”

Marmot’s concept of “the causes of the causes” reminds us that health outcomes are influenced
not only by individual choices, but also by the deeper social causes of exclusion, poverty,
poor education, insecure work, trauma and discrimination.

This helps explain why some groups have much poorer health and shorter life expectancy, even
when they may be making “healthy choices.”

## 7. Human Development Theories

Human development theories help explain how people grow and change over the life course.
In this module, key theorists include Freud, Erikson, Piaget and Kohlberg.

### Summary table

| Theorist | Main idea | Developmental focus |
|---|---|---|
| Freud | Personality develops through psychosexual stages | Early childhood experiences and drives |
| Erikson | Personality develops across psychosocial stages | Identity, trust, autonomy, industry, integrity |
| Piaget | Children develop through cognitive stages | Thinking and learning |
| Kohlberg | Moral reasoning develops in stages | Judgement, ethics and social values |

### Example overview

| Age range | Freud | Erikson | Piaget | Kohlberg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–18 months | Oral stage | Trust vs mistrust | Sensorimotor | Preconventional morality |
| 18 months–3 years | Anal stage | Autonomy vs shame/doubt | Preoperational | Preconventional morality |
| 3–5 years | Phallic stage | Initiative vs guilt | Preoperational | Conventional morality |
| 6–12 years | Latency stage | Industry vs inferiority | Concrete operational | Conventional morality |
| 12–14 years+ | Genital stage | Identity vs role confusion | Formal operational | Postconventional morality |

### Using these theories in nursing practice

When using developmental theories, nurses should consider:
1. They are frameworks, not fixed rules.
2. Development occurs across a continuum, not in exact age boxes.
3. Culture, environment, trauma and context shape growth.
4. Individuals vary; theory should guide, not replace, assessment.
5. We should use the theory to understand behaviour and inform patient-centred care.

## 8. Major Factors Influencing Growth and Development

Human growth and development are shaped by a range of interacting factors.

### Major influences

- Genetics and biology
- Prenatal environment and maternal health
- Nutrition and physical health
- Family relationships and attachment
- Culture and social context
- Education and stimulation
- Socioeconomic conditions
- Trauma, stress and adversity
- Emotional safety and supportive environments
- Access to healthcare and community resources

### Key nursing message

Development cannot be understood by looking only at an individual's behaviour. It is shaped by
complex social, biological and environmental influences.

## 9. Consumer and Patient Narratives

Patient narratives are personal stories about health, illness, treatment and lived experience.
These are important forms of evidence and should not be dismissed as “anecdotal.”

### Why narratives matter

- They provide deeper understanding of illness experience.
- They reveal what matters to the patient, not just the diagnosis.
- They influence trust, choice, communication and healthcare decision-making.
- They can improve patient-centred care and service improvement.

### Narrative functions in healthcare

Narratives can:
- help explain illness experience
- support healthcare decisions
- document patient preferences and concerns
- inform policy and service design
- improve communication and patient engagement

### In practice

Nurses should listen carefully to patient stories, ask open questions, and recognise that the
patient’s experience is a valid source of knowledge in clinical care.
