Purpose
-

Oxygen saturation reflects how effectively oxygen is being transported to the body’s tissues. Pulse oximetry is a quick, non‑invasive method used to assess oxygenation and detect early signs of hypoxia. Understanding the physiology behind oxygenation helps nurses interpret SpO₂ readings accurately and respond appropriately.
Quick recap - Physiology of oxygenation
Before measuring oxygen saturation, it’s important to understand how oxygen is transported in the body.
Oxygenation
Oxygenation refers to the delivery of oxygen to the body’s cells and tissues. Oxygen is carried in two forms:
- Bound to haemoglobin (most oxygen)
- Dissolved in plasma (small amount)
Effective oxygenation depends on:
- Ventilation – movement of air in and out of the lungs
- Gas exchange – diffusion of O₂ and CO₂ between alveoli and blood
- Perfusion – delivery of oxygenated blood to tissues
- Cellular respiration – oxygen used within cells

===== FILE: 11 - Terminology used.epub =====
Terminology used
-
-
Pulse
Atrial fibrillation - irregular and often rapid heart rhythm - arrhythmia
Bradycardia – a slower than normal heart rate – below 60 beats per minute
Pulse – measurement of the heart beat
Tachycardia – a faster than normal pulse rate – above 100 beats / minute
Respirations
Bradypnoea (slow breathing rate < 10/11 or below)
Dyspnoea – difficulty breathing causing a feeling of shortness of breath
Eupnoea – easy normal respiration
Tachypnoea (fast breathing rate > 20bpm)
Temperature
Febrile – a temperature generally over 39.5
Hyperthermia - an abnormally high body temperature
Hypothermia – temperature below 35.4
Pyrexia – where the body’s temperature is generally above the normal limit of 37
Thermoregulation - The body’s way of heat regulation to maintain a constant body temp
02 sats
Capillary refill – the time it takes blood to return to the capillaries after they have been compressed
Oxygen saturation – how much of the haemoglobin in the blood is carrying oxygen
Pain
Cutaneous pain – stimulation of the nerve endings in the skin
Somatic pain – non localised and originates in the support structures – ligaments, tendons, nerves – think about jamming your finger in the door
Visceral pain – internal organs; less localised more slowly transmitted than cutaneous pain, sometimes difficult to assess, as it may location may not be directly related to cause
Referred pain – happens in one organ but pain is felt elsewhere – heart attack pain can be in left arm or jaw
Acute pain - sudden onset, relative short duration, mild to severe in intensity; lasts day to about 3-4 weeks; is time limited, such as surgical pain
Chronic pain - constant pain daily 3 months or more
Nociceptive – normal processing of the noxious stimuli, may damage skin if it prolongs, e.g. Somatic pain, visceral pain & fractures
Neuropathic - abnormal processing of pain by peripheral or central nervous system, which could be due to lesions or disease processes, e.g., Peripheral – diabetic neuropathy, carpal tunnel syndrome;
Central – multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury
Blood pressure
Hypertension - a higher-than-normal BP
Hypotension - a lower-than-normal BP
Korotkoff’s sounds – the five distinct phases of sounds heard with a stethoscope during auscultation of the BP
Normotensive – a stable BP
AVPU
The AVPU scale is a quick and easy way to assess a patient’s level of consciousness. It helps you determine how aware and responsive a patient is in emergency or ward settings. Here’s a breakdown of each letter in a way that a first-year nurse can easily understand:
A – Alert - The patient is fully awake—you see that they open their eyes spontaneously, follow commands, and seem fully aware of their surroundings.
What to Look For: They make eye contact, answer questions appropriately, and can follow instructions without needing any stimulation. This is the ideal state, indicating good brain function.
V – Verbal - The patient isn’t completely alert but responds when spoken to.
What to Look For: When you talk to them (even if just calling their name), they open their eyes or make an effort to respond. Their responses might be slower or a bit confused compared to an alert patient. This shows that while they maintain some awareness, they need verbal cues to engage.
P – Pain - The patient doesn’t respond to verbal stimuli but shows a reaction when a painful stimulus is applied.
What to Look For: Use a standardised method like applying a gentle pressure to the nail bed or a sternal rub. A pain response could be a grimace, a slight withdrawal, or a change in their posture. It tells you that the patient’s brainstem is still active, but the level of consciousness is lower than ideal.
U – Unresponsive - The patient does not react at all—even when a painful stimulus is applied.
What to Look For: There is no eye-opening, no verbal response, and no movement. This is the most concerning sign and indicates that the patient is deeply unconscious, signifying a critical situation that needs prompt intervention.

Purpose and physiology of breathing
-
-
Purpose
Respiratory rate is one of the earliest indicators of clinical deterioration. Understanding how breathing works, what affects respiratory rate, and how to measure it accurately is essential for safe nursing practice.
Physiology of breathing
Respiration involves several key processes:
- Pulmonary ventilation — movement of air in and out of the lungs
- Inspiration — drawing air into the lungs
- Expiration — expelling air from the lungs
- External respiration — gas exchange between alveoli and blood
- Internal respiration — gas exchange between blood and tissues

Respiratory assessment (A&B of A-G)
Before counting respirations, nurses perform a brief respiratory assessment:
- Listen to breath sounds
- Observe chest movement
- Identify normal vs abnormal patterns
- Note work of breathing

Key concepts
-
-
Ventilation: Breathing in oxygen and exhaling carbon dioxide.
Diffusion: Movement of gases across concentration gradients (high → low).
Perfusion: Delivery of oxygenated blood to tissues; depends on adequate cardiac output.

- Tachycardia
- Anxiety or restlessness

Obstructive pulmonary diseases
- Asthma

- Chronic bronchitis
Restrictive pulmonary diseases
- Pneumonia
- Pulmonary fibrosis
Diffusion defects
- Emphysema
- Pulmonary oedema
Ventilation–perfusion mismatch
- Right‑sided heart failure
- Pulmonary hypertension

===== FILE: 23 - Pulse oximetry.epub =====
Pulse oximetry
-
-
Pulse oximeters measure how much haemoglobin is carrying oxygen. They are widely used because they are:
- non‑invasive
- inexpensive
- portable
- able to detect hypoxaemia earlier than visible cyanosis
Pulse oximetry is essential for monitoring patients receiving oxygen therapy and identifying early deterioration.
Measuring oxygen saturations
Pulse oximeters use light absorption to calculate oxygen saturation.
How it works:
- Light passes from the probe’s emitter → through the finger → to the detector
- Oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin absorb light differently
- The oximeter analyses the absorbed light to determine SpO₂
Factors affecting accuracy
- Poor perfusion
- Nail polish or acrylic nails
- Movement
- Cold extremities
- Carbon monoxide poisoning
- Incorrect probe placement

===== FILE: 24 - Purpose; Understanding blood pressure.epub =====
Purpose; Understanding blood pressure
-
-
Purpose
Blood pressure (BP) reflects the force of blood against arterial walls and is a key indicator of cardiovascular health. Accurate BP measurement is essential for recognising deterioration, guiding clinical decisions, and ensuring safe patient care. This skill takes practice — but with repetition, you will become confident and competent.
Understanding Blood pressure
Blood pressure is the pressure exerted by circulating blood as it moves through the arteries. Because blood flows in pulses, BP has two components:
- Systolic pressure — pressure during ventricular contraction
- Diastolic pressure — pressure when the ventricles relax
BP is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) and recorded as a fraction (e.g., 120/80 mmHg).

===== FILE: 27 - What do BP numbers mean_.epub =====
What do BP numbers mean?
-
-
When you measure BP, you document it as systolic/diastolic (e.g., 120/80 mmHg).
Systolic pressure (top number)
Pressure in the arteries when the heart contracts.
Diastolic pressure (bottom number)
Pressure in the arteries when the heart relaxes.
Both numbers are important for assessing cardiovascular health.
Normal blood pressure
A normal adult BP is generally:
- Systolic: 90–119 mmHg
- Diastolic: 60–79 mmHg
Elevated blood pressure
BP above 120/80 mmHg is considered elevated. Persistent elevation increases the risk of:
- hypertension
- heart disease
- stroke

Purpose
Pulse (heart rate) reflects how effectively the heart is pumping blood through the body. It is a key indicator of cardiovascular function and one of the earliest signs of improvement or deterioration. Nurses must be able to assess pulse accurately, interpret findings, and document results clearly.
Physiology of the pulse
The pulse is a throbbing sensation felt over a peripheral artery or heard over the apex of the heart. It occurs as a wave of blood is ejected into the arterial system with each contraction of the left ventricle.
Pulse = heart rate → the number of times the heart beats per minute.
Heart rate varies between individuals, so knowing your patient’s baseline is essential

-

First‑year students will assess the radial pulse using palpation.
Technique:

- Apply gentle pressure
- Count for one full minute
- Assess rate, rhythm, and volume
Why 2–3 fingers?
- One finger occludes excess pressure
- Middle finger senses the pulse
- Third finger helps eliminate interference from the ulnar artery
- Thumb is avoided due to its own pulsation

===== FILE: 38 - Purpose_ What is AVPU_.epub =====
Purpose: What is AVPU?
-
-
For the purposes of this unit, we will not delve into a comprehensive neurological assessment. However, we will focus on the AVPU scale, as it pertains to documentation on the vital signs chart. Understanding and accurately recording the AVPU scale is a necessary skill for your practice.
What is ACVPU?
The ACVPU scale is a rapid method used by healthcare professionals to assess and document a patient’s level of consciousness. It is an expanded version of the traditional AVPU scale and provides a clearer picture of a patient’s neurological status. ACVPU stands for Alert, Confusion, Voice, Pain, and Unresponsive, representing five possible levels of responsiveness.
AVPU stands for:
- A – Alert The patient is awake, responsive, and aware of their surroundings.
- C - Confusion - new onset or worsening confusion
- V – Verbal The patient responds to verbal stimuli (e.g., speaking, calling their name).
- P – Pain The patient responds only to painful stimuli (e.g., trapezius squeeze, sternal rub).
- U – Unresponsive The patient does not respond to verbal or painful stimuli.
This assessment is crucial in all clinical settings because it helps identify early neurological changes that may indicate deterioration or the need for urgent intervention. By determining whether a patient is alert, confused, responding to voice, responding to pain, or unresponsive, healthcare providers can make timely decisions about the urgency of care required.
Why ACVPU Matters
Assessing a patient’s level of consciousness using ACVPU helps detect changes that may be caused by conditions such as:
- stroke
- head injury
- hypoxia
- hypoglycemia
- infection
- medication effects
- deterioration requiring escalation
A sudden shift — for example, from A (Alert) to C (Confusion) or V (Voice) — is a red flag and must be escalated immediately according to Between the Flags criteria.
Documenting ACVPU
On the SAGO chart, ACVPU is documented using a single letter in the neurological section:
- A – Alert
- C – Confusion
- V – Responds to Voice
- P – Responds to Pain
- U – Unresponsive
Accurate documentation ensures the whole healthcare team is aware of the patient’s neurological status and any changes over time. This supports safe monitoring, treatment planning, and communication. Consistent, precise documentation also helps identify trends that may indicate improvement or deterioration.

Introduction to the Sago Chart
-
-
The Standard Adult General Observation (SAGO) Chart is the NSW Health tool used to document vital signs and recognise early deterioration. It uses colour‑coded zones (Yellow and Red) to guide escalation and supports the Between the Flags (BTF) safety system.
NSW-Health-Standard-Adult-General-Observation-Chart.pdf
Principles of accurate documentation
High‑quality documentation must be:
Timely
Recorded as soon as possible after care or observation to ensure accuracy.
Legible
Clear and easy to read to prevent misinterpretation.
Sequential
Entered in the correct time order to support trend recognition.
Consistent with the Patient’s Presentation
Values should match what you see clinically; recheck anything that doesn’t fit.
Free from Gaps or Overwriting
Each entry must be complete, with no blank spaces or altered records.
Verified When Values Are Unusual
Recheck any observation that seems unlikely or inconsistent.
Legal Requirements for Documentation
Legal considerations underpin every aspect of nursing practice, and documentation is no exception. Accurate, timely, and compliant record‑keeping is both a professional responsibility and a legal requirement that safeguards patient safety and supports accountability.
Accuracy and Timeliness
Documentation must reflect correct data and be recorded promptly after care or observation. This ensures clinical decisions are based on reliable, up‑to‑date information.
Clarity and Legibility
Entries must be easy to read and understand. Clear documentation prevents misinterpretation and reduces the risk of error.
Comprehensive Details
Include all relevant information—time, date, patient identifiers, and clinical context—so the record provides a complete and accurate picture of care.
Compliance with Policies and Standards
Follow organisational guidelines, professional standards, and legal requirements. This ensures consistency, accountability, and adherence to best practice.
Secure and Permanent Records
Documentation must be stored safely and remain permanent, whether electronic or paper-based. This protects patients confidentiality and provides a legal record of care

Why documentation matters
-
-

===== FILE: 57 - Patient safety.epub =====
Patient safety
-
-
-
“First, do no harm” is the most fundamental principle of any health care service. Patient safety, as defined by the is primarily concerned with preventing harm to patients within the healthcare system. This encompasses three key principles: preventing errors, learning from errors when they do occur, and fostering a culture of safety involving healthcare professionals, organisations, and patients. A more detailed explanation extends the concept to "freedom from accidental or preventable injuries produced by medical care."
Patient safety practices are those designed to reduce the risk of adverse events associated with medical care across various diagnoses and conditions. It's important to note that not all practices have been thoroughly studied for their effectiveness in harm prevention.
The World Health Organization (WHO) characterises patient safety as a healthcare discipline that has evolved in response to the increasing complexity of healthcare systems and the rise in patient harm within healthcare facilities. Its primary aim is to prevent and mitigate risks, errors, and harm that patients may encounter during their healthcare experience. Understanding the causes behind adverse outcomes is crucial for patients and nurses to minimise harm.
Patient safety holds immense importance because it aligns with the fundamental goal of providing the best care for patients. Achieving this involves various actions such as adhering to policies, raising concerns, critical thinking, self-reflection, and incident reporting. Incident reporting plays a significant role in improving healthcare safety and is a shared responsibility among healthcare professionals, including nurses.
The dedication to healthcare safety is underscored by establishing Australia's National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards (NSQHS Standards). This initiative, driven by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, aims to enhance patient safety and quality outcomes.

Factors contributing to errors in Healthcare
Errors in healthcare are often the result of a combination of factors rather than a single cause, highlighting the need for a comprehensive approach to error prevention and patient safety.
The Swiss Cheese Model is a risk management concept that was developed by James Reason in the 1990s and is used in various industries, including healthcare. It illustrates how multiple layers of defense, represented as slices of Swiss cheese, can have holes or vulnerabilities. When these vulnerabilities align or "line up," errors or accidents can occur, emphasising the importance of implementing multiple safeguards to prevent adverse events.

The Swiss cheese model of risk
Errors in healthcare rarely arise from a single mistake. Instead, they occur when multiple system weaknesses align. The Swiss Cheese Model, developed by James Reason, illustrates how each layer of defence has “holes” — vulnerabilities. When these holes line up, an error can reach the patient.
Understanding this model helps nurses recognise:
- How system factors contribute to harm
- Why multiple safeguards are essential
- How communication, workload, environment, and human factors influence safety
