## 1. Recognising Deterioration: Clinical Frameworks

### Between the Flags (BTF)
NSW Health's safety-net system for recognising and responding to clinical deterioration.
Uses colour-coded **"track and trigger"** charts to identify early warning signs and
standardise escalation.

- 🟡 **Yellow Zone** = Clinical Review
- 🔴 **Red Zone** = Rapid Response
- Standardised calling criteria
- Supports the *Recognising and Responding to Acute Deterioration* NSQHS Standard

Standard observation charts exist in both **paper and electronic** form (NSW public hospitals
use the Cerner eMR). Paper-based forms are used during downtime in facilities that normally
use eMR.

### A-G Assessment
A systematic method for rapid patient assessment, helping nurses identify life-threatening
problems early and intervene before moving on.

**Why it matters:**
- Provides structure in emergencies
- Helps identify deterioration early
- Links directly to vital sign interpretation
- Builds clinical reasoning skills

> The full A-G assessment is taught in NURS2004 / NURS5012 (Health Assessment Across the
> Lifespan) — this module introduces only the foundational concepts. Vital signs are explored
> in the order used on the **SAGO chart**, aligned with the A-G framework.

## 2. Understanding Vital Signs

Vital signs reflect the functioning of key body systems — **cardiovascular, respiratory,
neurological, and thermoregulatory**. Although these processes occur automatically, nurses
must assess whether they are functioning within expected ranges.

| Vital Sign | Abbreviation |
|---|---|
| Temperature | T |
| Pulse / Heart Rate | P/HR |
| Respiratory Rate | R |
| Blood Pressure | BP |
| Oxygen Saturation | SpO₂ |
| Pain (the "fifth vital sign") | — |
| AVPU / ACVPU (rapid neurological assessment) | — |

## 3. Respiratory Rate

### Physiology of Breathing
- **Pulmonary ventilation** — movement of air in and out of the lungs
- **Inspiration** / **Expiration**
- **External respiration** — gas exchange between alveoli and blood
- **Internal respiration** — gas exchange between blood and tissues

### Respiratory Assessment (A & B of A-G)
Before counting respirations:
- Listen to breath sounds
- Observe chest movement
- Identify normal vs abnormal patterns
- Note work of breathing

### Breathing Pattern Terminology
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Eupnoea | Easy, normal respiration |
| Tachypnoea | Fast breathing rate (>20 bpm) |
| Bradypnoea | Slow breathing rate (<10/11 bpm) |
| Dyspnoea | Difficulty breathing / shortness of breath |

### Factors Affecting Respirations
- Acute pain — ↑ rate, ↓ depth
- Age — neonates 30–60 bpm; adults 12–20 bpm
- Brain lesions — can alter rate and depth
- Exercise — ↑ rate and depth
- Gender — males more diaphragmatic; females more intercostal

### Measuring Respiratory Rate
Respirations must be counted for **one full minute**. While counting, assess:
- Chest rise and fall
- Symmetry
- Use of accessory muscles
- Work of breathing
- Noisy or abnormal sounds

### Documenting Respiratory Rate
- Use the **Airway/Breathing** section of the SAGO chart
- NSW and QLD charts differ — both are shown in this module
- Documentation may be paper-based or electronic (eMR)
- **Mandatory component of the Clinical Skills Assessment**

## 4. Oxygen Saturation (SpO₂) & Pulse Oximetry

### Key Concepts
- **Ventilation**: breathing in oxygen and exhaling carbon dioxide
- **Diffusion**: movement of gases across concentration gradients (high → low)
- **Perfusion**: delivery of oxygenated blood to tissues; depends on adequate cardiac output
- **Hypoxia**: low oxygen levels in tissues

**Common signs of hypoxia:** tachycardia, anxiety/restlessness, pallor or cyanosis (a *late*
sign), increased work of breathing, tachypnoea

**Disease processes affecting oxygenation:**
- Obstructive pulmonary diseases: asthma, emphysema, chronic bronchitis
- Restrictive pulmonary diseases: pneumonia, pulmonary fibrosis
- Diffusion defects: emphysema, pulmonary oedema
- Ventilation–perfusion mismatch: right-sided heart failure, pulmonary hypertension

### Understanding Oxygen Saturation
Oxygen saturation = the **percentage of haemoglobin molecules carrying oxygen**.
- Analogy: haemoglobin = "cars", oxygen = "passengers", blood vessels = "roads"
- If 8 of 16 haemoglobin "cars" carry oxygen → saturation = 50%
- "Normal" SpO₂ varies depending on the patient's condition, history, and baseline

### How Pulse Oximeters Work
- Light passes from the probe's emitter → through the finger → to the detector
- Oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin absorb light differently
- The oximeter analyses the absorbed light to calculate SpO₂

**Advantages:** non-invasive, inexpensive, portable, able to detect hypoxaemia earlier than
visible cyanosis

**Factors affecting accuracy:**
- Poor perfusion
- Nail polish or acrylic nails
- Movement
- Cold extremities
- Carbon monoxide poisoning
- Incorrect probe placement

### Documentation
- On the SAGO chart, under Airway/Breathing, there is an Oxygen component
- Since this unit does not administer oxygen therapy, document **"RA" (Room Air)** in the
  device/mode field
- **Mandatory component of the SAGO chart and the Clinical Skills Assessment**

## 5. Blood Pressure (BP)

### Understanding Blood Pressure
Blood pressure is the pressure exerted by circulating blood as it moves through the arteries.
Because blood flows in pulses, BP has two components:
- **Systolic pressure** — pressure during ventricular contraction
- **Diastolic pressure** — pressure when the ventricles relax

Measured in **mmHg**, recorded as a fraction (e.g., 120/80 mmHg).

### Physiology and Regulation
BP is influenced by:
- Cardiac output
- Blood volume
- Peripheral vascular resistance
- Arterial elasticity
- Blood viscosity

### What Do BP Numbers Mean?
| Category | Systolic | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|
| Normal adult BP | 90–119 mmHg | 60–79 mmHg |
| Elevated | above 120/80 mmHg | — |

Persistent elevation increases risk of hypertension, heart disease, and stroke.

### Factors Affecting Blood Pressure
| Non-modifiable | Modifiable |
|---|---|
| Age | Smoking |
| Genetics | Diet |
| Family history | Physical activity |
| | Stress |
| | Alcohol intake |
| | Obesity |

### Assessment of Blood Pressure
Equipment needed: sphygmomanometer, stethoscope, correct cuff size, correct patient
positioning, correct technique. (Wrong cuff size gives inaccurate readings.)

**Key steps:**
1. Perform hand hygiene
2. Position the patient comfortably
3. Locate the brachial or radial pulse
4. Apply the cuff correctly
5. Inflate and deflate at the correct rate
6. Identify systolic and diastolic sounds (Korotkoff sounds)
7. Document immediately

> Either the radial or brachial pulse can be used to obtain the baseline; this module
> **advocates using the radial pulse**.

### Terminology
- **Hypertension** — higher-than-normal BP
- **Hypotension** — lower-than-normal BP
- **Korotkoff's sounds** — the five distinct phases of sound heard with a stethoscope during
  auscultation of BP
- **Normotensive** — a stable BP

### Documenting Blood Pressure
- Use the **Circulation** section of the SAGO chart
- NSW and QLD charts differ — both shown in this module
- Record BP immediately after measurement
- **Mandatory component of the Clinical Skills Assessment**

## 6. Pulse / Heart Rate

### Physiology of the Pulse
The pulse is a throbbing sensation felt over a peripheral artery or heard over the apex of the
heart. It occurs as a wave of blood is ejected into the arterial system with each contraction
of the left ventricle. Pulse = heart rate → number of beats per minute. Heart rate varies
between individuals, so knowing the patient's baseline is essential.

### Characteristics of the Pulse
| Characteristic | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rate** | Beats per minute; normal adult range **60–100 bpm** (this module's reference range) |
| **Rhythm** | Regular or irregular; irregular = arrhythmia |
| **Volume (Strength)** | Force of blood with each beat: bounding, strong, weak, thready |

### Factors Affecting Pulse Rate
Exercise, fever, pain, anxiety, medications, haemorrhage, age, temperature, disease processes
(Beale & Nanayakkara, 2018)

### Pulse Sites
- **Radial** — most common for adults and children >12 months
- **Brachial** — used in BP measurement
- **Carotid** — used in emergencies
- **Femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis** — assess lower limb circulation
- **Apical** — auscultated in infants <12 months or when rhythm is irregular

### Assessing the Radial Pulse (Palpation Technique)
- Use **2–3 fingers** (never the thumb — it has its own pulse)
- Apply gentle pressure
- Count for one full minute
- Assess rate, rhythm, and volume

**Why 2–3 fingers?**
- One finger occludes with excess pressure
- The middle finger senses the pulse
- The third finger helps eliminate interference from the ulnar artery
- Thumb is avoided due to its own pulsation

### Terminology
- **Tachycardia** — faster than normal pulse rate (>100 bpm)
- **Bradycardia** — slower than normal heart rate (<60 bpm)
- **Atrial fibrillation** — irregular and often rapid heart rhythm (arrhythmia)

### Documenting Pulse/Heart Rate
- Use the **Circulation** section of the SAGO chart
- **Mandatory component of the Clinical Skills Assessment**

### ⚠️ Reflective Questions (bring to labs for discussion)
1. Why do we assess the **radial pulse** in first-year clinical assessments?
2. Why do we use **2–3 fingers instead of the thumb** when measuring pulse?

## 7. AVPU / ACVPU (Level of Consciousness)

> This unit does not cover a comprehensive neurological assessment — the focus is on the
> AVPU/ACVPU scale as it pertains to documentation on the vital signs chart.

### The Five Levels of ACVPU
| Letter | Meaning | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Alert | Fully awake, opens eyes spontaneously, follows commands, aware of surroundings |
| **C** | Confusion | New onset or worsening confusion |
| **V** | Verbal | Responds when spoken to (e.g., opens eyes/responds to name); may be slower or confused |
| **P** | Pain | Responds only to painful stimuli (e.g., trapezius squeeze, sternal rub) — grimace, withdrawal, posture change |
| **U** | Unresponsive | No reaction at all — no eye-opening, no verbal response, no movement |

### Why ACVPU Matters
Helps detect changes that may indicate: stroke, head injury, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia,
infection, medication effects, or deterioration requiring escalation.

> A sudden shift — e.g., from **A (Alert)** to **C (Confusion)** or **V (Voice)** — is a
> **red flag** and must be escalated immediately according to Between the Flags criteria.

### Documenting ACVPU
Recorded using a single letter in the neurological section of the SAGO chart (A/C/V/P/U).
**Mandatory component of the Clinical Skills Assessment.**

## 8. Pain — "The Fifth Vital Sign"

### Understanding Pain
> "Pain is whatever the experiencing person says it is, existing whenever they say it does."
> — Pasero & McCaffery (2011)

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential
tissue damage, influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors.

**Untreated pain can lead to:**
reduced mobility, decreased nutritional intake, impaired sleep, delayed healing,
immunosuppression, emotional distress (anxiety, depression), increased risk of chronic pain

### Types and Classification of Pain
**By duration:**
- **Acute pain** — sudden onset, short-term, protective
- **Persistent (chronic) pain** — lasts >3 months, may not have a clear cause

**By cause:**
- **Nociceptive pain** — tissue injury (somatic or visceral)
  - *Cutaneous pain* — stimulation of nerve endings in the skin
  - *Somatic pain* — non-localised, originates in support structures (ligaments, tendons,
    nerves), e.g. jamming a finger in a door
  - *Visceral pain* — internal organs; less localised, more slowly transmitted than cutaneous
    pain, sometimes difficult to assess since location may not relate directly to the cause
- **Neuropathic pain** — abnormal processing of pain by the peripheral or central nervous
  system due to lesions/disease
  - Peripheral: diabetic neuropathy, carpal tunnel syndrome
  - Central: multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury
- **Somatoform pain** — psychological origin

**Other classifications:**
- **Referred pain** — occurs in one organ but felt elsewhere (e.g., heart attack pain felt in
  the left arm or jaw)
- Phantom pain
- Breakthrough pain

(Hall, Glew & Rhodes, 2022)

### Factors Affecting the Pain Experience
Age, culture, previous pain experiences, emotional state, fatigue, attention/distraction,
meaning of pain, social support

### Assessing Pain
**First step:** Ask the patient — pain cannot be assumed, minimised, or judged.

Pain assessment includes: onset, location, duration, characteristics, aggravating/relieving
factors, impact on function, patient goals

**Pain assessment tools (focus of this module):**
- **Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)**: 0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain imaginable
- **Verbal Rating Scale (VRS)**: no pain / mild pain / moderate pain / severe pain /
  excruciating pain

(Appropriate for adolescents, adults, and older adults who can understand the scale)

### Documenting Pain
Documentation should include:
- Pain score (NRS or VRS)
- Location and characteristics
- Interventions provided
- Patient response to treatment
- Reassessment time

May be documented on paper-based charts, eMR, or the SAGO chart (pain score section).
**Mandatory component of the Clinical Skills Assessment.**

## 9. Body Temperature

### Understanding Body Temperature
Temperature reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss — measured in degrees
Celsius (°C).

**Normal core temperature range:** 36.0°C – 37.5°C (ranges vary slightly between textbooks
and clinical guidelines)

**Temperature varies due to:**
- Individual differences
- Time of day (lower in the morning, higher in late afternoon)
- Measurement site (core vs surface temperature)

**Common measurement sites:** tympanic, temporal, axilla, oral, rectal (Hall, Glew & Rhodes,
2022)

### Temperature Homeostasis
The body maintains a stable internal temperature through homeostasis, regardless of
environmental conditions. Thermoregulation involves:
- **Heat production** (metabolism, muscle activity)
- **Heat loss** (skin, respiration, sweating)
- **Neural control** (hypothalamus)

This balance is essential for enzyme function, cellular activity, and overall physiological
stability.

> Reflection: How does the environment affect temperature readings? Why do post-operative
> patients often shiver?

### Terminology
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pyrexia | Body temperature generally above the normal upper limit of 37°C |
| Febrile | Temperature generally over 39.5°C |
| Hyperthermia | Abnormally high body temperature |
| Hypothermia | Temperature below 35.4°C |
| Thermoregulation | The body's way of regulating heat to maintain constant body temperature |

## 10. Documentation

### Why Documentation Matters
- **Baseline indicators** — establish a starting point for the patient's normal physiological
  state; deviations may signal early deterioration or improvement
- **Trend monitoring** — regular documentation reveals patterns (rising temperature, falling
  BP, increasing respiratory rate) that may indicate clinical change
- **Clinical decision-making** — supports timely interventions, escalation, and treatment
  planning
- **Communication** — vital signs form a shared language across the healthcare team
- **Legal and ethical accountability** — documentation is a legal record of care provided

### Principles of High-Quality Documentation
| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| Clear | Easy to read and understand |
| Legible | Handwriting or digital entries must be unambiguous |
| Concise | Only relevant information included |
| Contemporaneous | Recorded immediately after measurement |
| Progressive | Showing changes over time |
| Accurate | Correct values, units, and interpretation |
| Complete | Includes assessments, actions taken, outcomes, and reassessment |
| Sequential | Entered in correct time order to support trend recognition |
| Free from gaps or overwriting | Each entry complete; no blanks or altered records |

**Documentation forms:** paper-based, electronic (eMR), or hybrid, depending on the facility.
**Examples:** care plans, observation charts, handover notes, referral letters, pathology
requests, operation notes, discharge summaries.

### Interpreting Vital Signs Before Documenting
Documentation is not simply recording numbers — it requires clinical interpretation.
Before documenting, ask:
- Is this value normal for this patient?
- Is it consistent with their baseline?
- Is there a trend developing?
- Does this require escalation (e.g., Between the Flags criteria)?
- Do I need to reassess sooner?
- Do I need to communicate this to the RN or medical team?

### Where to Document Vital Signs
- SAGO charts (NSW and QLD versions differ — both included in this module)
- Electronic medical records (eMR)
- Clinical notes, when relevant (escalation, interventions, patient response)

Each vital sign has a designated area on the chart: Temperature, Pulse/Heart rate,
Respiratory rate, Blood pressure, Oxygen saturation, Pain score, AVPU.

### Documentation and Patient Safety
Poor or missed documentation can lead to: misdiagnosis, delayed treatment, medication errors,
failure to escalate, preventable deterioration.

High-quality documentation supports: early recognition of deterioration, safe handover,
effective teamwork, patient advocacy, legal protection for the nurse.

### The SAGO Chart (Standard Adult General Observation Chart)
The NSW Health tool used to document vital signs and recognise early deterioration. Uses
colour-coded zones (Yellow and Red) to guide escalation and supports the Between the Flags
(BTF) safety system.

**Principles of accurate documentation:**
- **Timely** — recorded as soon as possible after care/observation
- **Legible** — clear and easy to read
- **Sequential** — entered in correct time order
- **Consistent with the patient's presentation** — recheck anything that doesn't fit
- **Free from gaps or overwriting** — complete entries, no blanks or altered records
- **Verified when values are unusual** — recheck any observation that seems unlikely

**Legal requirements for documentation:**
- Accuracy and timeliness
- Clarity and legibility
- Comprehensive details (time, date, patient identifiers, clinical context)
- Compliance with policies and standards
- Secure and permanent records

**Link to national standards:** Aligns with the **NSQHS Standards** set by the Australian
Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) — particularly **Standard 1**
(Clinical Governance) and **Standard 6** (Communicating for Safety).

## 11. Patient Safety

### Core Concept
> "First, do no harm" — the most fundamental principle of any healthcare service.

Patient safety, as defined by WHO, is primarily concerned with preventing harm to patients
within the healthcare system, encompassing three key principles:
1. **Preventing errors**
2. **Learning from errors when they occur**
3. **Fostering a culture of safety** involving healthcare professionals, organisations, and
   patients

A more detailed definition extends the concept to "freedom from accidental or preventable
injuries produced by medical care."

WHO characterises patient safety as a discipline that has evolved in response to the
increasing complexity of healthcare systems and the rise in patient harm within healthcare
facilities. Its primary aim is to prevent and mitigate risks, errors, and harm patients may
encounter.

**Achieving patient safety involves:** adhering to policies, raising concerns, critical
thinking, self-reflection, and incident reporting.

**Australia's response:** the **National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS)
Standards**, driven by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care.

### Factors Contributing to Errors in Healthcare — The Swiss Cheese Model
Developed by **James Reason** in the 1990s, this risk management concept is used across
industries including healthcare. It illustrates how multiple layers of defence (represented
as slices of Swiss cheese) each have "holes" — vulnerabilities. When these holes **align**,
an error can pass through all layers and reach the patient.

Errors in healthcare are rarely the result of a single mistake — they occur when multiple
system weaknesses align, emphasising the need for **multiple safeguards**.

Understanding this model helps nurses recognise:
- How system factors contribute to harm
- Why multiple safeguards are essential
- How communication, workload, environment, and human factors influence safety

### Learning From and Reporting Errors and Near Misses
Australia's health system performs well internationally, but safety challenges remain.
Learning from errors is essential for improvement. Reporting incidents — including
**near misses** — helps identify patterns, gaps, and risks that may otherwise go unnoticed.

**Incident reporting:**
- Supports system improvement
- Informs safety campaigns
- Prevents future harm
- Strengthens a culture of openness and accountability

> As a student, you will be supported when completing incident reports and guided through
> the process during placement.
