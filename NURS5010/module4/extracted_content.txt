Introduction to pressure area/skin tears
-
-
Pressure area, pressure sores, pressure ulcer, decubitus ulcer and bed sores are all commonly used terms to describe pressure injuries. A pressure injury is any injury to the skin and/or underlying structures/tissues. If pressure is placed on an area for long periods of time, or if the skin is placed under constant pressure for varying periods of time, it can result in limiting nutrients and oxygen supply to the tissues and eventually, the skin dies. Have a look at the picture, which identifies the most common areas for pressure injuries.
The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) has recognised pressure injuries as the fifth (5th) most commonly occurring preventable condition. It has also been identified that pressure injuries have a negative effect on the patient’s well-being, and it adds an additional financial cost to the healthcare system, with the cost of treatment for each pressure injury being around AU$98,489.22. Australian public hospitals estimated that around AU$983 million per annum is spent on factors relating to pressure injuries, which represents approximately 1.9% of all public hospital expenditure (Nguyen et al., 2015; Wilson et al., 2019).
Earlier in the module, you looked at skin integrity and read about the functions of the skin. We will now take that further and look at how a pressure injury develops. This is an introduction to pressure injuries, as they will be covered in more depth throughout other units in your nursing studies.

===== FILE: 07 - Skin integrity.epub =====
Skin integrity
-
-
Intact skin refers to the presence of normal skin and the skin layers being uninterrupted by wounds. Good skin integrity is vital for good health. Intact skin acts as a barrier that helps repel microorganisms, toxins, and the sun, to name a few, from damaging the skin and allowing us to remain infection-free (Payne, 2020).
As the skin loses some elasticity during the aging process, it is important that as nurses, you recognise the changes that occur and the maintenance that is needed to preserve skin integrity across the lifespan (Berman et al., 2021).
Chronic illnesses can take a toll on our patients’ skin, as observed in a patient who has an impairment of the cardiovascular system, and they have issues with their circulating blood. These patients may present with shiny skin on their legs and skin that can damage easily. Additionally, for our patients who are bed-bound due to illness, their skin integrity can be damaged as a result of pressure, and they can develop what are known as pressure injuries/pressure sores/ pressure wounds.

===== FILE: 10 - Skin assessment.epub =====
Skin assessment
-
-
Purpose
To develop your ability to recognise normal and abnormal skin findings, understand what they indicate about a patient’s health, and apply this knowledge to prevent complications such as pressure injuries.
What is Skin Assessment?
Skin assessment is a fundamental part of daily nursing care. It involves carefully observing the skin to identify what is normal and detecting early signs of abnormalities that may indicate underlying health issues.
Normal skin is typically:
- intact
- evenly coloured
- smooth in texture
- free from rashes, lesions, or breaks
Abnormal findings may include:
- redness
- swelling
- discolouration
- wounds or breaks in the skin
- areas of warmth, hardness, or moisture
These changes can signal problems such as pressure injuries, infection, inflammation, poor circulation, or systemic conditions like diabetes or vascular disease.
Why Skin Assessment Matters
Incorporating skin assessments into routine nursing care allows for early detection of issues before they become serious. For example:
- Persistent redness may indicate pressure injury risk
- Non‑healing wounds may suggest poor circulation or immune compromise
- Dry, fragile skin may reflect nutritional deficiencies or dehydration
Early recognition leads to timely intervention, preventing complications and improving patient outcomes.
How This Links to Pressure Injury Prevention
After viewing the consumer video, you’ve seen the real‑life impact of pressure injuries.
Skin assessment is one of the most effective nursing actions to prevent them.
By routinely assessing the skin, nurses can:
- identify pressure areas early
- implement repositioning and pressure‑relief strategies
- escalate concerns promptly
- protect patients from avoidable harm
This proactive approach supports safe, patient‑centred care and strengthens your clinical reasoning.
Quick Checklist: What to Look For During a Skin Assessment
Skin Assessment Checklist
When assessing a patient’s skin, look for:
1. Colour
- Normal: even tone, appropriate for ethnicity
- Abnormal: redness, pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, mottling
2. Temperature
- Normal: warm
- Abnormal: hot (infection/inflammation), cool (poor perfusion)
3. Moisture
- Normal: dry, not flaky
- Abnormal: excessive moisture, sweating, maceration
4. Integrity
- Normal: intact skin
- Abnormal: tears, cracks, blisters, open areas, wounds
5. Texture
- Normal: smooth, supple
- Abnormal: rough, thin, fragile, thickened
6. Sensation
- Ask the patient: pain? numbness? tingling?
- Loss of sensation increases pressure injury risk
7. Pressure Areas
Check bony prominences for:
- redness
- non‑blanching areas
- warmth or hardness
- early signs of pressure injury
8. Devices & Equipment
Look under/around:
- oxygen tubing
- catheters
- splints
- compression devices
- dressings
- These can cause pressure or friction injuries.

Introduction to APAT and Intentional Hourly Rounding
-
-
In clinical practice, nurses rely on structured assessment tools and routine safety checks to ensure patients receive consistent, high‑quality care. Two key tools that support this work are the Adult Patient Assessment Tool (APAT) and Intentional Hourly Rounding (IHR). Both play an important role in preventing patient deterioration, improving communication, and supporting safe pressure‑area care.
Adult Patient Assessment Tool (APAT)
The APAT is a comprehensive assessment framework that guides nurses through a systematic review of the patient’s condition. It prompts you to collect cues, interpret information, and identify risks — including those related to mobility, skin integrity, nutrition, continence, cognition, and comfort.
Because the APAT is structured around the same principles you have already learned — clinical reasoning, person‑centred care, emotional intelligence, and reflective practice — it helps you make sense of what you see, hear, and document. When used consistently, the APAT supports early recognition of pressure‑area risk, ensuring timely interventions such as repositioning, equipment use, skin inspection, and escalation.
Adult patient assessment tool (APAT)(1).pdfIntentional Hourly Rounding (IHR)
Intentional Hourly Rounding is a proactive nursing practice that ensures regular, purposeful contact with every patient. During each round, nurses check the “4 Ps” (or similar framework):
- Pain
- Position
- Personal needs
- Placement of items
This structured approach helps prevent pressure injuries by ensuring patients are repositioned regularly, supported with appropriate equipment, and assessed for discomfort or early skin changes. IHR also strengthens communication, reduces call‑bell use, and builds trust — aligning closely with the emotional intelligence and person‑centred care principles explored earlier in the module.
Intentional hourly rounding.pdf
How These Tools Work Together in Pressure‑Area Care
When combined, the APAT and IHR provide nurses with a powerful safety net:
- APAT identifies risk
- IHR provides routine prevention
- Both reinforce clinical reasoning, reflection, and timely action
They help nurses deliver consistent, evidence‑based care, reduce the likelihood of pressure injuries, and ensure patients feel seen, supported, and safe.

Waterlow Risk Assessment Tool
-
-
The Waterlow Risk Assessment Tool is a widely used clinical tool designed to assess a patient’s risk of developing pressure ulcers, also known as bedsores. Developed by Judy Waterlow in 1985, this tool evaluates various factors that contribute to pressure ulcer risk, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), skin type, continence, mobility, nutrition, and special risk factors such as existing medical conditions.
Why is it important?
- Early Identification: The Waterlow tool helps healthcare professionals identify patients at risk of developing pressure ulcers early, allowing for timely intervention and prevention strategies.
- Comprehensive Assessment: By considering multiple factors, the tool provides a holistic view of the patient’s condition, ensuring that all potential risks are accounted for.
- Guiding Interventions: The scores generated by the Waterlow tool categorise patients into different risk levels (low, moderate, high, and very high), guiding healthcare providers in implementing appropriate preventive measures, such as frequent repositioning, use of pressure-relieving devices, and enhanced skin care.
- Improving Patient Outcomes: Regular use of the Waterlow tool in clinical practice can significantly reduce the incidence of pressure ulcers, improving patient comfort, reducing complications, and enhancing overall health outcomes.
Understanding and utilising the Waterlow Risk Assessment Tool is essential for nurses and other healthcare professionals to provide high-quality, proactive care and prevent the development of pressure ulcers in vulnerable patients. In addition, there are various tools used in the hospital to assist with the prevention of pressure area care

Physiology of bowel elimination
-
-
The digestive system is constantly working to break down food, absorb nutrients, and eliminate waste products. Food travels through a gastrointestinal tract approximately nine metres long, taking up to 40 hours to move through the body. Elimination of waste is a normal and essential body function that supports overall health and wellbeing. Bowel elimination refers to the removal of solid waste from the body. When considering elimination, it is important to understand the role of the gastrointestinal system, which extends from the mouth to the anus and includes the organs responsible for digestion and waste removal.
Although elimination is a natural process, bowel habits vary between individuals. Differences in frequency, consistency, and ease of elimination are influenced by a range of factors and should always be considered within the context of what is normal for that person.

===== FILE: 17 - Abdominal assessment.epub =====
Abdominal assessment
-
-
Purpose
To introduce you to the fundamentals of abdominal assessment, with a focus on auscultating bowel sounds. This skill helps you identify normal and abnormal gastrointestinal function and supports safe decision‑making when caring for patients with altered elimination.
Why We Assess the Abdomen in Elimination Care
When a patient reports changes in bowel habits, discomfort, or altered elimination, abdominal assessment provides important clues about what is happening in the digestive tract. For this week, we are focusing specifically on bowel sounds, which help us understand gastrointestinal motility.
You will build on this foundation in NURS2004: Foundations of Health Assessment, where you will learn the full abdominal assessment sequence.
How we auscultate the abdomen: Focusing on bowel sounds
The evaluation of our patient’s digestive system includes listening to your patients bowel sounds. While generally you would undertake an abdominal assessment, for the purpose of this week, we will be primarily concentrating on the evaluation of bowel sounds.
After obtaining the patient’s history regarding elimination, the following step is to listen to the bowel noises in order to gather additional information about what is occurring in the lower digestive tract. It is best to address the patient’s discomfort first if they are complaining of it so that when you auscultate the abdomen, you will have a better idea of what is going on.
When auscultating the abdomen, we split it into four (4) quadrants by drawing an imaginary line across the middle, both above and downward, as shown below: left upper quadrant, left lower quadrant, right upper quadrant, and right lower quadrant. This is done because it gives a more comprehensive picture of the abdomen. Some will segment it into nine regions, but for the purposes of this article, we’ll stick with the four-region arrangement.
Below is a simple guide to help you understand the key steps involved in auscultating bowel sounds
1. Start with the Patient’s History
Before listening to bowel sounds, ask about:
- usual bowel habits
- recent changes
- pain or discomfort
- diet and fluid intake
- medications
If the patient is in pain, address this first — it will help you interpret what you hear.
2. Quadrants of the Abdomen
For assessment, the abdomen is divided into four quadrants:
- Right Upper Quadrant (RUQ)
- Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ)
- Left Upper Quadrant (LUQ)
- Left Lower Quadrant (LLQ)
This helps you systematically compare findings and identify where abnormalities occur.
https://medlineplus.gov/ency/images/ency/fullsize/19578.jpg
3. Why We Auscultate Before Palpation
Palpation can stimulate or alter bowel activity.
Listening first ensures you are hearing the bowel’s natural activity.
4. How to Auscultate
- Begin in the Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ)
- Move clockwise through all four quadrants
- Listen long enough to determine if sounds are present, absent, or abnormal
- Be aware that bowel sounds may be harder to hear in patients with a thicker abdominal wall
5. What Normal Bowel Sounds Sound Like
Normal bowel sounds are:
- high‑pitched
- gurgling
- irregular
- occurring every 5–15 seconds
They may be louder if the patient is hungry.
6. Abnormal Bowel Sounds
These sounds can indicate underlying pathology:
- Bruits – swishing over major arteries (vascular narrowing or aneurysm)
- Pops/tinkles – high‑pitched sounds suggesting early obstruction
- Rushes – high‑pitched sounds with cramping (obstruction)
- Borborygmi – loud, hyperactive sounds (hunger or hypermotility)
- Rubs – grating sounds (peritoneal inflammation)
- Venous hum – soft humming (portal hypertension)
- Decreased/absent sounds – peritonitis or paralytic ileus
This focused assessment helps you recognise early signs of altered gastrointestinal function and supports safe, evidence‑based care, and guide further assessment and escalation.

Assisting with bowel elimination (including suppository administration)
-
-
Purpose
Assisting with bowel elimination is an important aspect of nursing care, particularly for patients who are unwell, immobile, or unable to use the toilet independently. Nursing care in this area focuses on supporting safe elimination while maintaining patient comfort, dignity, and privacy. This may include helping patients use a bedpan, ensuring proper positioning, and providing hygiene care following elimination.
As a nurse, it is essential to combine clinical skill with sensitivity, recognising that patients may feel embarrassed or uncomfortable. Providing reassurance, maintaining a respectful approach, and protecting the patient’s dignity all contribute to a positive and safe care experience.
General Assistance Strategies
Supporting a patient with bowel elimination involves a combination of practical skills, communication, and sensitivity. As a nurse, your role is to promote comfort, dignity, and safety while helping the patient meet a basic physiological need.
Key strategies include:
- Positioning for effective elimination
- Ensure the patient is in the most natural and comfortable position possible. For patients in bed, raising the head of the bed and supporting the knees can help mimic a seated posture and promote bowel movement.
- Providing privacy and reassurance
- Close curtains, shut doors, and explain what you are doing. Many patients feel embarrassed, so a calm, respectful approach helps reduce anxiety.
- Using appropriate equipment
- Assist with bedpans, commodes, or urinals as needed. Check that the equipment is warm, stable, and positioned correctly to prevent spills or discomfort.
- Supporting hygiene and comfort
- After elimination, assist with perineal hygiene, apply barrier creams if required, and ensure the patient is dry and comfortable.
- Encouraging mobility and fluids (if appropriate)
- Gentle movement, walking, or repositioning can stimulate bowel activity. Adequate hydration also supports normal elimination.
- Observing and reporting changes
- Note stool characteristics (colour, consistency, amount), patient discomfort, or difficulty eliminating. These observations help guide further assessment
When a suppository may be needed
Suppositories are one of several interventions used to support bowel elimination when more conservative measures are not effective. They should always be used with clinical reasoning and patient consent.
Indications
A suppository may be considered when a patient has:
- constipation that has not responded to diet, fluids, or mobility
- difficulty initiating a bowel movement
- reduced mobility or neurological conditions affecting bowel function
- the need to clear the rectum before a procedure
- discomfort or straining that requires relief
When nurses might consider or prepare for one
Nurses may prepare for suppository administration when:
- the patient reports ongoing constipation or discomfort
- bowel sounds are present but elimination is not occurring
- the patient is on medications known to slow bowel motility
- the medical officer has prescribed a suppository
- conservative strategies (positioning, fluids, mobility) have been attempted
Before proceeding, always check the medication chart, confirm the indication, and explain the procedure to the patient.
What the patient might experience
Patients may feel:
- mild pressure or fullness in the rectum
- a sensation of needing to open their bowels
- slight embarrassment or discomfort due to the intimate nature of the procedure
Reassurance, clear communication, and maintaining dignity are essential.
Safety considerations
- Ensure the patient has no contraindications, such as rectal bleeding, recent rectal surgery, or severe haemorrhoids.
- Use gloves and appropriate infection control measures.
- Insert the suppository gently to avoid trauma.
- Allow the patient time and privacy to retain the suppository long enough for it to work.
- Monitor for discomfort, dizziness, or vagal responses (rare but possible).
Escalation or documentation
After administration, document:
- the type and dose of suppository
- the time given
- the patient’s tolerance of the procedure
- the outcome (e.g., bowel movement, stool characteristics, no effect)
- any concerns or abnormal findings
Escalate to the RN or medical officer if:
- the patient experiences severe pain
- there is rectal bleeding
- the suppository is ineffective after a reasonable timeframe
- the patient’s condition deteriorates
Administering a suppository
Suppository insertion is a common method used to support bowel elimination, particularly for patients who are unable to defecate independently or who have constipation that has not responded to more conservative measures. Suppositories are solid medications inserted into the rectum, where they dissolve and release their active ingredients to stimulate bowel movements. Because the medication acts directly on the rectal area, this method can be effective and fast‑acting.
Suppositories may be used for patients who are immobile, recovering from surgery, or experiencing constipation related to reduced mobility, medications, or illness. Proper insertion technique is essential to ensure the suppository is placed correctly, works effectively, and is comfortable for the patient. Understanding this procedure helps nurses support safe, dignified, and effective elimination care.

Safety considerations and documentation
-
-
Safety Considerations
When administering a suppository, patient safety and comfort must always guide your actions. Key considerations include:
- Check for contraindications
- Avoid suppository use in patients with rectal bleeding, recent rectal surgery, severe haemorrhoids, or suspected bowel obstruction unless specifically directed.
- Maintain infection control
- Use gloves, hand hygiene, and appropriate PPE. Ensure the environment is clean and private.
- Protect patient dignity
- Provide clear explanations, maintain privacy, and use draping to minimise exposure.
- Use gentle technique
- Insert the suppository slowly and carefully to avoid discomfort or rectal trauma.
- Monitor the patient
- Observe for pain, dizziness, or signs of a vagal response. Stay nearby if the patient is frail or unsteady.
- Support retention
- Encourage the patient to retain the suppository for the recommended time to allow it to dissolve and act effectively.
Documentation
Accurate documentation ensures continuity of care and supports safe clinical decision‑making. After administering a suppository, record:
- the type, dose, and time of administration
- the indication for use
- the patient’s tolerance of the procedure
- the outcome, including whether a bowel movement occurred and stool characteristics
- any abnormal findings, such as pain, bleeding, or no effect
- any escalation or communication with the RN or medical officer
Clear documentation helps the healthcare team evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention and plan further care.

Understanding urinary elimination
-
-
What is urinary elimination?
Urinary elimination is the process by which the body removes liquid waste (urine) through the urinary system. It helps maintain fluid balance, electrolyte levels, and waste removal.
Anatomy of the urinary system
The urinary system includes:
- Kidneys – filter blood and produce urine
- Ureters – carry urine from kidneys to bladder
- Bladder – stores urine
- Urethra – allows urine to exit the bod
How urine is formed
Urine formation occurs in three main steps:
- Filtration – blood is filtered in the kidneys
- Reabsorption – useful substances (water, glucose, electrolytes) are reabsorbed
- Secretion – additional wastes are added to urine
Normal characteristics of urine
Healthy urine typically has:
- Colour: pale yellow to amber
- Clarity: clear
- Odour: mild
- Output: ~1000–2000 mL/day (varies with intake)

===== FILE: 26 - Common urinary problems and their signs & symptoms.epub =====
Common urinary problems and their signs & symptoms
-
-
Urinary retention – inability to empty the bladder
Description: The bladder fills but does not empty completely, or cannot empty at all.
Signs and symptoms:
- Feeling of fullness or pressure in the lower abdomen
- Difficulty starting urination
- Weak or intermittent urine stream
- Dribbling after voiding
- Discomfort or pain
- Distended bladder on palpation
- Frequent small voids or inability to void at all
Urinary incontinence – loss of bladder control
Description: Involuntary leakage of urine due to weakened pelvic muscles, neurological issues, or other causes.
Signs and symptoms:
- Leakage when coughing, laughing, sneezing, or lifting
- Sudden, strong urge to urinate
- Frequent urination
- Bedwetting or nighttime leakage
- Skin irritation from moisture
Dysuria – pain when urinating
Description: Painful or uncomfortable urination, often caused by infection or inflammation.
Signs and symptoms:
- Burning or stinging sensation during urination
- Pain in the urethra or lower abdomen
- Increased urgency or frequency
- Cloudy or foul‑smelling urine
- Possible blood in urine
Polyuria – excessive urine output
Description: Producing abnormally large volumes of urine (often >3 L/day).
Signs and symptoms:
- Frequent urination
- Large volumes each time
- Increased thirst
- Possible dehydration if fluid intake doesn’t match output
- Fatigue
Oliguria – low urine output
Description: Reduced urine production (typically <400 mL/day in adults).
Signs and symptoms:
- Very small amounts of urine
- Dark, concentrated urine
- Fluid retention (swelling, weight gain)
- Signs of dehydration
- Possible confusion or lethargy if severe
Urinary tract infection (UTI)
Description: Infection of the urinary system, commonly the bladder (cystitis) or urethra.
Signs and symptoms:
- Burning or pain when urinating
- Urgency and frequency
- Lower abdominal or pelvic pain
- Cloudy, foul‑smelling, or bloody urine
- Fever or chills (if infection ascends to kidneys)
- Confusion in older adults

===== FILE: 28 - Assessment of urinary elimination.epub =====
Assessment of urinary elimination
-
-
Assessment of urinary elimination is an important component of nursing care and provides valuable insight into a patient’s fluid balance, kidney function, and overall health. As part of your assessment, you will consider the patient’s usual urinary patterns, including frequency, volume, and any recent changes. You should also observe the colour, clarity, and odour of urine, as well as any associated symptoms such as pain, urgency, or difficulty voiding.
Urinary assessment also contributes to understanding a patient’s fluid balance. Changes in urinary patterns—such as decreased output, increased frequency, or altered appearance—may indicate an imbalance in fluid status or underlying health concerns. For patients experiencing changes from their normal pattern, ongoing monitoring of urine output supports timely clinical decision‑making.
It is important to recognise that some patients may feel uncomfortable discussing urinary concerns. As a nurse, you play a key role in creating a respectful and supportive environment to encourage open communication and accurate assessment.
Key Assessment Components
Subjective history
Review the patient’s medical and surgical history, medications, and fluid intake. Ask about usual urinary patterns, and document the onset, frequency, and severity of any symptoms.
Urine output
Normal adult urine output is approximately 1,500 mL per day, or 0.5–1.5 mL/kg/hour. Output less than 30 mL/hour is considered low and requires prompt clinical attention.
Urine characteristics
Normal urine is typically clear and light in colour. Document any changes in colour (e.g., haematuria), clarity (e.g., cloudy), or odour, as these may indicate underlying conditions.
Symptom assessment
Identify symptoms such as:
- Dysuria (painful urination)
- Nocturia (urinating at night)
- Polyuria (increased urine output)
- Oliguria (reduced output)
- Anuria (absence of urine)
Bladder diary (when appropriate)
A bladder diary may be used to record urinary frequency, volume, and episodes of leakage over a 24-hour period to support assessment and care planning.
