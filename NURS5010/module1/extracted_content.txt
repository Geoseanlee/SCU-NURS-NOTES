===== FILE: 02 - The evolution of nursing in Australia.epub =====
The evolution of nursing in Australia
-
-
Colonial Origins and Early Care
Nursing in colonial Australia began as informal caregiving provided by family members, religious groups, and untrained attendants. Care was often delivered in challenging conditions and focused primarily on meeting basic physical needs, maintaining comfort, and supporting hygiene. Formal nursing education and standards of practice did not yet exist.
The Influence of Florence Nightingale and the Arrival of Trained Nurses
A major turning point occurred in 1868 when Lucy Osburn and five Nightingale-trained nurses arrived in Sydney. They introduced principles of sanitation, organisation, and structured nurse training, helping to establish nursing as a skilled and respected occupation. Their work laid the foundations for modern nursing practice in Australia.
Hospital-Based Nurse Training
During the late nineteenth and much of the twentieth century, nurses were trained through hospital-based apprenticeship programs. Student nurses lived in nurses' homes, worked long hours, and learned through supervised clinical experience. While this model provided extensive practical experience, there was less emphasis on the scientific and theoretical knowledge that underpins nursing today.
Nursing Becomes a Regulated Profession
The introduction of nurse registration across Australian states helped establish nursing as a recognised profession. Registration provided minimum standards for education and practice, helping to protect the public and ensure consistent, safe care. This marked an important shift from nursing as an occupation to nursing as a regulated profession.
The Move to University Education
From the 1970s onwards, nursing education gradually transitioned from hospitals to universities. This shift strengthened nursing's academic foundations and increased the focus on evidence-based practice, critical thinking, research, leadership, and professional decision-making. Nurses became increasingly recognised as autonomous health professionals with distinct knowledge and expertise.
Influential Australian Nursing Leaders
Many Australian nurses have contributed to the growth and recognition of the profession. Leaders such as Vivian Bullwinkel, renowned for her military nursing service and advocacy, and Pat Slater, who played a significant role in advancing nursing education, helped shape the profession and support the transition to tertiary education and professional practice.
Nursing Today
Today, nursing is a regulated, evidence-based profession that combines scientific knowledge, clinical skills, professional judgement, and person-centred care. Understanding how nursing has evolved helps us appreciate the profession's values, responsibilities, and ongoing commitment to improving health outcomes for individuals, families, and communities.

-
-
Nursing today is the result of decades of change in education, regulation, and professional practice. The transition from hospital-based apprenticeship training to university education marked a significant shift, positioning nursing as a profession grounded in research, critical thinking, and evidence-based practice. Contemporary Registered Nurses combine theoretical knowledge, clinical skills, ethical reasoning, and person-centred care to support safe and effective health outcomes.

Nurses work in increasingly complex healthcare environments and require strong communication skills, cultural capability, digital literacy, and sound clinical judgement.
Understanding the history of nursing and the development of nursing theories helps explain how the profession has evolved and why nurses practise the way they do today. These foundations continue to shape the knowledge, values, and professional behaviours expected of Registered Nurses in the 21st century.

Core principles of person centred care
-
-
Core Principles of Person-Centred Nursing
Person-centred nursing places the individual at the centre of care. Rather than focusing only on illness, tasks, or routines, nurses work in partnership with people to understand what matters to them and to support their individual needs, preferences, and goals.
The following principles underpin person-centred nursing practice:
Personhood
Every person is unique and should be recognised as an individual with their own values, beliefs, preferences, experiences, and goals. Personhood reminds nurses that care is not delivered to a diagnosis or medical condition, but to a person with a unique identity and life story.
Therapeutic Relationships
Person-centred care relies on developing respectful and trusting relationships between nurses and the people they care for. Therapeutic relationships are built through empathy, active listening, effective communication, respect, and professional boundaries. These relationships support safe, compassionate, and meaningful care.
Holistic Care
People are more than their physical health needs. Holistic care recognises that wellbeing is influenced by physical, emotional, social, cultural, spiritual, and psychological factors. Nurses consider all aspects of a person's wellbeing when planning and providing care.
Shared Decision-Making
Person-centred care involves working in partnership with people to make decisions about their care. Nurses provide information, support, and guidance while respecting a person's right to be involved in decisions that affect them. Shared decision-making promotes autonomy, trust, and positive healthcare experiences.
Putting the Principles into Practice
When nurses apply these principles, they:
- Listen to and respect individual preferences.
- Communicate openly and honestly.
- Build therapeutic relationships based on trust and respect.
- Consider the whole person, not just the illness or condition.
- Support people to participate in decisions about their care.
- Deliver care that promotes dignity, safety, and independence.
Key Takeaway
Person-centred nursing is more than completing clinical tasks. It is about understanding the person behind the patient, building meaningful partnerships, and providing care that respects individual values, preferences, and goals.

Person-Centredness in Healthcare
-
-
Person-Centredness in Healthcare
Person-centredness is a way of delivering healthcare that places the individual at the centre of all decisions and interactions. It recognises that people are more than their illness, diagnosis, or healthcare needs. Each person brings unique values, preferences, experiences, and goals that should be respected and considered throughout their care.
In contemporary healthcare, person-centred care is achieved through partnership. Healthcare professionals work collaboratively with individuals, families, carers, and other members of the healthcare team to ensure care is tailored to the person's needs and priorities.
Person-Centredness in Interprofessional Practice
Person-centred care is a key component of interprofessional practice. Effective healthcare relies on different professionals working together while ensuring the person's voice remains central to all decisions.
In person-centred interprofessional practice:
- Healthcare professionals work with people rather than making decisions for them.
- Individuals, families, and carers are recognised as partners in care.
- Shared decision-making supports informed choices about treatment and care.
- Different members of the healthcare team contribute their knowledge and expertise to achieve the best possible outcomes.
- Communication and collaboration help ensure safe, coordinated, and respectful care.
Why Person-Centred Care Matters
Person-centred care can improve the healthcare experience for individuals and families by promoting dignity, respect, communication, and partnership. It encourages people to participate in decisions about their health and supports care that aligns with their values, preferences, and goals.
For nurses, person-centred care helps build therapeutic relationships, strengthens trust, and contributes to safe and compassionate practice. It reminds us that effective nursing is not only about clinical skills, but also about understanding the person behind the patient.
Key Takeaway
Person-centred care places the individual at the heart of healthcare. By listening, communicating effectively, and working in partnership with people and healthcare teams, nurses can provide care that is respectful, responsive, and aligned with what matters most to the person.

The 6Cs of Person‑Centred Nursing
-
-
The 6Cs of Care
The 6Cs of Care provide a simple and memorable framework that supports person-centred nursing practice. They describe the values and behaviours that underpin compassionate, safe, and effective care and guide how nurses communicate, make decisions, and build therapeutic relationships with the people they care for.
The 6Cs Explained
Care
Providing safe, effective, and evidence-based care that responds to the person's individual needs, preferences, and goals.
Compassion
Responding with empathy, kindness, respect, and genuine concern for the person's wellbeing and experiences.
Competence
Maintaining the knowledge, skills, and professional judgement required to provide safe, high-quality care. Competence involves lifelong learning and ongoing professional development.
Communication
Listening actively, sharing information clearly, and building trust through respectful and effective interactions with people, families, and colleagues.
Courage
Speaking up when concerns arise, advocating for the person, and acting ethically even when situations are difficult or uncomfortable.
Commitment
Demonstrating dedication to professional standards, continuous improvement, and the consistent delivery of person-centred care.

Why Are the 6Cs Important?
The 6Cs help nurses translate the principles of person-centred care into everyday practice. Together, they support respectful partnerships, effective communication, safe care, and positive healthcare experiences.
By demonstrating care, compassion, competence, communication, courage, and commitment, nurses can build therapeutic relationships, advocate for the people in their care, and help create healthcare environments that promote dignity, safety, and wellbeing.
Key Takeaway
The 6Cs are not separate skills or tasks. They are professional values that influence every interaction a nurse has with patients, families, carers, and colleagues. When applied consistently, they help ensure care remains person-centred, compassionate, and safe.

Why self-care is a safety issue
-
-
Why Self-Care is a Safety Issue
The link between self-care and patient safety may not be immediately obvious, but it is an important aspect of professional nursing practice. Nurses who are physically exhausted, emotionally overwhelmed, or experiencing high levels of stress may be more likely to experience:
- Reduced concentration
- Impaired decision-making
- Slower reaction times
- Increased risk of errors
- Difficulty communicating effectively
A nurse's wellbeing directly influences the quality and safety of care provided to patients, families, colleagues, and visitors. By prioritising self-care, nurses strengthen their capacity to provide safe, effective, and person-centred care.
Evidence From Current Research
Research has consistently demonstrated a relationship between healthcare worker wellbeing and patient outcomes.
- Fendel and Grant (2017) emphasise that nurses' health and wellbeing can directly influence the quality and safety of patient care.
- Hofmeyer et al. (2020) argue that self-care knowledge and practices are essential for maintaining resilience, supporting wellbeing, and sustaining safe professional practice, particularly during periods of increased workplace stress.
Key message: Looking after yourself is an important part of looking after others.

Aspects of self-care
-
-
Aspects of Self-Care
Self-care is more than looking after your physical health. As a nurse, your wellbeing is influenced by many aspects of your life, including your physical, emotional, psychological, spiritual, relational, and professional health. Supporting these different areas helps build resilience, maintain capability for practice, and reduce the risk of stress and burnout.
Because self-care is personal, the strategies that work for one person may not work for another. Developing an awareness of your own needs is an important step in building sustainable self-care practices throughout your nursing career.
Professional Self-Care
Professional self-care involves strategies that support your confidence, competence, and ability to practise safely.
Examples include:
- Seeking supervision, mentorship, or guidance from experienced colleagues
- Participating in peer support groups
- Maintaining healthy boundaries between work, study, and personal life
- Engaging with evidence-based resources and professional literature
- Attending professional development activities
- Limiting unnecessary overtime and commitments where possible
Professional self-care helps nurses maintain competence, confidence, and professional wellbeing.
Physical Self-Care
Physical wellbeing provides the foundation for safe and effective nursing practice.
Examples include:
- Maintaining regular sleep routines
- Eating balanced and nourishing meals
- Staying hydrated throughout the day
- Taking scheduled breaks
- Engaging in regular physical activity
- Using sick leave when genuinely unwell
Looking after your physical health supports concentration, energy levels, and resilience.
Psychological Self-Care
Psychological self-care helps maintain mental wellbeing and supports clear thinking.
Examples include:
- Keeping a reflective journal
- Seeking mentorship or supervision
- Participating in enjoyable hobbies and interests
- Taking breaks from study or work-related responsibilities
- Limiting work communications during personal time
- Spending time with supportive people
These strategies can help reduce stress and maintain perspective during challenging periods.
Emotional Self-Care
Emotional self-care involves recognising, expressing, and managing emotions in healthy ways.
Examples include:
- Maintaining supportive friendships
- Participating in social or recreational activities
- Celebrating personal achievements and successes
- Discussing challenges with trusted friends, family members, or mentors
- Making time for activities you enjoy
Emotional self-care strengthens self-awareness, empathy, and emotional regulation.
Spiritual Self-Care
Spiritual self-care focuses on meaning, purpose, and personal values.
Examples include:
- Meditation or mindfulness practices
- Spending time in nature
- Religious or spiritual practices
- Yoga or relaxation activities
- Personal reflection
Spiritual wellbeing can support resilience and a sense of balance during periods of stress.
Relationship Self-Care
Healthy relationships provide connection, support, and a sense of belonging.
Examples include:
- Prioritising important relationships
- Maintaining contact with family and friends
- Attending important social and family events
- Creating time for meaningful interactions
- Establishing healthy boundaries in relationships
Strong relationships can help protect wellbeing and provide support during challenging times.
Why These Aspects Matter
Supporting your wellbeing across multiple areas of life helps you:
- Think clearly and make sound decisions
- Communicate effectively
- Maintain emotional balance
- Build therapeutic relationships
- Respond to challenges with resilience
- Practise safely and professionally
- Sustain your wellbeing throughout your nursing career
Key Takeaway
Self-care is not a single activity. It involves looking after multiple aspects of your wellbeing so that you can maintain your health, resilience, and capability for practice while providing safe, compassionate, and person-centred care.

Common Barriers to Self-Care
Time Pressure
Heavy workloads, long shifts, study commitments, and competing priorities can leave nurses feeling they have little time for self-care. As a result, basic needs such as hydration, nutrition, rest, and exercise may be overlooked.
Guilt
Many nurses feel guilty prioritising their own needs, believing they should always be available to support others. This can make self-care feel selfish, even though maintaining wellbeing is an important part of safe professional practice.
Not Recognising Personal Needs
In busy healthcare environments, it can be easy to ignore signs of fatigue, stress, hunger, thirst, or emotional strain. Over time, these behaviours may become normalised, making it harder to recognise when support or rest is needed.
Unrealistic Expectations
Some nurses place high expectations on themselves and may feel they need to cope independently or always perform at their best. These expectations can discourage help-seeking and contribute to stress and burnout.
Lack of Work-Life Balance
Balancing study, work, family, and personal responsibilities can be challenging. When personal time is consistently sacrificed, wellbeing and resilience can be affected.
Why Understanding Barriers Matters
Identifying barriers to self-care helps nurses recognise risks before they affect wellbeing and professional practice. When self-care is consistently delayed or neglected, it can impact concentration, decision-making, communication, emotional wellbeing, and ultimately the quality and safety of patient care.
Key Takeaway
Self-care is not simply about knowing what to do. It involves recognising and addressing the barriers that can prevent you from looking after your wellbeing. Developing this awareness is an important step towards becoming a safe, resilient, and sustainable nursing professional.

-

What Happens When Self‑Care Is Neglected
Self-care is essential for maintaining wellbeing, resilience, and capability for practice. When self-care is consistently neglected, physical, emotional, and psychological resources become depleted. Over time, this can affect both personal wellbeing and the ability to provide safe, high-quality care.
Neglecting self-care may contribute to:
- Reduced concentration
- Impaired decision-making
- Slower reaction times
- Increased risk of clinical errors
- Needle-stick or manual handling injuries
- Emotional exhaustion and burnout
- Depression or anxiety
- Chronic fatigue
- Poor sleep
- Increased absenteeism
Nursing is already a demanding profession. Without effective self-care, these risks can increase, affecting not only the nurse but also patient safety, team functioning, and quality of care.
Why Do We Ignore Our Own Needs?
As children, we often respond naturally to our body's signals. As adults, particularly in caring professions, we may learn to override these signals in order to meet competing demands and responsibilities.
Common thoughts might include:
- "I'll drink water later."
- "I don't have time for a break."
- "I'll rest when my shift is finished."
- "Other people need me more."
- "I can push through a little longer."
Although these decisions may seem small, they can accumulate over time and contribute to fatigue, stress, and reduced wellbeing.

Introduction to Emotional Intelligence [EI]
Emotional Intelligence (EI) refers to the ability to recognise, understand, and manage your own emotions while also recognising and responding effectively to the emotions of others. First described by Salovey and Mayer (1990), emotional intelligence helps people use emotional awareness to guide their thinking, behaviour, and interactions.
For nurses, emotional intelligence is closely linked to:
- Effective communication
- Empathy and therapeutic relationships
- Stress management and resilience
- Teamwork and conflict resolution
- Safe, person-centred decision-making
Research suggests that higher levels of emotional intelligence in nursing students are associated with improved clinical performance and professional capabilities. Emotional intelligence supports important nursing skills such as communication, self-awareness, accountability, empathy, and stress management, all of which contribute to safe and effective patient care.
Why Emotional Intelligence Matters in Nursing
Nurses frequently work in emotionally demanding environments where patients, families, and colleagues may be experiencing stress, uncertainty, or vulnerability. Emotional intelligence helps nurses:
- Recognise and understand their own emotional responses.
- Remain calm and professional during challenging situations.
- Identify and respond appropriately to the emotions of others.
- Communicate with empathy and respect.
- Make safe and considered decisions under pressure.
By developing emotional intelligence, nurses are better equipped to build therapeutic relationships, collaborate effectively within healthcare teams, and provide compassionate, person-centred care.

Emotional intelligence can be understood through four key domains that work together to support professional nursing practice.

Description

Self‑Regulation

Social Awareness (Empathy)
Understanding the emotions, needs, and perspectives of others. Empathy is essential for building trust, supporting patients, and working effectively in teams.
Relationship Management
Using awareness of your own emotions and others’ emotions to communicate clearly, manage conflict, and build positive professional relationships.
These four domains work together to support safe, compassionate, and person‑centred nursing care.
Key Takeaway
Emotional intelligence is a professional capability that can be developed over time. By strengthening self-awareness, self-regulation, social awareness, and relationship management, nurses can communicate more effectively, build stronger therapeutic relationships, and provide safe, compassionate, and person-centred care.

Reflective Practice and Well-being
-
-
What Is Reflective Practice?
Reflective practice involves examining your experiences, including your thoughts, emotions, actions, and outcomes, to gain insight and improve future practice. It is a deliberate and structured process that supports learning, self-awareness, critical thinking, and professional growth.
Rather than simply asking "What happened?", reflective practice encourages nurses to consider why it happened, what was learned, and how future practice could be improved.
A Brief History of Reflective Practice
Reflection has been explored by several influential educators and scholars.
- John Dewey (1933) described reflection as a process of active, thoughtful learning.
- Donald Schön (1983) introduced the concepts of:
- Reflection-in-action: thinking while doing.
- Reflection-on-action: thinking after an event has occurred.
- Edwards later expanded these ideas by adding:
- Reflection-before-action
- Reflection-beyond-action
Together, these approaches recognise that reflection can occur before, during, and after practice, helping nurses continually learn and improve.
Reflective Practice in Nursing
Reflective practice has long been part of nursing. Florence Nightingale used reflective writing to support learning, self-awareness, and improvements in patient care. Student nurses were encouraged to keep journals and discuss their experiences with mentors, an early form of structured reflection and professional support.
Today, reflective practice remains an important component of nursing because it helps nurses:
- Develop clinical competence
- Strengthen critical thinking
- Support lifelong learning
- Improve patient outcomes
- Enhance professional accountability
- Build confidence and professional identity
Effective reflection requires honesty, curiosity, openness to feedback, and a willingness to learn from experience.
Reflective Practice and Wellbeing
Reflective practice is closely linked to self-care and emotional wellbeing. Taking time to reflect can help nurses better understand their emotional responses and develop healthier ways of coping with challenging situations.
Reflection can help you:
- Recognise early signs of stress or fatigue
- Identify emotional triggers
- Challenge unhelpful assumptions or beliefs
- Respond more effectively in unfamiliar situations
- Prevent the accumulation of unresolved emotions
- Build self-awareness and emotional resilience
By supporting emotional intelligence and self-awareness, reflective practice helps nurses maintain wellbeing in demanding clinical environments.
Contemporary Reflective Models
Reflective practice continues to evolve in response to contemporary healthcare environments and the needs of diverse learners.
One example is the SPROUT model, developed by Australian nurse educators to support structured reflection in both clinical and academic settings. SPROUT encourages learners to consider:
- Situation
- Person
- Reasoning
- Outcome
- Understanding
- Transfer
Models such as SPROUT, alongside established frameworks like Gibbs' Reflective Cycle, provide practical tools that help nurses learn from experience and apply insights to future practice.
Why Reflective Practice Matters
Reflective practice helps nurses:
- Improve clinical judgement and decision-making
- Enhance communication and teamwork
- Recognise personal biases and emotional triggers
- Strengthen person-centred care
- Develop resilience and prevent burnout
- Learn from both successful and challenging experiences
Importantly, reflection is not only used when things go wrong. Reflecting on positive experiences, such as effective teamwork, compassionate care, successful communication, or clinical achievements, can help build confidence, reinforce good practice, and support professional growth.
Key Takeaway
Reflective practice is a lifelong professional skill that supports learning, wellbeing, and safe nursing care. By regularly reflecting on experiences, nurses can develop greater self-awareness, strengthen clinical reasoning, and continually improve the care they provide.

What is Ethics?
-
-
What Is Ethics?
Ethics is the study of morality, including the values, principles, and beliefs that guide decisions about what is right and wrong. In nursing, ethics provides a framework for making decisions in situations that may involve vulnerability, risk, cultural diversity, competing interests, or uncertainty.
Ethical nursing practice involves more than knowing what is right. It also requires nurses to recognise ethical issues, make informed decisions, and act in ways that uphold professional values and patient wellbeing.
Four key capacities support ethical practice:
- Moral sensitivity - recognising when an ethical issue exists.
- Moral judgement - determining the most appropriate course of action.
- Moral motivation - prioritising ethical values and professional responsibilities.
- Moral courage - acting ethically, even when doing so is difficult.
Together, these capacities help nurses respect the values, rights, and choices of others while providing safe, compassionate, and person-centred care.

Principles of nursing ethics
-
-
Ethical principles provide a practical framework for decision-making in everyday nursing practice. They help nurses balance competing needs, respect patient rights, and provide safe, person-centred care.
Autonomy
Respecting a person's right to make informed decisions about their own healthcare.
Example: Supporting a competent patient's decision to accept or refuse treatment, even when you may not agree with their choice.
Beneficence
Acting in ways that promote the wellbeing of others and contribute positively to their health and care.
Example: Providing care that supports comfort, recovery, and quality of life.
Non-Maleficence
Avoiding actions that may cause harm and taking steps to minimise risk.
Example: Following safe medication administration procedures to reduce the risk of errors.
Justice
Providing fair, equitable, and unbiased care to all individuals.
Example: Ensuring patients receive care according to their needs, regardless of age, culture, gender, religion, or socioeconomic status.
These principles are embedded within the NMBA Code of Conduct and underpin safe, ethical, and person-centred nursing practice.

Law in nursing practice
-
-
Law provides the formal boundaries within which Registered Nurses practise. It defines the responsibilities, rights, and accountabilities of nurses and establishes standards that help protect the public. In healthcare, law guides decision-making in areas such as informed consent, negligence, duty of care, confidentiality, documentation, and patient safety.
For nurses, understanding the legal framework is essential. It helps ensure care is delivered safely, ethically, and in accordance with professional standards. Legal knowledge also supports nurses to recognise risks, advocate for patients, and practise confidently in complex healthcare environments.
Civil and Criminal Law
Law establishes expectations for how individuals and organisations should behave. In healthcare, these laws help protect patients and ensure care is delivered safely, responsibly, and professionally.
Criminal Law
Criminal law applies when an individual's actions breach a law designed to protect the wider community. Criminal offences generally involve both a prohibited act and criminal intent.
Penalties may include fines, community service, or imprisonment.
Examples relevant to healthcare may include:
- Assault
- Theft
- Fraud
- Deliberate falsification of records
- Serious breaches of professional conduct
Civil Law
Civil law deals with disputes between individuals or organisations when a person's rights have been affected or harmed. Civil cases commonly focus on compensation rather than punishment.
In healthcare, civil law often relates to:
- Negligence
- Breaches of duty of care
- Failure to obtain valid consent
- Breaches of confidentiality
- Inadequate documentation
Civil law aims to restore or compensate the person who has experienced harm.

Health Law
-
-
Health Law
Health law refers to the legal frameworks that govern healthcare delivery and the regulation of health professionals. It shapes how nurses practise, how healthcare decisions are made, and how patient rights are protected. Health law covers areas such as consent, privacy, confidentiality, documentation, duty of care, negligence, public health, mental health legislation, and emerging technologies in healthcare.
For nurses, health law:
- Provides a legal framework for safe and lawful practice.
- Clarifies professional responsibilities and scope of practice.
- Establishes boundaries for decision-making and accountability.
- Protects the rights and safety of patients.
- Supports public confidence in the nursing profession.
Health practitioner conduct is regulated through state and territory legislation, together with national frameworks administered by the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) and the Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia (NMBA). These frameworks work together to support safe, ethical, and accountable nursing practice.
Duty of Care and Negligence
Registered Nurses have a legal duty of care to provide safe and appropriate care that meets professional standards.
A breach of duty of care may occur when a nurse fails to act in a way that a reasonable nurse would under similar circumstances. If this breach results in harm, it may be considered negligence.
Examples may include:
- Failing to monitor a deteriorating patient.
- Administering incorrect medication.
- Inadequate documentation.
- Failing to communicate important clinical information.
Understanding duty of care helps nurses recognise their responsibilities and reduce the risk of harm to patients.
Informed Consent
Informed consent is a fundamental legal and ethical requirement in healthcare. It is the process by which a person voluntarily agrees to a proposed treatment or intervention after receiving information that is accurate, relevant, and understandable.
Consent protects patient autonomy and ensures individuals are active participants in decisions about their care.
For consent to be valid, it must be:
Voluntary
Given freely, without coercion, pressure, or undue influence.
Informed
Based on clear information about the nature, benefits, risks, and alternatives of the proposed intervention.
Given by a Person with Capacity
The individual must be able to understand, retain, evaluate the information provided, and communicate a decision.
Consent may be implied, verbal, or written depending on the situation and level of risk involved.
The Nurse's Role in Supporting Consent
Nurses support informed consent by:
- Communicating information clearly and respectfully.
- Checking patient understanding.
- Encouraging questions.
- Advocating for the patient's right to make decisions.
- Recognising when consent may not be valid.
- Seeking assistance when capacity is uncertain.
Informed consent is not a one-off event but an ongoing process that supports safe, person-centred, and legally compliant care.
Key Takeaway
Health law provides the legal framework for nursing practice. Understanding concepts such as duty of care, negligence, consent, and professional accountability helps nurses protect patient rights, meet professional standards, and provide safe, ethical, and person-centred care.

Capacity to consent
-
-
When seeking consent, healthcare professionals must consider whether a person has the legal capacity to make the specific decision being discussed. Capacity relates to a person's ability to understand information, make a decision, and communicate that decision.
To have capacity, a person must be able to:
- Understand the information relevant to the decision.
- Retain that information long enough to use it.
- Weigh the information to reach a reasoned decision.
- Communicate their decision in some way, such as through speech, writing, gestures, or assistive technology.
Figure: The four elements of decision-making capacity.
Capacity is decision-specific and can change over time. A person may have capacity to make some decisions but not others. Factors such as illness, pain, fatigue, medication, stress, or confusion may temporarily affect a person's ability to make decisions.
Nurses play an important role in supporting capacity by communicating clearly, checking understanding, and creating an environment that enables people to participate in decisions about their care. When capacity is uncertain, nurses should seek guidance from senior clinicians and follow relevant legislation, organisational policies, and interprofessional processes.
Remember
A person's ability to demonstrate capacity can sometimes be affected by factors other than a lack of capacity. For example:
- Language barriers
- Hearing or vision impairment
- Communication difficulties
- Anxiety or distress
- Cultural differences
- Environmental distractions
In these situations, nurses should take steps to reduce barriers and support effective communication before concluding that a person lacks capacity.

Professionalisation
Professionalisation is the process through which an occupation develops into a recognised profession with its own body of knowledge, standards of practice, ethical obligations, and regulatory requirements. Nursing has undergone significant professionalisation over time, evolving from informal caregiving roles to a regulated, university-educated profession grounded in evidence-based practice.
As part of this process, nurses develop a professional identity: an evolving understanding of themselves as members of the nursing profession. Professional identity is shaped by personal values, professional expectations, knowledge, skills, experiences, and interactions with others.
Your professional identity will continue to develop throughout your studies and into practice. It is influenced by:
- Clinical and academic experiences
- Feedback from peers, educators, and supervisors
- Exposure to professional standards, values, and expectations
- The role models you encounter in practice
- Reflection on your experiences and learning
Professional Identity in Interprofessional Practice
In contemporary healthcare, nurses work closely with professionals from many disciplines. Developing a strong professional identity helps nurses contribute confidently to interprofessional teams while maintaining a clear understanding of their own role, responsibilities, and scope of practice.
Working effectively within interprofessional teams requires:
- Respect for the expertise of other professions
- Effective communication and collaboration
- Openness to different perspectives
- Confidence in your own professional role and contribution
The development of these skills begins during your nursing education and continues throughout your professional career.

Professionalisation has shaped nursing into a recognised, regulated, and evidence-based profession. As nursing students, you begin this journey by developing your own professional identity and learning how to contribute effectively within healthcare teams.

Professional Responsibilities and Accountability
Professional nursing practice requires more than clinical knowledge and technical skills. Nurses are accountable for their actions, decisions, and behaviours, and must demonstrate professionalism in every aspect of care. This includes communicating effectively, maintaining accurate documentation, engaging in ongoing learning, and practising within professional and legal standards.
Key Professional Skills
Professionalism is strengthened through the development of core skills that support safe, effective, and person-centred care. These skills help nurses communicate clearly, make sound decisions, and contribute positively to healthcare teams.
Key professional skills include:
- Conflict resolution – supporting patients and colleagues to navigate disagreements respectfully and constructively.
- Critical thinking – making informed decisions based on evidence, clinical reasoning, and reflection.
- Attention to detail – ensuring accuracy in assessment, care planning, documentation, and communication.
- Adaptability – responding effectively to changing clinical situations, environments, and priorities.
- Leadership – taking initiative, supporting others, and contributing to shared goals.
- Commitment to growth – engaging in self-reflection, feedback, and lifelong learning.
These skills contribute directly to patient safety, teamwork, and quality care.
Professional Accountability
Accountability is a fundamental component of professional nursing practice. Nurses are responsible for the decisions they make, the care they provide, and the actions they take. Professional accountability extends across multiple levels and helps ensure care is delivered safely, ethically, and in accordance with professional standards.
Layers of Accountability
Accountability in nursing means being answerable for your actions, decisions, and professional conduct. It is a core component of professionalism and ensures that nurses practise safely, ethically, and within recognised standards. Accountability operates across several layers, each reinforcing the responsibility nurses have to patients, colleagues, the profession, and the public.
1. Personal Accountability
Nurses are responsible for maintaining their own competence, fitness to practise, and professional behaviour. This includes reflective practice, seeking support when needed, and recognising personal limitations.
2. Professional Accountability
Nurses must adhere to the NMBA Registered Nurse Standards for Practice, the Code of Conduct, and ethical principles. These frameworks outline the expectations for safe, respectful, and evidence‑based care.
3. Organisational Accountability
Nurses are accountable to their employer and must follow workplace policies, procedures, and reporting requirements. This includes documentation standards, incident reporting, and safe use of equipment and resources.
4. Legal Accountability
Nurses must comply with relevant legislation, including privacy laws, mandatory reporting requirements, and the Health Practitioner Regulation National Law. Legal accountability ensures public protection and reinforces the consequences of unsafe or unethical practice.
5. Interprofessional Accountability
In team‑based care, nurses are accountable for communicating clearly, collaborating effectively, and contributing to shared decision‑making. Accountability supports safe handover, coordinated care, and positive team culture.
Documentation and Professional Responsibility
Accurate, timely documentation is a key professional responsibility. Documentation supports continuity of care, communicates important information among healthcare professionals, and provides evidence of assessments, decisions, and nursing interventions.
Effective documentation should be:
- Accurate
- Clear
- Objective
- Timely
- Complete
- Legible and professional
Poor or incomplete documentation can compromise patient safety and may result in professional, ethical, or legal consequences.

- Standard 1 – Thinks critically and analyses nursing practice
- Standard 6 – Provides safe, appropriate and responsive quality nursing practice
These standards require nurses to maintain comprehensive, accurate, and timely records as part of their professional responsibility and accountability.

The National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards outline the level of care consumers can expect from Australian healthcare organisations. The standards aim to:
- Protect the public from harm.
- Improve the quality and safety of healthcare.
- Promote consistent expectations across health services.
Professionalism aligns closely with the NSQHS Standards because both emphasise:
- Safety
- Accountability
- Communication
- Teamwork
- Person-centred care
By demonstrating professional behaviours and maintaining accountability, nurses contribute to a positive safety culture and high-quality patient care.

Professional responsibility begins from the first day of nursing study. Although nursing students are not yet Registered Nurses, they are expected to demonstrate professional behaviours in academic, simulation, laboratory, and clinical environments.
As a nursing student, professional responsibilities include:
- Attending classes, laboratories, and placements prepared and on time.
- Maintaining patient confidentiality and privacy.
- Communicating respectfully with patients, peers, educators, and healthcare staff.
- Following workplace and university policies and procedures.
- Practising within the limits of your knowledge, skills, and experience.
- Seeking guidance and supervision when unsure.
- Accepting feedback and using it to improve practice.
- Taking responsibility for your learning and professional development.
- Demonstrating honesty, integrity, and accountability in all aspects of study and practice.
Developing these professional behaviours early helps build a strong professional identity and prepares you for the responsibilities of Registered Nurse practice.
Key Takeaway
Professionalism is demonstrated through the skills, behaviours, and responsibilities that guide nursing practice every day. Effective communication, critical thinking, accountability, and accurate documentation are essential components of safe, ethical, and person-centred care.

The Australian Regulatory Framework
Australia's nursing profession is governed by a national regulatory system designed to protect the public and ensure nurses provide safe, competent, and ethical care. This framework brings together legislation, regulatory bodies, professional standards, and codes that guide nursing practice across all healthcare settings.
AHPRA – Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency
AHPRA administers the national registration and accreditation scheme for health practitioners in Australia. Working in partnership with the National Boards, AHPRA:
- Processes practitioner registration and renewal
- Maintains the public register of health practitioners
- Investigates notifications and concerns about practitioner conduct, performance, or health
- Supports the regulation of registered health professions
- Helps protect the public through regulatory oversight
For Registered Nurses, AHPRA works closely with the NMBA to ensure practitioners meet required professional standards.
NMBA – Nursing and Midwifery Board of Australia
The NMBA regulates nurses and midwives in Australia. Its responsibilities include:
- Setting the Registered Nurse Standards for Practice
- Publishing the Code of Conduct and other professional guidelines
- Developing decision-making frameworks and professional expectations
- Approving accreditation standards for nursing education programs
- Making decisions about professional conduct and practice issues
The NMBA defines what safe, competent, and professional nursing practice looks like.
The National Law
The Health Practitioner Regulation National Law provides the legal foundation for the regulation of health practitioners in Australia. It outlines:
- Registration requirements
- Protected professional titles (for example, Registered Nurse)
- Mandatory reporting obligations
- Professional conduct and disciplinary processes
- The powers and functions of AHPRA and the National Boards
How the Regulatory Framework Works Together
The Australian regulatory framework can be viewed as three interconnected components:
- National Law provides the legal authority.
- AHPRA administers and supports the regulatory system.
- NMBA establishes the standards and expectations for nursing practice.
Together, these components help protect the public, maintain professional standards, and support safe, accountable nursing practice.
Key Takeaway
The Australian regulatory framework exists to protect the public and support safe nursing practice. Understanding the roles of AHPRA, the NMBA, and the National Law helps nurses recognise their professional responsibilities and practise within the expectations of the profession.

Interprofessional Education (IPE) prepares students to work effectively in healthcare teams by learning with, from, and about one another. Contemporary healthcare relies on collaboration between multiple professions, making teamwork and communication essential skills for safe, coordinated, and person-centred care.
Historically, many health professionals were educated separately within their own disciplines. While this approach developed profession-specific knowledge and skills, it provided limited opportunities to learn how different healthcare professionals work together in practice. - Understand the roles and responsibilities of different healthcare professions.
- Communicate respectfully and effectively across disciplines.
- Work collaboratively to support safe, coordinated care.
- Develop confidence in team-based decision-making.
- Improve patient experiences and health outcomes.
Research has shown that interprofessional education can strengthen communication, teamwork, collaborative practice, and attitudes towards other professions, all of which contribute to safer and more effective healthcare (Fox et al., 2018; Spaulding et al., 2021).

As a nursing student, you will study and work alongside students and practitioners from a range of healthcare professions. Chain of infection
-
-
The Chain of Infection
Infections follow a predictable pattern known as the Chain of Infection. Understanding how infections develop and spread helps nurses identify opportunities to prevent transmission and protect patients, colleagues, visitors, and themselves.
The Chain of Infection consists of six links:
- Infectious Agent (Microorganism)
- The pathogen that causes disease, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
- Reservoir
- The place where the microorganism lives and multiplies, such as people, animals, equipment, surfaces, food, or water.
- Portal of Exit
- The way the microorganism leaves the reservoir, such as through coughing, sneezing, blood, wound drainage, or other body fluids.
- Mode of Transmission
- How the microorganism spreads from one place or person to another. Transmission may occur through contact, droplets, airborne particles, contaminated equipment, food, water, or vectors.
- Portal of Entry
- The route through which the microorganism enters a new host, such as broken skin, mucous membranes, the respiratory tract, or invasive devices.
- Susceptible Host
- A person who is vulnerable to infection due to factors such as age, illness, immune status, chronic disease, or invasive procedures.
Breaking the Chain
If any one of these links is broken, the infection cannot spread. This is why infection prevention and control measures are so important.
Examples include:
- Hand hygiene
- Personal protective equipment (PPE)
- Environmental cleaning
- Respiratory hygiene
- Safe sharps handling
- Appropriate use of aseptic technique
- Cleaning and disinfection of equipment
These measures interrupt the chain of infection and help prevent healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).
Why This Matters for Nurses
Nurses play a key role in breaking the chain of infection. Every time a nurse performs hand hygiene, uses PPE correctly, cleans equipment, or follows standard precautions, they are helping to reduce the spread of infection and promote patient safety.

Understanding Microorganisms and infection
-

Understanding Microorganisms and Infection
Before nurses can prevent infection, they must understand what microorganisms are and how they affect the body.
Microorganisms are tiny living organisms found everywhere — on our skin, in the environment, and on the surfaces we touch. Many are harmless or even beneficial, but others can cause disease when they enter the body or reach areas where they do not belong.
In nursing, we extend beyond basic school‑level microbiology to ask deeper, practice‑focused questions:
- What exactly are microorganisms?
- How do they cause infection?
- What is the difference between colonisation and infection?
- How do these processes influence patient safety?
Understanding these fundamentals helps nurses recognise risk, anticipate problems, and apply the right precautions to protect patients and colleagues.
Healthcare‑Associated Infections (HAIs)
Healthcare‑associated infections occur when a patient acquires an infection during the course of receiving healthcare, rather than from their original condition.
As the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care notes,
“Healthcare‑associated infections are one of the most common preventable complications of hospitalised patients… They cause unnecessary pain and suffering, prolong hospital stays, increase costs, and greatly increase morbidity and mortality.”
HAIs can occur in any healthcare setting — hospitals, aged care, outpatient clinics, and even home care. They often arise when microorganisms take advantage of:
- invasive devices (catheters, IV lines, ventilators)
- surgical wounds or breaks in the skin
- contaminated surfaces or equipment
- lapses in hand hygiene or PPE use
- environmental contamination (e.g., fungal spores)
Common HAIs include:
- Ventilator‑associated pneumonia (VAP)
- Central‑line‑associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI)
- Catheter‑associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI)
- Surgical site infections (SSI)
- Clostridioides difficile infections
- MRSA bacteraemia
These infections are strongly influenced by systems and environments — cleaning processes, staffing levels, equipment maintenance, and workflow design all contribute to risk. Nurses must therefore understand both the microbiology and the context in which care is delivered.

Key Components of Standard Precautions
-
-
Standard Precautions include the following core practices:
Hand Hygiene — The single most effective action to prevent the spread of microorganisms. Performed before and after every patient interaction, after contact with bodily fluids, and after touching the environment. (explored in more detail int he next section)
Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) — Items such as gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection that reduce exposure to infectious material. PPE is selected based on the task and the risk of contamination.
Safe Disposal of Sharps — Using approved sharps containers and never recapping needles prevents injuries and reduces the risk of blood‑borne virus transmission.
Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette — Covering coughs, using tissues, performing hand hygiene after contact with respiratory secretions, and maintaining distance when needed to reduce droplet spread.
Environmental Cleaning — Regular cleaning of surfaces, equipment, and patient surroundings removes microorganisms and prevents them from becoming reservoirs for infection.
Waste and Linen Management — Correct handling, segregation, and disposal of contaminated materials prevents cross‑contamination and protects staff and patients.
These practices are simple, routine, and highly effective — but only when performed consistently.
Standard vs Transmission‑Based Precautions
Standard Precautions are used for all patients, all the time.
Transmission‑Based precautions are used in addition to Standard Precautions when a patient is known or suspected to have an infection spread by:
Contact Precautions — Used when infections spread through direct touch (skin‑to‑skin) or indirect contact with contaminated surfaces or equipment. Requires gloves, gowns, dedicated equipment, and enhanced environmental cleaning.
Droplet Precautions — Used for infections spread through respiratory droplets produced by coughing, sneezing, or talking. Requires a surgical mask, spatial separation, and attention to respiratory hygiene. Droplets generally travel short distances (about 1 metre).
Airborne Precautions — Used for infections spread through tiny particles that remain suspended in the air and travel longer distances. Requires a P2/N95 respirator, negative‑pressure rooms (where available), and strict adherence to fit‑checking.
These additional precautions target specific modes of transmission and are applied based on clinical assessment and organisational policy.

===== FILE: 92 - Sharps Safety.epub =====
Sharps Safety
-
-
Sharps injuries pose a serious risk of exposure to blood‑borne viruses such as HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C. Incorrect handling of sharps can lead to severe injuries, and many devices used in healthcare settings may be contaminated with blood or body fluids. Safe sharps practices protect nurses, patients, and all healthcare workers.
Most sharps injuries occur during or immediately after use — often due to distraction, poor workflow, or incorrect disposal. Understanding and applying safe sharps principles is essential for clinical placement, OSCEs, and everyday nursing practice.
Safe Sharps Practices
Never recap needles
Recapping is one of the most common causes of sharps injuries. If a device has a safety mechanism, activate it immediately after use.
Dispose of sharps immediately after use
Place used sharps directly into an approved sharps container at the point of care. Containers should be within arm’s reach and never overfilled.
Use approved sharps containers only
Sharps must never be placed in general waste, linen bags, pockets, or on the bedside table. Approved containers reduce the risk of accidental injury.
Keep hands behind the sharp at all times
Maintain safe hand positioning during procedures to avoid accidental punctures. Avoid passing sharps between people whenever possible.
Report all sharps injuries immediately
If an injury occurs, follow organisational policy:
- perform first aid
- report the incident
- complete required documentation
- attend recommended follow‑up testing
- Prompt reporting ensures timely assessment and protects both staff and patients.

Policies require staff to follow the 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene. In practice, this means cleaning your hands before touching a patient, before a procedure, and after any exposure risk — every single time.
PPE selection and use
Policies outline when PPE is required and how it must be applied and removed. In practice, this means choosing the correct PPE for the task, donning it before entering the patient zone, and doffing it safely to avoid contamination.
Sharps management
Policies specify safe handling and disposal of sharps. In practice, this means never recapping needles, disposing of sharps immediately, and using approved containers within arm’s reach.
Environmental cleaning
Policies define cleaning schedules and responsibilities. In practice, this means ensuring equipment is wiped between patients and reporting any spills or contamination promptly.
Waste and linen handling
Policies outline correct segregation and disposal. In practice, this means placing contaminated linen in designated bags and disposing of clinical waste in the correct bins.
Staff training and competency
Policies require regular IPC training. In practice, this means completing mandatory hand hygiene modules, PPE updates, and following local procedures during placement.
Why This Matters for You
As a student and future nurse, following organisational IPC policies is part of your professional responsibility. These policies ensure:
- safe care for patients
- protection for staff
- compliance with legal and regulatory requirements
- consistent practice across the organisation
Understanding how policies translate into everyday actions helps you work safely, meet expectations during placement, and demonstrate competence in your assessments.

===== FILE: 106 - Safe lifting limits and vulnerable groups.epub =====
Safe lifting limits and vulnerable groups
-
-
In healthcare, there is no single, legally defined “safe maximum weight” for manual lifting because the risk of injury depends on the task, posture, environment, and individual capability. However, Australian workplace guidelines consistently emphasise that healthcare workers should not manually lift a patient unless it is a life‑threatening emergency.
Heavy loads exceeding 16 kg require a formal risk assessment or the use of mechanical aids.
The “No Lift” Policy
Because full manual lifts lead to high rates of back and musculoskeletal injuries, most healthcare organisations follow a “No Lift” or “Zero Lift” approach.
- Patient handling: Workers should not manually lift a patient. Mechanical aids such as hoists, slide sheets, and standing aids must be used.
- Patient participation: Encourage patients to assist or bear weight where appropriate.
- Exceptions: Manual lifting is reserved for immediate, life‑threatening emergencies where equipment is unavailable or inappropriate.
General Weight and Task Guidelines
While there are no universal legal limits, Work Health and Safety (WHS) bodies provide guidance to reduce the risk of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs):
- Seated work: Avoid lifting items heavier than 4.5 kg from a seated position.
- Standing work: Injury risk increases significantly when lifting objects heavier than 16 kg.
- Team lifts: Loads over 55 kg should never be lifted manually without mechanical support or a coordinated team lift.
- (Assist Group, 2023)
Safe lifting limits depend on several factors:
- weight
- distance
- posture
- environment
- individual capability
Vulnerable Groups
Some workers are more vulnerable to manual handling injuries, including:
- younger workers (less experience, less training)
- older workers (reduced strength, flexibility)
- workers returning from injury
- fatigued or stressed staff
The National Code of Practice emphasises:
- tailored training
- ergonomic practices
- risk‑aware decision‑making
- (Safe Work Australia)

===== FILE: 114 - Promoting ability and preventing immobility.epub =====
Promoting ability and preventing immobility
-
-
Why Mobility Matters
The significance of mobility often becomes apparent when it is lost. Individuals facing disruptions in their usual health patterns may encounter difficulties with mobility and movement. For example, someone with rheumatoid arthritis might struggle to move in and out of bed. The ability to move freely is vital for maintaining independence, and a loss of mobility can significantly impact an individual's capacity to perform daily activities.
Nursing Interventions to Support Mobility
As nurses, we employ various interventions to encourage patient mobility and movement. These may include assisting patients with walking, positioning the limbs of bed‑bound patients to prevent contractures, performing passive range‑of‑motion exercises, and using supportive equipment and transfer techniques (e.g., bed‑to‑chair transfers).
Why Mobility Declines in Hospital
Balance and mobility are often poorer in hospital compared with a person’s usual level of function. This may be due to medications (including anaesthetics), acute illness (e.g., stroke, hip fracture, infection), cognitive impairment, or delirium. Mobility may further deteriorate if the person is less active than usual due to their condition or the hospital environment.
Barriers to mobility in hospital
In acute care settings, mobility might sometimes be considered a lower priority compared to the patient's other medical needs. However, it is vital to understand that reduced or impaired mobility can lead to serious complications, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pneumonia, and pressure injuries. Regardless of why a person is admitted to the hospital, their ability to move and care for themselves will determine the care and services they need while in and after discharge. Older people who are admitted to the hospital, particularly those with diminished mobility, are at a higher risk of functional decline and de-conditioning as early as two days after admission.
As nurses, we should encourage all patients, especially older people, to maintain or improve their mobility and self-care skills by participating in activities suitable for their presenting conditions. The hospital environment is not conducive to patient mobility, with various barriers such as time constraints, staffing limitations, the excessive use of medical devices like urinary catheters, and a common belief that mobilising patients increases their risk of falling.
Assisting patients with mobilisation is a fundamental aspect of nursing care. Nurses must adopt a patient-centred approach and foster effective coordination among physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and patients. Thus, a multi-disciplinary approach is essential to promoting mobility and preventing immobility.

Complications of limited mobility
-
-
How limited mobility affects the body
Effective mobility relies on the seamless interaction of various bodily systems. Coordinated body movements result from the intricate collaboration of the skeletal, muscular, and nervous systems. The musculoskeletal system encompasses bones, joints, ligaments, tendons, cartilage, and skeletal muscles. On the other hand, the nervous system plays a pivotal role in regulating movement and maintaining posture, which includes balance. Understanding the functions of these systems and their potential impacts on an individual's mobility is vital, as several pathophysiological factors can adversely affect mobility. These influences may include:
- Postural Abnormalities: Postural abnormalities can lead to pain and restrictions in a patient's range of motion.
- Impaired Muscle Development: Muscle weakness can result from inadequate muscle development.
- Central Nervous System Damage: Conditions like Parkinson's disease, which affect the central nervous system, can significantly hinder mobility.
- Musculoskeletal Trauma: Direct trauma to the musculoskeletal system, such as fractures or acquired/congenital conditions, may affect mobility.
Part of your role is to recognise and identify the complications of limited mobility. Consequently, understanding how immobility affects our patients and knowing the measures to assist them is essential.

Identifying Mobility and Self-Care Issues
-
-
When assessing the level of mobility-related assistance needed, engaging in open discussions with patients and their carers is imperative, facilitating their active participation in developing a comprehensive care plan. In cases where a patient presents with cognitive impairment or demonstrates symptoms of agitation, anxiety, or impulsivity, it is essential to consider additional strategies for managing their assistance requirements.
The assessment of mobility and self-care challenges often occurs during the initial health history and physical examination conducted when a person is admitted to the hospital. These assessments are crucial for identifying potential issues related to falls or frailty and gaining insights into the patient's overall health and needs. It is of utmost importance that we recognise patients at risk of the following:
- De-conditioning: A decline in strength, balance, or endurance
- Falls
- Loss of independence
Mobility assessment process
Upon a patient's admission to the hospital, mobility and self-care become key determinants influencing the length of stay, discharge planning, and the necessity of support services. Understanding a patient's prior abilities, customary mobility, and self-care practices in their living environment is essential.
Health History Assessment:
- During the health history assessment, you will evaluate the patient's ability to perform Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), including tasks like dressing, feeding, and personal hygiene. You will also examine their exercise routines, activity tolerance, and lifestyle. Additionally, you should inquire about any medications they may be taking for mobility issues, such as pain medication for arthritis.
Functional Assessment:
- A functional assessment focuses on the patient's capacity to independently manage their ADLs, such as dressing, grooming, and bathing. It also involves evaluating their walking ability, whether aided or unaided and the level of assistance required. This assessment may include determining if the patient uses mobility aids like a walking stick or a wheeled walker and whether they are proficient in using them correctly.
Falls risk
- Part of the admissions information is for you to identify if your patient is considered to be a falls risk. You will complete this by going through their health history and functional assessment. On the Adult admission and discharge assessment form, there is a heading that specifically relates to falls assessment. here you have a series of prompts to answer and then score the patient accordingly.
Multidisciplinary collaboration
- Collaboration with other multi-disciplinary healthcare team members is integral to your nursing responsibilities. You may need to involve a physiotherapist or occupational therapist to further evaluate the patient's needs. This ensures that your patient receives comprehensive care and support to achieve their highest level of functioning. This information is also invaluable in planning for the patient's discharge, as it provides a baseline for any necessary rehabilitation services.

- A – Acknowledge: Recognise the patient’s feelings and situation.

- D – Duration: Explain how long the procedure or intervention will take.
- E – Explanation: Clarify what you are doing and why.
- T – Thank: Show appreciation for the patient’s cooperation.
Relevance to Turning and Repositioning:
- Builds trust by explaining why repositioning is necessary (e.g., preventing pressure injuries).
- Reduces anxiety by preparing the patient for what will happen.
- Encourages cooperation, making repositioning safer for both patient and nurse.
- Supports consistent communication across shifts so repositioning schedules are followed.
-
2. Adult Interdisciplinary Care Plan
Purpose:
The Adult Interdisciplinary Care Plan is a collaborative document developed by nurses, physiotherapists, physicians, dietitians, and other healthcare professionals. It outlines the patient’s holistic needs and the interventions required to meet them.
Relevance to Turning and Repositioning:
- Identifies patients at risk of immobility‑related complications (pressure injuries, pneumonia, DVT).
- Provides scheduled interventions such as two‑hourly turns, pressure‑relieving devices, and mobility exercises.
- Ensures accountability across disciplines:
- nurses document repositioning
- physiotherapists support mobility
- dietitians optimise nutrition for skin integrity
- Promotes continuity of care so repositioning is not overlooked during busy shifts.
Why This Matters for First‑Year Nurses
- Patient Safety: Regular turning prevents pressure injuries, respiratory compromise, and circulatory issues.
- Professional Development: HAIDET builds communication confidence; the care plan strengthens teamwork and documentation skills.
- Holistic Care: Repositioning is not just a task — it is part of a coordinated, patient‑centred approach involving the whole healthcare team.

Communication techniques in the handover
-
-
Effective communication is central to safe, person‑centred care. According to Standard 2.7 of the Registered Nurse Standards for Practice, nurses must actively contribute to a culture of safety and learning.
Examples of professional communication in practice
- educating colleagues about new processes or procedures
- escalating concerns about patient deterioration
- handing over care at the beginning or end of a shift
- transferring care between environments (e.g., community to acute care)
What is handover?
Handover occurs whenever responsibility for patient care is transferred between healthcare providers or teams. These transitions are high‑risk moments where communication errors can lead to:
- missed or incorrect information
- delays in care
- adverse outcomes
- patient distress
ISBAR provides a structured approach to reduce these risks.
What is ISBAR handover?
ISBAR:
- standardises communication
- bridges differences in communication styles
- ensures completeness of information
- supports assertive, professional recommendations
- focuses on the clinical problem, not the hierarchy
- builds confidence in communication
It is especially useful when communicating changes in a patient’s condition or when making referrals.
ISBAR and the National Safety and Quality Health Care Standards
Clinical handover is covered under NSQHS Standard 6: Communicating for Safety. This standard recognises that poor communication is a major contributor to adverse events.
Communication can be influenced by:
- The situation: urgency, complexity, distractions
- The method: face‑to‑face, phone, written
- The location: bedside, meeting room, handover space
- The participants: nurses, doctors, allied health, patients, families
ISBAR helps maintain clarity regardless of these variables.

===== FILE: 137 - What is the Clinical Reasoning Cycle_.epub =====
What is the Clinical Reasoning Cycle?
-
-
The Clinical Reasoning Cycle (CRC) is a structured framework that helps nurses think through clinical situations in a clear, logical, and safe way. It guides you to gather information, make sense of what you are seeing, identify what the patient needs, and decide on the most appropriate actions. Rather than relying on guesswork or isolated observations, the CRC supports consistent, evidence‑based decision‑making.
Developed by Tracy Levett‑Jones and colleagues, the CRC is widely used in nursing education and practice across Australia. It reflects how experienced nurses naturally think, but breaks this process into clear steps that students can learn and apply. Each stage of the cycle builds on the one before it, helping you move from assessment → interpretation → planning → action → evaluation.
Understanding the CRC will help you:
- recognise what information is important
- connect assessment findings to patient needs
- make safe, person‑centred decisions
- reflect on your actions and improve your practice
In this topic, you will explore each step of the CRC and learn how to apply it to simple clinical situations, including fluid balance scenarios.


===== FILE: 138 - The eight stages of the clinical reasoning cycle.epub =====
The eight stages of the clinical reasoning cycle
-
-
The Clinical Reasoning Cycle is made up of eight interconnected stages. Nurses move through these steps to make sense of a patient’s situation, decide what actions are needed, and evaluate whether those actions were effective. Although the cycle is shown in a circle, in real practice nurses often move back and forth between stages as new information becomes available.
Below is a simple, first‑year‑friendly explanation of each stage.
1. Consider the Patient Situation
This is your starting point. What is happening right now? Why is the patient here?
2. Collect Cues / Information
Gather relevant information from:
- observations (vital signs, fluid balance, appearance)
- the patient (what they say, how they look)
- documentation (charts, notes)
- the environment (IV fluids, medications)
3. Process the Information
Think about what the cues mean. Look for patterns, changes, or anything that doesn’t fit.
4. Identify Problems / Issues
Based on your interpretation, identify the most important problem(s).
5. Establish Goals
Decide what you want to achieve for the patient. Goals should be realistic and person‑centred.
6. Take Action
Carry out the nursing interventions that will help achieve the goals.
Example: Encourage oral fluids, monitor intake/output, escalate concerns to the RN or medical team.
7. Evaluate Outcomes
Check whether your actions have been effective. Has the patient improved, stabilised, or deteriorated?
8. Reflect on the Process and New Learning


===== FILE: 139 - Common CRC mistakes (and how to avoid them).epub =====
Common CRC mistakes (and how to avoid them)
-
-
Understanding the CRC takes practice, and it’s completely normal for first‑year nursing students to make mistakes as they learn. The list below highlights some common errors and explains how to avoid them. These points are designed to support your learning, build confidence, and help you develop safe, effective clinical reasoning skills.
1. Jumping to conclusions too early
Students sometimes identify the problem before collecting enough cues.
Tip: Slow down. Gather information first, then interpret it.
2. Focusing on one cue instead of the whole picture
It’s easy to fixate on a single symptom (e.g., “dry lips”) and miss patterns. ‘
Tip: Look for clusters of cues — they tell a clearer story.
3. Confusing “identify the problem” with making a diagnosis
Students may try to diagnose a medical condition instead of identifying a nursing problem.
Tip: Keep it simple. Think in terms of needs (e.g., hydration, safety, comfort).
4. Skipping straight to actions
Students often want to “fix” the problem immediately.
Tip: Actions come after goals. Think before you act.
5. Not linking cues to the problem
Students may list cues but not explain what they mean.
Tip: Processing information is about making connections — why does this cue matter?
6. Forgetting to evaluate outcomes
Students may take action but not check whether it worked.
Tip: Evaluation is essential for safe practice and continuous improvement.
7. Treating the CRC as a checklist instead of a thinking process
The CRC is flexible — nurses move back and forth between stages.
Tip: Use the cycle as a guide, not a rigid sequence.
8. Overlooking subtle cues
Especially in older adults, children, or mental health presentations.
Tip: Small changes can be significant — pay attention to behaviour, appearance, and patterns.
9. Not considering the patient’s perspective
Students sometimes focus only on clinical data.
Tip: The CRC is person‑centred — include what the patient says, feels, and prefers.
10. Feeling pressured to be “right”
Students may worry about making mistakes.
Tip: The CRC is about thinking, not perfection. Safe reasoning grows with practice.
