In Module 5, we shift our focus from elimination to personal hygiene, an essential component of safe, person‑centred nursing care. ===== FILE: 04 - Personal hygiene.epub =====
Personal hygiene
-
-
Personal hygiene includes a range of self‑care activities such as bathing, grooming, toileting, and maintaining overall body cleanliness. This involves caring for the skin, hair, nails, teeth, and body, including the perineal and genital areas.
Maintaining personal hygiene is essential for health, comfort, and wellbeing. It helps prevent infection, supports skin integrity, and contributes to a person’s sense of dignity and self‑worth. While many individuals manage their own hygiene needs, illness, injury, or reduced mobility can limit a person’s ability to do so independently. In these situations, nurses play a vital role in providing assistance that is safe, respectful, and responsive to individual needs.
Personal hygiene practices vary across cultures, routines, and personal values. As nurses, it is important to recognise and respect these differences while ensuring care is delivered in a culturally safe and person‑centred manner. Supporting patients with personal hygiene not only promotes comfort and confidence but also provides valuable opportunities to assess their physical condition, identify potential concerns, and support recovery.

===== FILE: 05 - Nursing interventions to promote personal hygiene.epub =====
Nursing interventions to promote personal hygiene
-
-
Nurses play an important role in supporting patients with personal hygiene. These activities are not only essential for maintaining cleanliness but also for promoting comfort, dignity, and overall wellbeing. Personal hygiene practices vary widely between individuals and may be influenced by personal preferences, cultural values, and religious beliefs. Nurses should avoid making assumptions and instead take time to understand each patient’s usual routines and preferences.
Before assisting with hygiene care, it is essential to assess the patient’s needs, preferences, and level of independence. Observing a patient’s appearance can provide valuable cues — for example, unkempt hair or poor hygiene may indicate limited mobility, reduced access to resources, or changes in mental health. When concerns are identified, nurses should respond with sensitivity, seeking to understand the underlying causes and provide appropriate, person‑centred care.

Shaving a male patient
-
-
Assisting with Personal Care: Shaving a Patient
Shaving is a common nursing care activity that contributes to patient comfort, dignity, and hygiene. It also provides an opportunity to assess skin integrity and reinforce infection‑prevention practices. Although it may appear simple, shaving requires careful preparation and attention to patient safety.
Why This Skill Matters
- Maintains dignity and personal identity
- Grooming supports a patient’s self‑esteem and sense of normalcy.
- Supports skin assessment
- Shaving allows nurses to observe skin condition and identify abnormalities.
- Promotes infection prevention
- Clean, well‑maintained skin reduces infection risk.
Risks and Considerations
- Skin trauma and cuts
- Nicks or abrasions increase the risk of bleeding and infection.
- Infection risk
- Shaving disrupts the skin barrier — particularly important for immunocompromised patients.
- Folliculitis and irritation
- Poor technique or unclean equipment may lead to inflammation.
- Cross‑contamination
- Shared or improperly cleaned equipment can spread microorganisms.
- Patient comfort and preferences
- Cultural, personal, or religious beliefs may influence shaving practices.
- Clinical considerations
- In some settings (e.g., pre‑operative care), clipping is preferred over shaving to reduce infection risk.
Assisting with Shaving
1. Position the patient
Ensure the patient is comfortable and supported (upright or semi‑reclined).
2. Prepare the skin
- Cleanse the area with warm water to soften hair
- Apply shaving cream or gel to protect the skin
3. Perform the shave
- Hold the skin taut
- Use short, gentle strokes
- Shave in the direction of hair growth
- Rinse the razor frequently
4. Post‑procedure care
- Rinse the skin and pat dry
- Apply moisturiser or aftershave product if appropriate
- Assess for cuts, irritation, or abnormalities
- Dispose of equipment safely (e.g., sharps container if required)
- Remove gloves and perform hand hygiene
Documentation and Patient Education
Document:
- Completion of the procedure
- Skin integrity and any issues
- Patient comfort and tolerance
Provide simple education:
- Basic skin care
- Infection‑prevention principles
- When to report concerns (e.g., irritation, bleeding)

===== FILE: 09 - Factors affecting sleep and rest.epub =====
Factors affecting sleep and rest
-
-
A range of factors can influence a patient’s ability to achieve restful sleep. These may relate to the individual, their health condition, or the healthcare environment. Understanding these factors helps nurses assess sleep disturbances and implement appropriate strategies to promote rest and recovery.
Common Factors
- Environmental factors
- Noise, lighting, temperature, and interruptions such as observations or alarms.
- Physical factors
- Pain, discomfort, illness, or medical procedures.
- Psychological factors
- Anxiety, stress, fear, or unfamiliar surroundings.
- Lifestyle and habits
- Sleep routines, caffeine intake, and activity levels.
- Medications and treatments
- Certain medications may disrupt sleep or cause drowsiness.
These factors should always be considered when assessing sleep and planning care to support restorative rest.

The challenges of sleeping in the hospital
-
-
Sleep in a hospital environment is often very different from sleep at home. Patients may be unwell, anxious, or uncomfortable, and the clinical environment itself can make rest difficult. Understanding these challenges is important for nurses, as it helps you recognise the barriers patients face and consider how your care can support better sleep.
Patients in hospital settings often experience disrupted sleep due to factors such as noise, lighting, clinical procedures, and unfamiliar surroundings. These challenges can significantly impact recovery, comfort, and overall wellbeing. Gaining insight into the patient experience is essential for providing care that supports rest and promotes healing.

Understanding sensory impairment
-
-
The World Health Organization (WHO) defines impairment as:
“Any temporary or permanent loss or abnormality of body structure or function, whether physiological or psychological… affecting functions that are mental, sensory, or related to internal organs or body structures.”
Sensory impairments—such as visual, auditory, and tactile changes—significantly affect a person’s ability to interact with their environment and perform daily activities. For nurses, understanding these impairments is essential to providing effective, compassionate, and person‑centred care.
Sensory changes can influence:
- Safety — increased risk of falls, burns, or medication errors
- Communication — difficulty receiving or interpreting information
- Independence — challenges with mobility, self‑care, and daily tasks
- Emotional wellbeing — frustration, anxiety, loss of confidence, or social withdrawal
Recognising these challenges allows nurses to tailor care plans to meet each patient’s unique needs, promoting dignity and improving health outcomes.
Nurses equipped with knowledge about sensory impairment can implement strategies such as:
- Using clear, simple language or visual aids for patients with hearing loss
- Ensuring adequate lighting and removing obstacles for those with visual impairment
- Providing reassurance, emotional support, and referrals to services such as occupational therapy or support groups
This holistic approach addresses not only physical needs but also the emotional and social impacts of sensory loss.
Importantly, an individual does not need to experience complete loss of a sense to be considered sensory impaired. Even partial changes in vision, hearing, taste, smell, or touch can significantly affect daily living and mental health. Sensory loss can also contribute to social isolation, affecting not only the individual but also their partners, family, carers, and friends.
