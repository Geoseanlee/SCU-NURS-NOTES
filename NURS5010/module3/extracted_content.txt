Overview
-
-
In this module, we begin exploring the essential foundations of nursing practice through hydration, nutrition, fluid balance, and documentation. Hydration and nutrition are central to maintaining patient health, supporting recovery, and preventing complications. Nurses play a critical role in assessing intake, recognising deficits, and promoting safe, evidence‑based interventions.
We also introduce fluid balance and fluid balance charting, where accurate measurement of intake and output provides vital information about a patient’s physiological status. Precision in charting is not only a clinical skill but also a professional responsibility, ensuring continuity of care and supporting clinical decision‑making. Documentation underpins all of these practices, reinforcing accountability, communication, and patient safety.
In the labs, we’ll use paper charts for practice, but the real focus is on understanding the process and developing your competence. When you’re on your Work Integrated Learning (WIL) placement, you’ll use electronic systems for documentation. By learning the principles now, you’ll be ready to adapt easily and confidently in the clinical environment.
This is your opportunity to build practical skills that directly impact patient outcomes. Every technique you learn here brings you one step closer to becoming the kind of nurse who delivers exceptional care.
Furthermore, fluid balance and accurate charting are essential aspects of your Week 7 clinical assessment, emphasising the importance of mastering this skill in your learning journey.
Module objectives
At the completion of this module you will be able to:
- discuss the importance of maintaining homeostasis within the body
- discuss the importance of documentation in nursing
- identify the various ways of supporting the patients' nutritional needs
- discuss the importance of maintaining an accurate fluid balance chart
- competently complete fluid balance charting

Introduction
-
-
You’ve probably heard the saying, “You are what you eat.” There’s truth in that. The food and fluids we consume provide the fuel our bodies need to function, heal, and stay healthy. When we don’t give the body the right fuel, things start to break down — just like putting the wrong fuel in a car. If you filled a petrol engine with diesel, it wouldn’t run properly. The same applies to the human body: too much of the wrong foods, or not enough of the right ones, can lead to obesity, cardiovascular disease, undernourishment, and other health issues.
Hydration works the same way. The body relies on fluids to regulate temperature, transport nutrients, remove waste, and keep every system running smoothly. Without adequate hydration, even simple tasks become harder, and recovery slows.
So how do nutrition and hydration work together, and why are they so important in nursing care? By the end of this module, you’ll understand how these two fundamentals support homeostasis, influence patient outcomes, and guide the decisions you make as a nurse.
Basic concepts of nutrition
Nutrition provides the body with the energy and building blocks it needs to function, heal, and stay healthy. The key nutrients — carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals — each play a specific role in maintaining homeostasis. When patients are unwell, their nutritional needs often change, and understanding these needs helps nurses support recovery.

Undertaking a nutritional assessment
A nutritional assessment helps nurses identify whether a patient is receiving adequate nutrition or is at risk of malnutrition. This assessment includes reviewing dietary intake, medical history, physical signs, and relevant laboratory results.
Early identification of nutritional issues allows for timely intervention, supports healing, and helps prevent complications. Nutritional assessments are especially important for patients recovering from illness or surgery, or those living with chronic conditions such as diabetes or cardiovascular disease.
By understanding a patient’s nutritional needs — including cultural preferences, values, and beliefs — nurses can provide personalised, respectful, and effective care.

Providing nutritional support
Nurses play a key role in ensuring patients receive adequate nutrition to support healing and maintain health. For patients who can eat and drink normally, this includes monitoring intake, encouraging balanced meals, and addressing barriers such as poor appetite or swallowing difficulties.
When oral intake isn’t possible, alternative methods such as nasogastric tubes (NGT) or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tubes may be used. These methods deliver nutrition directly to the stomach and ensure patients continue to receive essential nutrients.
Understanding both oral and alternative feeding methods allows nurses to provide safe, holistic care that meets each patient’s individual needs.

Topic purpose and overview
-
-
Body fluids consist of water and dissolved substances such as electrolytes and gases. Because fluid makes up most of the human body, maintaining fluid balance is essential for health. Almost every organ contributes to regulating this balance, and disruptions can quickly lead to deterioration. Understanding how the body manages fluids — and how nurses assess and document these changes — is a fundamental part of safe nursing practice.
At the completion of this topic, you will be able to:
- Describe the functions of body fluids
- Describe the function, regulation, sources and losses of fluid, within the body
- Accurately assess and document a patients fluid balance

The role of fluid balance within the body
We often hear that we should drink 2–3 litres of water a day, but the real reason goes deeper than “staying healthy.” The body must maintain homeostasis — a balance between fluid intake and output — to support metabolic processes, temperature regulation, circulation, and cellular function. In this topic, we explore how the body maintains this balance, how fluids move between compartments, and how electrolytes work alongside water to keep the body functioning.

Homeostasis
-
-
Homeostasis refers to the body’s ability to maintain a stable internal environment despite external changes. This requires constant adjustments by body systems to stay within a normal range (the “set point”). Understanding homeostasis helps nurses recognise when fluid balance is disrupted and why early intervention is essential.

Fluid management in hospitalised patients
Many hospitalised patients cannot regulate their own hydration due to illness, surgery, or impaired consciousness. When oral intake is inadequate, intravenous (IV) fluids may be required to restore balance. While you will learn more about IV therapy later in your degree, it is important to understand that dehydration, bleeding, burns, and surgical recovery often require careful fluid replacement.
Assessment of fluid balance in the patient
Assessing fluid balance is a core nursing skill. At a student level, this includes:
- History taking: recent illness, surgery, medications, vomiting, diarrhoea, or chronic conditions
- Physical assessment: skin turgor, mucous membranes, oedema, vital signs
- Daily weights: the most sensitive indicator of fluid changes
- Intake and output monitoring: oral, IV, urine, vomit, drains
- Laboratory results: electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, haematocrit
- Observation of symptoms: thirst, dizziness, fatigue, breathlessness
Accurate documentation on a fluid balance chart transforms these observations into actionable data for clinical decision‑making.


===== FILE: 15 - Alterations in fluid balance.epub =====
Alterations in fluid balance
-
-
Fluid imbalances can occur when the body retains too much fluid or loses too much. Conditions such as heart failure, kidney disease, liver disease, vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, and endocrine disorders can all disrupt fluid regulation. These imbalances affect cardiovascular stability, renal function, and cellular health, making early recognition essential.
Common conditions and fluid balance
Conditions Leading to Fluid Overload
Heart Failure:
Reduced cardiac output causes the body to retain fluid, leading to oedema and shortness of breath.
Example: A 72‑year‑old with chronic heart failure presents with swollen ankles and breathlessness. Their fluid balance chart shows a positive balance, and diuretics are prescribed.
Renal Impairment
When the kidneys cannot excrete fluid effectively, hypervolemia can occur.
Example: A patient with stage 4 kidney disease has generalised oedema and high blood pressure. Strict intake/output monitoring and fluid restriction are required.
Liver Disease:
Reduced protein synthesis and portal hypertension can cause ascites and peripheral oedema.
Reduced protein synthesis and portal hypertension can cause ascites and peripheral oedema. Example: A patient with cirrhosis develops abdominal distension. Nursing care includes monitoring abdominal girth and administering diuretics as ordered.
Conditions Leading to Fluid Deficit
Gastrointestinal Disorders:
Vomiting and diarrhoea can rapidly deplete fluids and electrolytes.
Example: A child with gastroenteritis shows dry mucous membranes and tachycardia. Oral rehydration therapy is commenced.
Burns
Damage to the skin causes significant fluid loss and shifts into injured tissues.
Example: A patient with 30% burns requires aggressive fluid resuscitation to prevent hypovolemic shock.
Endocrine Disorders:
Diabetes insipidus causes excessive fluid loss, while SIADH leads to inappropriate fluid retention.
Example: A patient with diabetes insipidus presents with polyuria and intense thirst. Monitoring urine output and administering prescribed desmopressin are key interventions.
Why this matters for nursing practice
Fluid imbalances affect cardiovascular stability, kidney function, and cellular health. Early recognition — through assessment, daily weights, and accurate fluid balance charting — is essential for preventing complications and supporting recovery.

===== FILE: 17 - Importance of fluid balance documentation.epub =====
Importance of fluid balance documentation
-
-
Accurate fluid balance documentation is essential for safe, high‑quality nursing care. Fluid charts provide a clear record of a patient’s intake and output, allowing healthcare teams to identify trends, recognise early signs of deterioration, and make informed clinical decisions. When documentation is incomplete or inaccurate, the risk of dehydration, fluid overload, misdiagnosis, and treatment delays increases.
In Australia, nurses are professionally and legally required to document clinical observations—including fluid balance—in a timely, clear, and truthful manner. These records support interprofessional communication, continuity of care, and compliance with national safety and quality standards. They also contribute to auditing, quality improvement, and evaluation of nursing performance.
Effective documentation is more than a task—it is a critical component of patient safety, professional accountability, and evidence‑based practice.
Accurate fluid balance documentation is essential for effective communication in nursing care. Because many healthcare professionals contribute to a patient’s treatment, fluid balance charts—whether paper‑based or electronic—serve as a shared reference point. When intake and output are not recorded clearly or consistently, communication breaks down, increasing the risk of dehydration, fluid overload, misdiagnosis, and preventable harm.
Fluid balance charts provide written evidence of observations, interventions, and reassessments. They reflect the patient’s physiological status and can highlight early signs of deterioration or improvement. These records also support communication between nurses, doctors, allied health staff, patients, and families, ensuring continuity and safety in care.
The NSQHS Communicating for Safety Standard (Action 6.11) requires that critical information, reassessment outcomes, and changes to care plans are documented contemporaneously. Fluid balance charts directly support these requirements, reinforcing their role as both a clinical and communication tool in multidisciplinary practice.
Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care. (2021). National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards (2nd ed.). ACSQHC. https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au
Documenting fluid balance: Legal requirements and professional accountability
Accurate fluid balance documentation is not only a clinical responsibility—it is a legal and professional obligation. Every entry forms part of the patient’s permanent healthcare record and must be truthful, complete, and recorded at the time of observation. Inadequate or delayed documentation can compromise patient safety and expose practitioners to legal scrutiny.
Fluid balance charts are considered legal documents. They must reflect the nurse’s assessment, reasoning, and actions across all stages of care. Poor documentation can interrupt continuity of care and contribute to adverse outcomes such as dehydration, fluid overload, or medication errors.
Electronic medical records (EMRs) are now widely used, but the shift to digital systems does not reduce the nurse’s accountability. Each entry must still be accurate, timely, and aligned with organisational policies.

Types of documentation
-
-
Documentation can be paper-based, electronic or a mix of both. It can also take a number of forms, including the care plan, handover notes, checklists, pathology results, operation reports and discharge summaries. All organisations are required to have in place systems to ensure the essential information about a persons care is documented in the healthcare record (NSQHS, n.d)
You will be introduced to the various types of documentation for nursing notes in upcoming units, however, for this unit we are going to focus on the following types of documentation
- fluid balance charting
- vital signs charting (covered in module 2)
Fluid balance charting
Hospitalised patients are at increased risk of fluid balance disorders, and many ofthese can be prevented with accurate monitoring. Fluid balance charting is a fundamental nursing skill that supports safe, evidence-based care by guiding fluid prescriptions and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions such as diuretics.
Fluid balance refers to the difference between fluid intake and output over a 24-hour period.  It helps clinicians determine whether a parient's fluid status is positive, negative or balanced. For this unit, the focus is on oral intake and measurable output such as urine, vomit and liquid stool.
Although fluid balance charts are widely used, research shows that errors in documentation, such as incomplete calculations, are common  (Leinum et al., 2023). Accurate, timely recording is essential to support clinical decision-maling and maintain patient safety.
