# PHAR2001 — Module 0: Unit Orientation

## Overview

Module 0 is the entry point to PHAR2001 Introductory Pharmacology. It explains what the unit is designed to develop, how to approach its online and tutorial components, where the main teaching and calendar information appears, and how the interactive pharmacology textbook presents learning. The orientation is not a substitute for the later pharmacology modules: it prepares students to use them, know where support and assessment information is located, and practise the lesson format before the assessed foundational material begins. [S1] [S8] [S9] [V9] [V10]

The Unit Assessor links success with working through the lessons and activities rather than skipping around, and the calendar overview similarly distinguishes online module study from tutorial attendance: tutorials support preparation but do not replace the online content. Module 0 therefore gives a practical model for the study habits used through the rest of the unit: follow the study plan, watch the short teaching videos, attempt the associated activities, use the feedback, and make organised notes that can be reviewed. ## 1. Course expectations and learning purpose

### The learning outcomes set the destination

PHAR2001 begins with three Unit Learning Outcomes. By the end of the unit, students should be able to explain core principles of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics; describe mechanisms of action, pharmacological effects, therapeutic uses, contraindications, safety issues, and side effects for major drug classes and commonly used therapeutic agents; and discuss drug toxicity and drug interactions using examples while considering individual variability in drug response. These outcomes give the unit a progression: understand the principles that explain medicines, apply that understanding to drug classes and agents, and then reason about harms and interactions in context. [S1]

The outcomes are broad rather than a list of individual medicines to memorise. They ask students to connect a medicine with what it does, why it is used, when it may be unsuitable, and what safety concerns or unwanted effects matter. The final outcome also explicitly asks for examples and individual variability, so the intended learning is not limited to repeating a general definition. Module 0 does not teach the pharmacology mechanisms themselves; it points forward to the foundational and drug-focused modules where those outcomes are developed. [S1] [V1] [V9]

The Unit Assessor describes the reasoning pathway behind learning pharmacology: first understand relevant anatomy and physiology, then consider how a disorder changes that normal functioning, and then place a medication in that setting. In that approach, a drug is not an isolated fact. The learner asks how it addresses a disorder’s signs and symptoms, or whether it reverses part of the disorder. The assessor also emphasises forming a mental picture of complex processes and clinically relevant medicine information, rather than relying only on fact collection. [V1]

This way of thinking is consistent with the unit outcomes. A mechanism of action becomes easier to interpret when connected to the body process it affects; therapeutic use becomes more meaningful when placed beside the condition being treated; and safety issues become part of the same explanation rather than a detached list. These are study implications of the outcomes and the assessor’s explanation, not extra pharmacology content supplied by Module 0. [S1] [V1]

### Engagement is part of the course design

The Unit Assessor asks students to engage with all the videos and activities and to stay with the teaching sequence. The welcome video contrasts this complete participation with skipping sections or doing very little work. It reports that students who fail have often engaged with very little of the unit, but this statement is the assessor’s observation in the welcome video; it should not be turned into a personal prediction or a guaranteed result for any individual. The practical course expectation is to work through the material, not to assume that a quick scan will be enough. [V1]

The same expectation appears in the online learning description. The interactive textbook moves from a video to an activity, and the video describes the activity as a way to turn material from something passively watched into knowledge the student can actively use. A student who watches the clip but skips the activity has not completed the learning sequence being demonstrated. Likewise, attending a tutorial does not remove the need to study the corresponding online module. [V2] [V10]

Module 1 receives particular emphasis because it supplies foundational information needed to understand later medication content. The calendar video describes it as substantially larger than an average module and says that the Week 2 quiz provides a checkpoint on that foundational material. The welcome and wrap-up videos also describe Module 1 as a larger-than-usual introduction, including pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. This is a reason to pace the work and make notes as the course proceeds, rather than leaving the first module until immediately before the quiz. [V1] [V2] [V3] [V9]

### Assessment conduct and the limits on GenAI

The orientation’s GenAI guide says that a tool may be used for an assessment only where the assessment brief specifically approves it. It lists Assessment 1, the quiz, as “NO AI AT ALL”; for Assessment 2, the workbook, Grammarly is permitted only for formatting, spelling, and grammar; and for Assessment 3, GenAI may assist in finding suitable resources for answering questions. The guide also explains the university’s three levels of permitted use: full use, purpose-specific use, or no use. Students must follow the level and boundaries set in the relevant brief rather than treating approval for one task as approval for another. [S3]

The same guide frames responsible use as more than choosing a tool. Students are told not to enter personal details, assessment questions, or sensitive SCU information; to check the accuracy of generated material; and to ensure that the tool supports rather than replaces learning. It asks students to keep a record of tool use using the PAIR framework, acknowledge and reference use as instructed, and review the GenAI Declaration before submitting an assessment. These are course expectations in the orientation, and the assessment-specific brief remains the place to check exactly what is allowed. [S3]

## 2. Teaching team, communication, and feedback

### Who teaches the unit

The Unit Assessor introduces himself in the welcome video as Ken Wojciechowski and says that he will be the unit’s assessor for the term. He describes teaching clinical and biomedical science units at Southern Cross University for 23 years at the time of recording, and identifies pharmacology and pathophysiology as subjects he particularly enjoys teaching. The teaching approach the assessor describes is relevant to how students should engage with explanations. He wants learners to build an understanding of the background physiology, recognise what changes in a disorder, and then connect a medicine to that context. He also highlights visualising complex processes and clinically important information. This introduction does not provide a list of all teaching staff or their individual responsibilities; it gives the assessor’s identity and the teaching approach he says he will bring to the unit. [V1]

Tutorials are also an important part of the teaching arrangement. The calendar and assessment videos describe the tutorials as compulsory or mandatory, and say that the tutorial activities prepare students for assessments. ### How to ask questions and share feedback

The wrap-up video says that questions remaining after orientation may mean a student missed one of the videos or activities. It suggests first revisiting the orientation material, or posting a question on the discussion board. The board is described as monitored by the Unit Assessor, with other students also able to contribute. This gives students both a self-check route—return to the relevant item—and a shared route for asking a question and seeing responses. [V9]

The unit’s feedback page offers informal and formal channels. Quick questions, suggestions, or immediate concerns can be sent to the Unit Assessor or tutors by email or raised in workshops and tutorials. The examples show that feedback has been associated with changes to assessment timing or workload, but they do not mean that every suggestion leads to a specific change. [S6]

Students are invited to offer feedback because the unit is intended to improve over time. In the final video, the assessor asks for feedback at the end of term as part of making the learning experience better for future SCU students. Taken together, the direct-contact options, discussion board, and official survey provide different ways to raise a question or comment; the appropriate route depends on whether the need is immediate help, a question for wider discussion, or formal feedback about the unit. ## 4. Pre-term orientation: rehearse the learning format

### Why the pre-term activities are included

The unit’s first welcome page tells students to complete the pre-term activities at the end of the orientation, just before the modules. It describes these activities as an introduction to the videos and to the way learning will work in PHAR2001. The pre-term section then invites students to practise using the interactive pharmacology textbook through an introduction and three example lessons. The purpose is orientation and practice with the course format, not a replacement for the subsequent modules. [S1] [S9]

The introduction to the three lessons asks students to download the lesson notes if useful, then open the video and engage with the activities. It also recommends beginning a folder of notes that can be useful for the final exam. This is a specific organizational suggestion: assemble learning material as it is encountered, rather than waiting until a later assessment to build a record of what was taught. The exact exam conditions and allowed materials should be confirmed in the relevant assessment instructions; Module 0’s note-making advice is about preparation. [S9] [S13]

The pre-term activities demonstrate that a lesson may include more than a video. Students may need to open a linked table, complete a drag-and-drop exercise, answer a multiple-choice item, or review feedback after an answer. One activity explicitly points out that there are two drag-and-drop exercises, which is a reminder to check the whole activity area rather than stopping at the first interaction. A complete attempt includes both viewing and responding. [S10] [S11] [S12]

The activity pages mention video speed controls as an accessibility or pacing choice: once the video starts, a student can hover over the “1X” control to access other speeds. The point is not to change what the lesson contains, but to adjust the playback pace to the learner. The formulation lesson is described as a short video with a table and two drag-and-drop exercises; the pregnancy-category lesson is described as a brief video followed by a multiple-choice question. [S10] [S11] [S12]

### Practice lesson 1: scheduling of drugs

The scheduling lesson asks students to watch a short video and complete a drag-and-drop exercise. The activity page includes a correction to the video wording: it says that alprazolam (Xanax) and flunitrazepam are the only two diazepines that are Schedule 8 “at this time”. The statement is explicitly time-bound and tied to the course activity, so it should be read as the clarification supplied in that page rather than generalized into a timeless regulatory claim. Students should use current authoritative information when a current scheduling question requires verification. [S10]

The Panopto video summary describes Australian scheduling as a national framework and mentions the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA), along with Schedules 2, 3, 4, and 8. The summary distinguishes pharmacy medication, pharmacist-only medication, prescription-only medication, and controlled drugs requiring physician approval. Because the primary page asks students to complete the video and activity, this example also shows why the activity matters: the student is practising how categories are represented, not just watching a clip. The video summary is an orientation-level account, not a substitute for the full lesson or a current regulatory reference. [V5] [S10]

The scheduling example also illustrates the importance of jurisdiction and date when learning a regulation. The video summary stresses that Australian scheduling differs from other countries, while the activity page adds a qualifier that limits its statement to “at this time”. A student should therefore retain the course-specific distinction, avoid importing an overseas schedule into an Australian context, and check a current source if asked for present-day regulatory status. [V5] [S10]

### Practice lesson 2: medicinal formulations

The medicinal-formulations activity asks students to watch a two-minute video, use an accompanying table, and complete two drag-and-drop activities. It specifically warns that a second word-drag activity is present, indicated by a blue circle to the right. This makes the lesson a useful demonstration of the interactive format: the table and both activities are part of the practice, and completion requires checking for each component. [S11]

The video summary focuses on how the route of administration can matter when a medicine is extensively metabolised by the liver. It describes blood from the gastrointestinal tract reaching the liver before entering systemic circulation, and gives sublingual sprays and transdermal patches as alternative routes mentioned in the lesson. It also says the supplied table contains the relevant information for that lesson. These ideas belong to the medicinal-preparations content; the orientation uses them here to show how a short explanatory video and a compact reference table work together. [V6]

The learning task is to connect the video explanation with the table and then use the two activities to practise the distinctions presented. The orientation does not include the table’s contents, so students should open the linked table in Blackboard instead of trying to reconstruct it from this note. The video summary says the table is intended to contain the material students need for the lesson, which makes the provided table the appropriate reference for the activity. [S11] [V6]

### Practice lesson 3: pregnancy categories

The third example is a short video on pregnancy categories followed by a multiple-choice question. Its role in Module 0 is to demonstrate another interaction pattern: watch the explanation, then test recognition or understanding with a question. The orientation page does not reproduce the question’s answer or provide a full category table. Students should complete the actual activity and use the lesson material rather than infer an answer from the title alone. [S12]

The video summary identifies a feature of the Australian pregnancy-category system that can be confusing: the categories are not a simple ranking in which each letter always means a predictable increase or decrease in risk. It highlights category B as a category that may reflect insufficient information for a definitive classification and which may later be classified as A, C, or D. The summary contrasts this with a strictly hierarchical interpretation and describes category X as a category associated with teratogenicity and avoidance in women of childbearing age. These are the key distinctions reported in the supplied video summary; the full lesson should be consulted for the precise teaching context. [V7]

For study purposes, the lesson warns against treating a category label as if it were a complete explanation. The video summary says the Australian system is not strictly hierarchical and that category B does not, by itself, settle a medicine’s eventual classification. The activity is therefore a prompt to read what the category means in its own system and context, rather than assuming that a letter can be interpreted without the accompanying explanation. [V7] [S12]

### What the three examples teach about participation

Across the three examples, the student encounters different response formats: drag and drop for scheduling, a table and two word-drag activities for formulations, and a multiple-choice question for pregnancy categories. The course instructs students to engage with each video and activity, and in the scheduling activity explicitly says that some answers provide feedback. Reading that feedback matters because an interaction is not complete merely when an answer is selected; the feedback is part of the learning material. [S10] [S11] [S12]

The pages also make clear that small details in the online interface matter. A second activity may be placed beside the first, a supporting table may need to be opened separately, and a playback control may offer a different speed. Checking the page for all its linked components is therefore a practical part of studying the interactive text. The course’s own examples model this careful navigation. [S10] [S11]

## 5. How the interactive pharmacology textbook works

### A lesson is a sequence, not a video library

The assessor describes the interactive textbook as a sequence of short videos followed by activities. Videos provide a focused explanation and help students form a picture of the material; the activity then gives the learner an opportunity to use or retrieve that information. In the assessor’s description, this is the movement from passive exposure while watching toward active knowledge. Thus, the unit’s learning model depends on completing both parts, not treating the video as the whole lesson. [V10]

The orientation video about practising the first three lessons describes the upcoming format as a series of videos with activities. Its summary says that content usually spread across two chapters of a standard pharmacology textbook has been condensed into about 7.5 minutes of video content and related activities. This indicates that the lessons are designed to focus attention on selected material and then let students practise it. It does not mean that students can ignore the activity or that every topic in the unit is limited to 7.5 minutes. [V4]

The later video about online learning says that the content folder contains the interactive pharmacology textbook and that the material is presented through short videos, often around five minutes, followed by activities intended to consolidate what was studied. It also says that discipline leads contributed to deciding what content to include, with the aim that the selected material would be valuable for future study and career. This statement explains the design intention presented by the assessor; it does not add a separate list of module topics. [V10]

### Where to find notes and presentations

Students are told that notes are provided for the interactive textbook. The assessor explains that the unit is divided into six modules, each with individual chapters, and each chapter with lessons and interactive activities. Notes for a chapter are found near the top of its introductory page. For Modules 2–6, a PowerPoint presentation is also available. This organization means students should look at the chapter introduction before beginning its lessons to locate the study notes. [V8]

The notes are intended to be used alongside, not instead of, the videos and activities. The orientation activity invites students to download notes for the first three lessons if those notes suit their way of working and to begin a notes folder. The assessor later suggests adding to the provided notes while watching a video and keeping separate space for notes on the activities. These recommendations preserve a distinction between instructor-provided material and a learner’s own observations or answers. [S9] [S13] [V8]

The assessor proposes one concrete paper-based layout: print notes single-sided, annotate the open side while watching the video, and use the reverse side for notes about the activities. This is a suggested method, not a mandatory format. Its value is that it keeps the supplied explanation and the student’s activity reflections together while leaving them distinguishable. Students can adapt the arrangement, but the underlying purpose is to make a reviewable record of both kinds of learning. [V8]

The orientation also says that material from videos and activities in Module 1 is assessed in the Week 2 quiz, and the pre-term warm-up page reminds students that the preceding lessons are assessable in that quiz. The assessment video describes the quiz as a test of foundational information from Module 1. Students should therefore use the interactive textbook materials and the tutorial preparation, and check the assessment brief for the exact coverage and conditions. [S10] [S14] [V2] [V3]

### A repeatable study loop

The source material supports a simple loop for each lesson. First, open the chapter introduction and locate its notes. Next, watch the lesson video with the notes available so that key explanations can be added in the student’s own words. Then complete every associated interaction, including any table, extra drag-and-drop item, or question. Finally, read any feedback and add a concise note about what the activity required or clarified. This sequence follows the described placement of chapter notes and the video-then-activity structure. [S10] [S11] [S12] [V8] [V10]

After a lesson, check that the record contains both explanation and practice. Notes copied or downloaded without personal engagement may preserve the supplied information but do not by themselves show that the student has attempted the activity. Conversely, completing an activity without recording what it taught may make later review harder. The assessor’s advice to annotate notes during videos and reserve space for activity notes gives a practical way to keep both parts together. [S9] [S13] [V8] [V10]

At the end of the orientation, the assessor encourages students to proceed to Module 1 when ready and says to return to the orientation or ask on the discussion board if questions remain. The next module is described as larger because it introduces foundational material such as pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. The transition is therefore deliberate: use the orientation to learn the structure, practise the interaction pattern, and organise notes, then move into the substantial foundational content. [V9] [S1]

### What to do when an online module feels difficult

The final orientation video directly addresses students who may dislike online units. The assessor offers help and encourages students to follow the study plan. This is not a promise that every online format suits every learner; it is guidance to try the designed sequence and seek help when needed. [S15] [V10]

Choosing the most relevant route can prevent a navigation question from becoming a content misunderstanding, and it keeps the learner connected to the materials and people named by the course. ## Common confusions

**“If I attend the tutorial, I can skip the online module.”** The calendar videos say the tutorial prepares students for assessment but does not remove the need to engage with the online module. For the Week 2 quiz, both the Module 1 study and the tutorial are part of preparation. [V2] [V3]

**“Watching the video completes the lesson.”** The interactive textbook is designed as video followed by an activity. The activity is intended to move learning into active use, and some activity pages include more than one exercise or a separate table. Check and complete every component. [S10] [S11] [V10]

**“The quiz is only about what appears in the PowerPoint.”** The assessment overview says the quiz covers Module 1 foundational material and includes questions that rely on the compulsory tutorial; the answers to those questions are not simply available in the PowerPoints or from websites. Use the module and tutorial together. [V2] [V3]

**“Every workbook question must be answered.”** The workbook provides space for three multipart questions, but the assessment overview says students choose two. Confirm the exact task instructions in Blackboard, especially if choosing the Week 5 question and its stated two-day extension. [V3]

**“Module content will directly provide case-study answers.”** The calendar and assessment videos say module material is background needed to understand the case study, while students must draw on resources introduced in the Week 1 tutorial to find accurate information. [V2] [V3]

**“A category label can be interpreted without its system or context.”** The pregnancy-category video summary specifically warns that the Australian categories are not strictly hierarchical and that category B may reflect insufficient information for a definitive classification. Read the explanation supplied with the category rather than assuming a simple letter ranking. [V7]

**“A permission for GenAI applies to every assessment.”** The guide gives different rules for the quiz, workbook, and case study. Check each assessment brief; do not transfer one assessment’s permitted use to another. [S3]
