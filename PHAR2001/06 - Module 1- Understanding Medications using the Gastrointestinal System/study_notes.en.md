# PHAR2001 Module 1 — Understanding Medications using the Gastrointestinal System

## Overview

Module 1 builds the foundation of pharmacology by using the medicines of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) as worked examples. The first chapter starts with the simplest group — antacids and laxatives — because these act by direct chemical or physical means and are largely not absorbed. Later chapters reuse the same clinical habit to introduce cellular receptors and pharmacodynamics, absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME), enzymatic inhibition, and finally neurotransmission and antiemetics. [S1][S2]

The module is deliberately larger than the others: roughly four hours of video lessons plus reinforcing activities, because every later module assumes these concepts. The end-of-term quiz is built from the videos and activities, so the animated explanations are part of the required text rather than decoration. [S1][S3]

## What you should be able to do

By the end of this module you should be able to:

- Explain why antacids, laxatives and mineral oil can act without being absorbed, and relate that property to their safety profile. [S2][V25]
- Compare the four antacid types by mechanism, absorption, electrolyte load, bowel effect and rebound potential. [S5][V44]
- Apply the Rome criteria to decide whether constipation is present, and identify the red flags that make laxatives dangerous. [V28]
- Distinguish osmotic, stimulant, mineral-oil and detergent (faecal-softener) laxatives by mechanism, onset, indications and harm. [V33][V24][V40]
- Describe the four types of cellular receptor and explain affinity, potency, selectivity, agonism, antagonism and inverse agonism. [V6][V18][V30]
- Explain each ADME process, first-pass metabolism, protein binding, half-life, and first-order versus zero-order elimination. [V36][V34][V35][V7]
- Explain how reversible and irreversible enzyme inhibitors work, using the proton pump as the model, and predict drug interactions. [V11][V31][V43]
- Map the four emetic pathways to their neurotransmitters and select the appropriate antiemetic for motion sickness, chemotherapy or GIT irritation. [V12][V10][V2]

## 1. The GIT as a teaching laboratory

The digestive system is a good first subject because its medicines let a beginner separate three ideas that are often blurred together: direct chemical action, receptor action and enzyme action. Antacids neutralise acid by a chemical reaction; laxatives change the physical behaviour of the bowel contents; loperamide acts on receptors; omeprazole inhibits an enzyme. Once those four pictures are clear, the same questions can be asked of any drug in any system. [S2][V25][V16]

The module follows a fixed sequence. Chapter 1 covers direct and chemical actions through antacids and laxatives. Chapter 2 introduces cellular receptors and pharmacodynamics using loperamide and the opioids. Chapter 3 introduces pharmacokinetics using the histamine 2 blockers, then reviews ADME by following omeprazole. Chapter 4 studies enzymes and enzymatic inhibition through the proton pump inhibitors. Chapter 5 studies neurotransmission and the antiemetics. [S1][V8][V26][V22]

| Chapter | Main drug examples | Conceptual focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Antacids, laxatives, mineral oil, docusate | Direct and chemical action; safety before treatment |
| 2 | Loperamide, other opioids | Cellular receptors; pharmacodynamics |
| 3 | Histamine 2 blockers; omeprazole review | Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion |
| 4 | Omeprazole and other proton pump inhibitors | Enzymes; reversible and irreversible inhibition |
| 5 | Antihistamines, anticholinergics, 5-HT3 and dopamine-2 antagonists | Neurotransmission; emesis pathways |

The table summarises the module architecture described in the overview and chapter introductions. [S1][S12][S21][S30][S37]

The method taught throughout is more important than any single drug. For every medicine, first learn the disorder it is meant to treat and the safety issues of that disorder; then learn the normal anatomy and physiology of the organ; then the disordered physiology, or pathophysiology; only then place the drug into that picture. This order prevents the student from memorising indications and contraindications as unrelated lists. [S7][V15][V28][V32]

Safety comes first because the presenting complaint can be more dangerous than the symptom the patient wants removed. Severe constipation may signal an intestinal blockage; giving a laxative then increases movement and fluid ahead of the obstruction and can cause rupture, peritonitis, hospitalisation and death. Diarrhoea can dehydrate an infant faster than an adult; vomiting can produce metabolic alkalosis. A medicine that relieves the symptom while hiding the cause can delay life-saving treatment. [V5][V27][V19]

The relevant anatomy is simple but must be pictured. The GIT has an outer longitudinal and an inner circular muscle layer, both controlled by the enteric nervous system, a large local nerve network running through the gut wall. Glands throughout the small and large intestine secrete mucus and other products into the lumen. The muscles relax and contract in a coordinated way to move contents forward. When the gut senses a blockage, or a bacterial toxin, it responds with stronger contractions and more secretion, which is why obstruction causes colicky pain and mucus-filled “diarrhoea”. [V28]

In the stomach, the lining is folded into gastric pits containing parietal cells. These cells secrete hydrogen ions (protons) into the lumen through the hydrogen–potassium ATPase pump, and the protons combine with chloride to form hydrochloric acid. The same parietal cell is the target of both histamine 2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors, which is why those two classes are best understood together. [V25][V13][V31]

Stomach acid is not waste. It begins the breakdown of food, helps absorb some nutrients and many drugs, and reduces the number and growth of ingested microorganisms. Each of those functions predicts a side effect when acid is suppressed for months or years: poorer nutrient absorption, more infection, and altered drug absorption. [V29]

The module also teaches how to study. Learning has levels: remembering a fact, understanding the concept, and applying it to a scenario. Quizzes after each lesson are not obstacles; they force retrieval and application, which is what makes the memory durable and builds clinical reasoning. [V1]

## 2. Antacids

An antacid is a base that neutralises acid already present in the stomach. It does not reduce acid production. The magnesium and aluminium salts are poorly absorbed and act locally and briefly; the sodium and calcium salts are absorbed and therefore carry systemic effects. The accepted indication is occasional heartburn; the important contraindication is chronic heartburn, because persistent reflux is no longer simple heartburn but gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD, also written GERD). [S3][V25][V44]

The distinction matters. Heartburn is the everyday term for acid moving up from the stomach into the oesophagus. When that reflux becomes long-standing it is called GORD, and at that point neutralising acid after the event does not protect the oesophageal lining. Acid that repeatedly splashes onto oesophageal cells can produce dysplasia, a pre-cancerous change, and dysplasia can progress to oesophageal cancer. A patient who self-treats for ten, twenty or thirty years may never be assessed; most doctors recommend endoscopic evaluation after about twenty years of reflux. [V25][V38]

Reflux happens when the barrier at the lower end of the oesophagus fails or when stomach contents are pushed upward. A muscular ring, the lower oesophageal sphincter, normally stays partly contracted and acts as a gate. Substances that lower its tone include alcohol, muscle relaxants, cigarette smoke and opioids. A very full stomach, as in binge eating, or central obesity with weight around the abdomen, pushes contents toward the top of the stomach. A hiatal hernia, in which the top of the stomach moves through the diaphragm, is the least reversible predisposition and affects about one in five people. [V25]

The chemistry of neutralisation is straightforward. Using magnesium hydroxide as the example, the molecule dissociates in the stomach; magnesium combines with chloride from hydrochloric acid, and the two hydroxide groups combine with hydrogen to form two water molecules. The products are water and magnesium chloride. No receptor and no enzyme are involved. [V44]

There are four antacid types in common Australian use, and they differ mainly by what happens after that reaction. [S5][V44]

| Antacid type | Fate | Main bowel effect | Other problems |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium hydroxide or magnesium citrate | Poorly absorbed; forms osmotically active magnesium chloride | Draws water into the lumen, so diarrhoea | Diarrhoea is the main drawback |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Poorly absorbed; forms aluminium chloride | Constipation | Binds some drugs and nutrients, reducing absorption |
| Sodium bicarbonate | Absorbed | — | High sodium (salt) load; systemic effects |
| Calcium carbonate | Absorbed | — | The most extreme rebound hyperacidity |

The two absorbed salts, sodium bicarbonate and calcium carbonate, produce more systemic side effects precisely because they leave the gut. Sodium bicarbonate adds a salt load; calcium carbonate triggers the strongest rebound. For those reasons neither is usually the preferred antacid. Magnesium and aluminium salts have the fewest systemic effects, but their opposite bowel actions become the basis for choosing between them. [V44]

That choice is a simple piece of clinical reasoning. A client who is normally constipated will usually tolerate a magnesium antacid, because the osmotic magnesium chloride softens the stool. A client who already has loose stools is better served by an aluminium antacid, which tends to constipate. The prescriber is not treating the heartburn alone; they are matching the salt to the patient's bowel pattern. [S5][V44]

Rebound is the second safety problem. When an antacid lowers stomach acidity, the stomach detects the change and signals for more acid secretion. The result can be a more acidic environment than before the dose, called rebound hyperacidity. This is a specific example of homeostasis: the body defends its internal set point, and a drug that disrupts the set point provokes a compensating response. The same logic explains why a diuretic may lower blood pressure for a time and then lose effect as the body adjusts. [V38]

Rebound also explains why antacids should not be taken at the same time as other medicines or with meals if it can be avoided. Acid is needed for the absorption of some drugs and nutrients, and aluminium salts additionally bind some drugs directly. Taking an antacid one to two hours before or after food or other medicines reduces both the pH effect and the binding interaction. [V38][V44]

Antacids are available over the counter, but “over the counter” is not a synonym for “without risk”. The safety issues are reliance instead of assessment, rebound, electrolyte and bowel effects, drug binding, and the serious progression of untreated reflux through dysplasia to oesophageal cancer. [S4][V38][V44]

## 3. Constipation and laxatives

Laxatives are the most frequently abused medicines because many people do not know what constipation actually is. The Rome criteria require at least two of five features within any twelve-week period: fewer than three bowel movements per week; hard stools in more than a quarter of bowel movements; a sense of incomplete evacuation in more than a quarter; excessive straining in more than a quarter; and digital manipulation to assist evacuation. Without two features, a laxative is being used for a sensation rather than a diagnosis. [V28]

Even when constipation is present, a laxative is not first-line. The first recommendations are more water, more fibre-rich food, or added psyllium, and then a review of whether a laxative is still needed. Most guidelines are explicit about this order. [V15]

Understanding why requires the motility picture. The circular and longitudinal muscle layers move contents forward under the control of the enteric nervous system. That local system is itself influenced by the autonomic nervous system. The sympathetic “fight or flight” branch shuts down digestion so that blood and attention can go to muscles; the parasympathetic “rest and digest” branch increases gut motility and secretion. Medicines that mimic sympathetic action, or that block parasympathetic action, therefore cause constipation. Opioids are the clearest example because they act directly on the enteric nervous system to slow motility. [V28]

Other causes follow from the same physiology. A person may have inherently low motility or low mucus secretion. Adequate fibre is needed to break up and bulk the contents, and adequate fluid is needed to keep them soft. A well-hydrated person who repeatedly ignores the urge to defecate will reabsorb more water from the colon and dry the stool. Constipation can also be the first symptom of hypothyroidism or another systemic disorder, which is why persistent constipation deserves assessment rather than another box of laxative. [V28]

The red flags of obstruction must be recognised before any laxative is offered. An obstruction produces colicky pain that comes in waves, small-volume mucus-like diarrhoea as only mucus passes the blockage, and abdominal distension. Not every patient has all three, but any of them changes the plan. A laxative in the presence of a blockage increases movement and fluid ahead of it and can cause rupture, peritonitis, hospitalisation and death. Laxatives are therefore contraindicated in intestinal obstruction. [V28][V5][V33]

### Osmotic laxatives

Osmotic laxatives pull water into the intestinal lumen. Cell membranes are semi-permeable: water crosses freely, but osmotically active particles do not. Water therefore moves toward the side with the higher concentration of particles. When magnesium citrate, magnesium hydroxide or lactulose is present in the lumen, water follows the drug into the gut, the contents become softer, and constipation eases. [V33][S8]

Magnesium salts have a dual identity in this module. As antacids they neutralise acid and produce magnesium chloride; as laxatives the same osmotically active magnesium chloride draws water into the intestine. This is why a magnesium antacid can cause diarrhoea and why milk of magnesia works as a laxative. [V33][V44]

Osmotic laxatives are contraindicated in obstruction for the reason above: fluid accumulates before the blockage and can rupture the bowel. They can also dehydrate a patient who takes a large dose without drinking enough, which is a particular risk because the drug's effect is proportional to dose. [V33]

That proportionality is called a linear dose response. On a graph of dose against effect, the line keeps rising: more drug gives more effect. This is unusual. Most medicines reach a maximum effect just above the recommended dose, and additional doses add toxicity rather than benefit. Paracetamol is the standard counter-example used in the module: a little above the standard dose gives the maximum analgesic effect, and further doses cause liver toxicity without additional pain relief. Assuming that every drug behaves like an osmotic laxative is therefore dangerous. [V33]

Lactulose is a poorly absorbed sugar. It is very sweet and not well tolerated by everyone, so it is often taken with water, fruit juice or milk. Magnesium citrate and magnesium hydroxide are the other common agents. [V33]

### Stimulant laxatives

Stimulant laxatives irritate the gut. The irritation increases movement and secretion, and faster transit leaves less time for fluid reabsorption, so the stool stays wetter and is easier to pass. The group includes bisacodyl, sodium picosulfate, senna and castor oil. [V24]

They are strong and effective, which is exactly why they are restricted. Acceptable uses are occasional constipation when nothing else has worked, and bowel preparation for colonoscopy or another procedure. They are not for regular use. [V24]

The adverse effects follow from that intensity. First, rebound constipation: the smooth muscle adapts to strong stimulation and then needs that level of stimulation to move, so intrinsic motility falls when the drug is stopped. Second, prolonged use can damage the colon and reduce its ability to move faeces permanently. Third, severe electrolyte abnormalities can occur. Marketing that describes the products as gentle does not change those risks. [V24]

Sodium picosulfate is a prodrug, meaning it must be metabolised to become active. It is not absorbed, so the liver cannot activate it; intestinal bacteria perform the conversion. That is why sodium picosulfate is effective only when taken orally, because only the oral route puts it in prolonged contact with colonic bacteria. It is often combined with an osmotic laxative for bowel cleansing before colonoscopy or imaging. [V24]

Bisacodyl has an additional adverse effect: mild proctitis, an inflammation of the inner rectum with sloughing of the rectal mucosa. [V24]

Onset depends on route. Oral stimulant laxatives usually act in about six to fifteen hours, suppositories in about fifteen to sixty minutes, and enemas in about five to fifteen minutes. Oral preparations are normally enteric coated so that the drug passes through the stomach without dissolving; enteric-coated tablets must be swallowed whole and never chewed. [V24][S9]

### Mineral oil and faecal softeners

Mineral oil is a medicinal oil that the body cannot absorb and cannot excrete. That sounds harmless, but it means that any oil that does enter the circulation has no exit. It is used intermittently, never as a long-term dependence. It also dissolves fat-soluble vitamins, so it interferes with vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin E and vitamin K absorption and should be taken on an empty stomach. [V40][S10]

Faecal softeners, also called emollients or surfactants, include docusate. They have detergent-like properties that allow water and fat to mix within the stool, hydrating and softening it. The same detergent action increases the absorption of fat-soluble medicines and can therefore increase their toxicity. [V40]

Combining mineral oil with a detergent softener is the dangerous case. The detergent emulsifies the oil into smaller droplets and acts on the phospholipid bilayer of gut cells, so some mineral oil is absorbed. Because there is no excretion route, the immune system treats the droplets as foreign material and builds a granuloma around them, using B cells, T cells, neutrophils, macrophages and finally fibroblasts. The result is a hard nodule that remains for life unless it is surgically removed. The combination is therefore strictly contraindicated. [V40]

“Contraindicated” and “strictly contraindicated” are not the same. Many drugs are contraindicated together except in specific circumstances; strictly contraindicated means never together. The terms also apply to conditions rather than only to drug pairs: a medicine can be contraindicated in pregnancy, in congestive heart failure, or for seven days before surgery. [V40]

| Laxative group | Examples | Mechanism | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Osmotic | Magnesium citrate, magnesium hydroxide, lactulose | Draws water into lumen | Dehydration; contraindicated in obstruction; linear dose response |
| Stimulant | Bisacodyl, sodium picosulfate, senna, castor oil | Irritates gut; increases motility and secretion | Rebound constipation; colonic damage; electrolyte loss |
| Mineral oil | Medicinal mineral oil | Not absorbed | Fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption; no excretion route |
| Faecal softener | Docusate | Detergent mixes water and fat | Increases absorption and toxicity of fat-soluble drugs |

The table condenses the four groups; the mechanisms are explained above. [V33][V24][V40]

## 4. Diarrhoea: safety before treatment

Diarrhoea is the presenting complaint for the next chapter because loperamide is used to treat it. The safety issues are dehydration, electrolyte abnormality and acid–base disturbance. Normally the kidneys regulate fluid, electrolytes and pH; in diarrhoea the gut overrides that control. Patients lose potassium and magnesium in stool and excrete bicarbonate, the alkaline molecule that prevents blood from becoming too acidic. Serious abnormalities can cause convulsions, other neurological symptoms, coma and death. [V27][S13]

Susceptibility is not evenly distributed. Infants and the very old dehydrate fastest, and infants also suffer brain damage or death from smaller electrolyte shifts. Obesity is an independent risk: adipose tissue is only about ten per cent water, while other tissues average around seventy per cent, so an obese person has less water reserve for the same body mass. [V27]

The signs are dry mouth, concentrated dark urine, excessive thirst and reduced skin turgor. Skin turgor is tested by pinching the skin on the back of the hand and watching how quickly it returns; dehydrated skin returns slowly. In a very elderly person the baseline is already reduced, so the finding must be interpreted against that baseline. [V27]

The primary treatment is fluid: plenty of drinking fluid and oral rehydration solution. Medicines to reduce the diarrhoea come after the patient is safe from dehydration. This ordering is the same safety-first logic used for constipation, and it is the reason the module spends a whole lesson on the disorder before naming the drug. [V27]

## 5. Drug nomenclature and drug classes

Every medicine has at least three kinds of name. The chemical name describes the molecule and is used by laboratory scientists; the brand name is the manufacturer's commercial label; the generic name is the shortened chemical name that appears on the dispenser label. Hundreds of brand names can exist for one molecule, so the generic name is the one worth learning first. [V20][S14]

The generic name often contains an affix that signals the drug class. A drug ending in -olol is a beta blocker; these slow the heart and lower blood pressure. Names ending in -azepam or -azolam are benzodiazepines, used for anxiety and seizures. Corticosteroids are less tidy: the stems -asone, pred- and -cort may appear at the end, the beginning or the middle of the name. [V20]

A drug class is a group of medicines with similar chemical structure, similar mechanism, or both. Antacids neutralise acid; histamine 2 blockers also reduce acid but by a completely different mechanism, so they belong to a different class. Within a class, most members share a suffix and much of their behaviour, although the opioids show that a broad class can contain important exceptions. [V3]

| Class | Affix | Examples | Core action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beta blockers | -olol | Metoprolol, atenolol, propranolol, nebivolol, timolol | Slow heart rate; lower blood pressure |
| Benzodiazepines | -azepam, -azolam | Diazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam, alprazolam, temazepam | Anxiety, seizures |
| Corticosteroids | -asone, pred-, -cort | Prednisone, methylprednisolone, mometasone, fluticasone, hydrocortisone | Suppress immune response |
| Histamine 2 blockers | -tidine | Cimetidine, famotidine, ranitidine | Reduce stomach acid |
| Proton pump inhibitors | -prazole | Omeprazole | Reduce stomach acid |

The affixes and examples are those used in the nomenclature lessons. [V3][V20][S26]

Learning four classes gives a surprising return. Recognising the beta blockers, benzodiazepines, corticosteroids and histamine 2 blockers covers about twenty-two of the two hundred most prescribed medicines. The point is not to guess at a drug's every property from its ending, but to recognise the family and then look up the details. [V3]


## 6. Cellular receptors

A human cell is enclosed by a phospholipid bilayer. The lipid tails attract one another through Van der Waals forces and the heads through hydrogen bonding, so the membrane is held together without being rigid. Embedded in and on the membrane are structural proteins, enzymes, channels, transport carriers and receptors. Three of the four receptor types sit on the cell surface as transmembrane proteins spanning the full thickness of the membrane; the fourth sits inside the cell. [V6][S16]

A receptor receives a ligand, the natural molecule for which it was made. Binding produces a conformational change, a change in the protein's shape, and that shape change starts a cascade inside the cell. The insulin receptor is the model for an enzyme-linked receptor: insulin binds, the receptor changes shape, and the cell places more glucose transport carriers on its surface. A G-protein-linked receptor works through a similar conformational change and a cascade. A ligand-gated ion channel changes shape to open a pore, letting charged particles cross the membrane. An intracellular receptor has a lipid-soluble ligand that crosses the bilayer, and the ligand–receptor complex enters the nucleus, binds DNA and alters protein production. [V6]

| Receptor type | Location | How it signals | Example from the module |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enzyme-linked | Cell surface | Conformational change; enzymatic cascade | Insulin receptor increases glucose transporters |
| G-protein-linked | Cell surface | Conformational change; G-protein cascade | Most common receptor type; examples not specified in the module |
| Ligand-gated ion channel | Cell surface | Opens a pore for ions | Simplest receptor used to explain agonism |
| Intracellular | Inside the cell | Ligand–receptor complex acts on DNA | Lipid-soluble hormones |

The four types and their signalling logic are the core of the receptor lesson. [V6][S16]

Receptor number is not fixed. A cell can insert more receptors or remove them from the surface, changing how many messages it receives. This is the mechanism behind opioid tolerance. When a person takes pharmacological doses of an opioid for about three weeks, the cell recognises an abundance of stimulation and pulls opioid receptors off the surface, degrades them and reuses the parts. Fewer receptors means less pain relief from the same dose, so the patient experiences more pain and asks for a stronger opioid. The compensation does not happen at every receptor, but it explains many rebound effects and why some medicines must not be continued longer than recommended. [V6]

## 7. Affinity, potency and selectivity

Binding is not a simple lock-and-key shape match. The same forces that hold the membrane together also hold a ligand in its receptor: Van der Waals attraction, hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds, with ionic bonding the strongest of the three. A useful analogy is a device with magnetic snap-in fittings. The accessory is guided into place by attraction, and it holds until it is pulled away. Most drug–receptor binding is reversible, but the strength of the hold varies. [V18][S17]

Affinity is that strength of binding. A high-affinity drug stays on the receptor longer and is harder to pull off; a low-affinity drug pops off quickly. Because a receptor sends messages while it is occupied, a longer occupancy can produce more signalling. [V18]

Potency is a different idea: it is the amount of drug needed to produce a given effect. If drug A produces the same effect at three milligrams as drug B produces at thirty milligrams, drug A is more potent. Potency can be influenced by affinity, but affinity is not the only determinant. [V18]

Selectivity describes how well a drug discriminates between receptors. Adrenaline binds both alpha-1 receptors on peripheral blood vessels, causing constriction and raising blood pressure, and beta-2 receptors on the bronchi, causing dilation and improving airflow. A non-selective adrenergic drug would do both. A selective beta-2 drug was developed so that asthma could be treated by opening the bronchi without constricting blood vessels. Conversely, early antihistamines blocked histamine 1 receptors as intended but also blocked acetylcholine receptors, and that second binding produced side effects. Drug development continually pushes toward greater selectivity because a medicine that binds only the receptor responsible for the therapeutic effect has fewer off-target effects. [V18]

The opioid analgesics show all three concepts at once. Morphine and fentanyl both bind the mu opioid receptor in the spinal cord, where a gating mechanism reduces the number of pain impulses reaching the brain. Fentanyl is about one hundred times more potent than morphine, so roughly one hundredth of the dose produces the same analgesia. A major reason is that fentanyl has a much stronger affinity for the mu receptor, stays bound longer and generates more signalling. [V18]

The mu receptor also has two subsets with different jobs. Mu-1 mediates analgesia. Mu-2 mediates respiratory depression, constipation and the euphoria that drives addiction. A drug discovery program would therefore seek mu-1 selectivity and high potency at mu-1, because that combination could relieve pain with less respiratory depression and less addiction. This is the practical reason selectivity is studied rather than merely defined. [V18]

## 8. Agonists, antagonists and inverse agonists

At a receptor, a drug can do one of four things. A full agonist binds and produces the same response as the natural ligand. A partial agonist binds and produces less than the full response, even when every receptor is occupied. An antagonist binds without producing a response and blocks the natural ligand. An inverse agonist binds and reduces activity below the receptor's resting level, which is only observable when the receptor has constitutive activity. [V30][S18]

The ligand-gated ion channel makes the distinctions visible. The natural ligand binds, an ionic bond pulls part of the protein into a new shape, and the channel opens so that calcium or another ion enters. A full agonist opens the gate to one hundred per cent of its capacity. A molecule of the same shape that is slightly too large to let the gate open fully behaves as a partial agonist. A molecule with the same shape but without the critical ionic bond binds at the same site and produces no shape change; it is an antagonist, and because it occupies the same site it is a competitive antagonist. A large amount of antagonist makes it much less likely that the natural ligand will find the receptor. [V30]

An inverse agonist is best remembered as an antagonist that also suppresses ongoing activity. Some receptors are not silent when unoccupied; they show constitutive activity simply because the protein is present. An inverse agonist lowers that baseline. Not every receptor has constitutive activity, and some genetic disorders respond only to an inverse agonist, which is why the category exists. [V30]

| Drug behaviour | Occupies receptor? | Produces response? | Effect of raising the dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full agonist | Yes | Yes, full | Response rises to the receptor maximum |
| Partial agonist | Yes | Yes, submaximal | Response plateaus below the full maximum |
| Antagonist | Yes | No | Blocks more ligand; effect depends on ligand concentration |
| Inverse agonist | Yes | Lowers constitutive activity | Greater suppression of baseline activity |

The table separates occupancy from efficacy, which is the central idea of the lesson. [V30]

The distinction between “inhibitor” and “antagonist” is worth fixing early. Agonists, antagonists and inverse agonists describe cellular receptors. Inhibitors describe enzymes. A drug that blocks the hydrogen–potassium ATPase is an enzyme inhibitor, not a receptor antagonist, even though both prevent a physiological effect. Keeping the vocabulary separate prevents confusion in later chapters. [V30][V16]

## 9. Loperamide and the opioids

Opioids are both a drug class and a group of endogenous neurotransmitters. As neurotransmitters they are chemical messengers released from one neuron to influence the next, and they can be excitatory or inhibitory. The endogenous opioids are inhibitory and act as central nervous system depressants. Exogenous opioids include morphine, codeine, heroin, oxycodone and tramadol; some are natural products of the opium poppy and some are synthetic. They relieve pain because they mimic the body's own analgesic system. [V37][S15]

Opioid receptors are not confined to pain pathways. In the brain they produce euphoria; in the brainstem they produce respiratory depression; in the enteric nervous system they reduce intestinal motility. Those three locations explain the classic triad of opioid effects and side effects: analgesia with euphoria, dangerous slowing of breathing, and constipation. [V37]

Loperamide is an opioid, but at normal doses it does not relieve pain, does not cause euphoria and does not depress respiration. The explanation is pharmacokinetic rather than receptor-based: almost none of it is absorbed. Figures given in the lessons range from about one-third of one per cent to less than half a per cent, so the drug remains in the gut and acts on the enteric nervous system close to the lumen rather than on the brain. [V4][V37][S15]

Its therapeutic action is to slow peristalsis. Peristalsis is the wave-like muscular contraction that moves gut contents forward. Endogenous opioids normally restrain it, allowing more time for fluid absorption and producing firmer stool. Loperamide is an agonist at those same opioid receptors in the gut, so it slows transit and relieves diarrhoea. Because a pharmacological dose is much larger than the body's own opioid release, the effect is more pronounced. [V41][S19]

Loperamide is not the only option. Most opioid medicines reduce diarrhoea, and anticholinergic drugs are also used. Loperamide is normally preferred because most of it stays in the GIT and works locally, avoiding the central and systemic effects of other opioids. [V41]

Misuse reverses that advantage. Taking thirty or more times the recommended dose can cause severe constipation, intestinal obstruction, acute peritonitis, hospitalisation and death from septicaemia. The user may not obtain the desired opioid high, and may instead suffer a fatal arrhythmia, because high-dose loperamide is cardiotoxic. The lesson also notes that more than one hundred thousand people die from opioid overdose each year, and that many of those deaths involve loperamide taken above the recommended dose. The chapter summary condenses the same points: generic names are the priority, endogenous opioids act as neurotransmitters, and limited absorption is why loperamide avoids the usual opioid effects. [V37][V39]

The opioid receptor system can also be blocked deliberately. Methylnaltrexone is an opioid antagonist with very poor absorption, almost the pharmacological opposite of loperamide. It is given to relieve severe opioid-induced constipation, for example in a patient on high-dose opioid analgesia for cancer pain, because it blocks opioid receptors in the gut and is poorly absorbed, so its action stays largely peripheral. Naloxone is an opioid antagonist used for opioid overdose with respiratory depression. It binds strongly enough to displace opioids already on the receptor and rapidly reverses the life-threatening effect, but it has a very short half-life and may need to be given more than once while the original opioid is cleared. [V41]

## 10. Pharmacokinetics: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion

Pharmacodynamics asks what the drug does to the body. Pharmacokinetics asks what the body does to the drug: how it is absorbed, where it is distributed, how it is metabolised and how it is excreted. The Greek roots help the division: pharmaco means drug or poison, and kinetics means movement. [V8][S21]

| Process | Question answered | Key variables |
|---|---|---|
| Absorption | How does the drug reach the blood? | Route, lipophilicity, surface area, motility, blood flow, food |
| Distribution | Where does the drug go? | Protein binding, body compartments |
| Metabolism | How is the drug changed? | Liver, cytochrome P450, phase I and II reactions |
| Excretion | How is the drug removed? | Kidney, half-life, reaction order |

The four processes are the skeleton of the chapter. [V8][S21]

### Absorption

Absorption is the movement of drug from the site of administration into the systemic circulation. The route determines the path. An oral tablet passes down the oesophagus to the stomach, dissolves, is absorbed mainly in the small intestine, and then travels in the portal venous blood to the liver before reaching the right heart, the lungs, the left heart and finally the systemic arterial circulation. A sublingual spray, a transdermal patch or a lower rectal suppository enters the venous system directly and bypasses the liver on that first pass. A subcutaneous injection enters the intercellular space and then the venous blood. An inhaled anaesthetic enters the alveoli and then the pulmonary capillaries, leaving only the left heart before the systemic circulation. [V34][V35][V36][S22]

The only routes that require no absorption are intravenous and intra-arterial injection, because the drug is placed directly into the blood. Those routes give one hundred per cent bioavailability. Every other route produces a lower and more gradual rise in plasma concentration. The peak concentration, or Cmax, is lower for an oral dose than for the same dose inhaled or injected, and the time to peak is longer. A high Cmax is associated with a greater risk of overdose and side effects because a high concentration is more likely to bind receptors or enzymes that are not the intended target. The concentration also falls fastest after the highest peak, which corresponds to the first half-life. [V36]

Very little absorption occurs in the stomach. Even aspirin, whose chemistry favours absorption in acid, is poorly absorbed there because it does not stay long and because the stomach surface area is small. The small intestine is specialised for absorption, with villi and microvilli providing more than thirty square metres of surface, roughly three hundred and twenty square feet. [V23]

Drug properties matter. Smaller molecules, the acidic or basic nature of the drug, and lipophilicity all influence absorption. Lipophilic drugs cross cell membranes easily and are usually absorbed faster and more completely than hydrophilic drugs. Water-soluble drugs can pass through channels, but the lipid bilayer is a barrier to them. This is why a water-soluble cream stays largely local on the skin, where keratin protects the surface, while a transdermal patch is formulated with a lipophilic drug that can penetrate to the dermis and reach the blood. [V23]

Physiological factors matter too. Faster movement through the GIT reduces contact time and absorption. Blood flow to the gut affects how quickly absorbed drug is carried away. Eating increases blood flow, which might suggest taking medicines with food, but food often lowers bioavailability, the fraction of the dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. In general, oral medicines are best taken about an hour before or two hours after a meal. [V23]

### Distribution

After absorption, drug distributes among four broad compartments: blood, fat, extracellular fluid and intracellular fluid. Before it reaches the tissues, an orally absorbed drug passes through the liver, the hepatic first-pass effect. Many drugs also bind to plasma proteins, especially albumin and the globulins, and highly lipophilic drugs are often highly bound. [V34][S23]

Only the unbound fraction can leave the blood, reach receptors and be metabolised. This sounds as though a highly bound drug would be mostly inactive, but binding is a reversible equilibrium. If eighty per cent is bound and twenty per cent free, the free fraction enters the hepatocytes and is metabolised; as the free concentration falls, more drug dissociates from the protein to restore the equilibrium. Eventually all reversibly bound drug becomes available. A highly bound drug may take longer to act and longer to be cleared. [V34]

Protein binding also creates a displacement interaction. Mrs Ling is stable on drug A, which is highly bound to albumin. She is prescribed drug B, also highly bound to albumin but with a stronger affinity. Drug B can displace drug A from the binding sites, raising the free concentration of drug A and potentially causing toxicity. The risk is often overstated in textbooks, but it is real, and it contributes to adverse reactions in people with low protein levels, such as liver disease or some kidney diseases. In liver disease the bigger problem is usually reduced metabolism, because the liver is the main metabolic organ. [V34]

### Metabolism

The liver is the main site of drug metabolism because it contains the full set of metabolic enzymes and receives all blood draining the GIT. Other tissues, including the gut wall, also metabolise drugs, but the liver is the most important. The purpose of metabolism is not simply inactivation. It is to make the molecule more water-soluble so that the kidney can excrete it. In the process, an inactive prodrug can become active, an active drug can become more active, an active drug can become inactive, or a drug can become more toxic. [V35][S24]

Metabolism proceeds in two phases. Phase I reactions, mainly carried out by cytochrome P450 enzymes, introduce or expose a polar group. This increases water solubility and also creates a handle for phase II. Phase II reactions attach a polar group, making the metabolite strongly water-soluble and easy to excrete. If the metabolite is still too lipophilic after phase I, phase II completes the job. [V35]

Aspirin illustrates the sequence. Aspirin is a prodrug taken as acetylsalicylic acid. On first pass it is converted to salicylic acid, mainly in the liver and to some extent in the stomach and gut wall; this is a phase I reaction. A second enzyme then attaches a very polar molecule, converting the active metabolite into a relatively inactive water-soluble substance that the kidney can excrete. [V35]

First-pass metabolism explains three clinical choices. Glyceryl trinitrate given orally has a bioavailability below one per cent, so it is given sublingually as a spray, bypassing the liver and reaching the circulation quickly. Morphine has an oral bioavailability of about thirty per cent, so it can be given orally but is often given intravenously for one hundred per cent bioavailability. Codeine is a prodrug with about ninety per cent bioavailability; it is inactive until the liver converts it to morphine. [V35]

The liver can also activate toxicity. Ethanol is converted first to acetaldehyde, which is much more toxic than ethanol itself. This is why the liver's role cannot be reduced to “detoxification”. [V35]

### Excretion and half-life

Excretion is the removal of drug from the body, mainly by the kidney, with bile and faeces as other routes. The central concept is half-life: the time required for the concentration of drug in the body to fall by half. If eight milligrams are present at midday and the half-life is one hour, about four milligrams remain at one o'clock. [V7][S25]

Most drugs are eliminated by first-order kinetics. The amount removed per unit time is proportional to the concentration, so the calculation restarts from the remaining amount: two milligrams at two o'clock, one milligram at three o'clock. About five per cent remains after roughly four and a half half-lives. [V7]

Alcohol is the standard exception. It is eliminated by zero-order kinetics, meaning a fixed amount per unit time rather than a fixed fraction. This happens when the dose overwhelms the capacity of the metabolising enzymes. For alcohol, roughly one standard drink is cleared per hour, so we do not speak of an alcohol half-life in the same way. Alcohol also induces the enzymes that metabolise it, increasing their number, which is why a person who drinks heavily may clear alcohol differently from someone drinking for the first time in a long while. [V7]

The arithmetic is worth practising. If four milligrams are present at noon and the half-life is four hours, two milligrams remain at four o'clock and one milligram at eight o'clock. The decline is not linear in amount; it is linear in the logarithm of amount. [V7]

Omeprazole ties the four processes together. It is enteric coated so that it survives the stomach and dissolves in the small intestine. Absorption takes about three hours; about ninety-five per cent is protein bound; bioavailability is about sixty per cent. On first pass through the liver, cytochrome P450 enzymes alter it. About eighty per cent is excreted by the kidneys and twenty per cent in faeces, some of the latter after being shunted into bile canaliculi. Its half-life is about one and a half hours, yet its effect lasts up to seventy-two hours. The reason is that it irreversibly inhibits the proton pump, so the effect outlasts the drug concentration. [V22][V21][S31]


## 11. Histamine 2 blockers and acid suppression

Histamine receptors are not all the same. The H1 receptor is the one involved in allergic rhinitis, itchy eyes and the runny nose that “antihistamines” are taken for. The H2 receptor is on the parietal cell and drives acid secretion. A third subtype is also mentioned in the lessons. Because the two main subtypes sit in different organs and do different jobs, a drug selective for H2 does not relieve allergy, and an antihistamine selective for H1 does not reduce acid. [V13][V26]

The parietal cell is stimulated by gastrin, histamine and acetylcholine together, not by any one of them alone. When all three have bound their receptors, the hydrogen–potassium ATPase pumps begin secreting protons. Histamine 2 blockers are competitive antagonists at the H2 receptor: they occupy the receptor and compete with histamine. With less histamine binding, fewer protons are secreted and acidity falls. [V13][V31]

Competition sets a ceiling. Even at maximum dose, some histamine is still released and some will reach the receptor, and gastrin and acetylcholine can still drive the pump through their own receptors. The H2 blockers are therefore about eighty per cent effective, not ninety-nine per cent. Proton pump inhibitors act downstream at the pump itself and can achieve about ninety-nine per cent reduction. This is the mechanistic reason H2 blockers are not first-line for severe acid disease. [V13][V22]

Their main uses are peptic ulcer disease, GORD and conditions in which the stomach produces too much acid. Cimetidine and famotidine are both among the two hundred most prescribed medicines. [V29]

Long-term suppression of acid produces a predictable set of adverse effects, because acid has four jobs. It begins digesting food, helps absorb some nutrients, limits ingested microorganisms and supports the absorption of some drugs. Reducing it for months can therefore reduce absorption of calcium, magnesium and vitamin B12, increase GIT and respiratory infections, alter the absorption of some medicines, and increase food allergy risk as food proteins are less completely broken down. A slight risk of a faster or altered heartbeat is also described. Vitamin B12 deserves particular attention in older people, whose absorption already declines with age, because deficiency can cause a dementia-like picture; monitoring is suggested when treatment continues for months. Calcium and magnesium losses matter for bone health. [V29][S27]

Ranitidine, sold as Zantac, was the most popular H2 blocker until 2020, when some components were found to break down into carcinogenic substances. The problem worsened over time and with improper storage, such as a hot car. The manufacturer moved its brand to famotidine, marketed as Zantac 360. A patient who says they take “Zantac” should be asked which formulation they have, because leftover ranitidine may still be in the cupboard. Other H2 blockers, including famotidine and cimetidine, were tested for the same carcinogens and none were found. [S28]

| Feature | Histamine 2 blockers | Proton pump inhibitors |
|---|---|---|
| Target | H2 receptor on parietal cell | Hydrogen–potassium ATPase pump |
| Mechanism | Competitive antagonism | Irreversible enzyme inhibition |
| Maximum acid reduction | About 80 per cent | About 99 per cent |
| Duration | Shorter; depends on drug concentration | Up to 72 hours despite short half-life |
| Typical role | Mild to moderate acid suppression | First-line for peptic ulcer disease and GORD |
| Main long-term concerns | Nutrient absorption, infection, drug absorption | Same acid-related concerns plus CYP interactions |

The comparison is drawn from the histamine 2 and proton pump lessons. [V13][V29][V22]

## 12. Enzymatic inhibition and proton pump inhibitors

Enzymes are proteins that catalyse reactions. A substrate binds the enzyme's active site, a molecular interaction occurs, and the substrate is cleaved or converted into products. Without the enzyme a reaction might take hours; with it, the reaction can occur in milliseconds. Because enzymes run so much of biology, it is not surprising that many medicines inhibit them. [V11][S32]

Enzymatic inhibitors fall into two large groups. Reversible inhibitors bind, inhibit and then leave, so the enzyme recovers when the drug is cleared. Irreversible inhibitors usually form a covalent bond, normally at the active site, and inactivate the enzyme until the drug is degraded or the cell makes a new enzyme. Reversible inhibitors are the larger group and include at least four subsets; the clinically most important is competitive inhibition, in which the drug occupies the active site and competes with substrate. Adding more substrate can overcome competitive inhibition. A non-competitive inhibitor binds at a different, allosteric site and changes the active site so that substrate cannot bind. [V11]

Acetylcholinesterase is the teaching example for competitive inhibition. The enzyme breaks acetylcholine into acetate and choline, and one enzyme molecule can degrade about twenty-five thousand acetylcholine molecules per second. A competitive inhibitor occupying the active site stops that degradation for as long as it is bound. This matters at the neuromuscular junction, where acetylcholine instructs muscle to contract and must then be removed immediately so that the muscle can relax. Nerve gases are irreversible inhibitors of the same enzyme; acetylcholine accumulates, muscles contract continuously, and the person dies of respiratory distress because the diaphragm cannot relax. Penicillin is an irreversible inhibitor of an enzyme that bacteria have and humans do not, which is the basis of its selective toxicity. [V11]

Aspirin poses an apparent paradox. It irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX), yet its anti-inflammatory effect lasts only about four hours while its effect on platelets lasts the platelet's whole life of about nine days. Blood services ask about aspirin in the past ten days and will not use platelets from a recent user. The explanation is that platelets have no nucleus and therefore cannot transcribe DNA to make new COX. Other cells can degrade the drug and replace the inhibited enzyme. [V11][S32]

The proton pump is the model enzyme inhibitor. The hydrogen–potassium ATPase is a large transmembrane protein in the parietal cell that pumps hydrogen ions into the stomach lumen. It is both a pump and an enzyme: it catalyses the conversion of ATP to ADP, and the energy released drives the transport. The pump is energy-dependent because hydrogen ions are charged and cannot simply diffuse across the membrane. The sodium–potassium ATPase illustrates why such pumps matter; if it stops, sodium accumulates inside the cell, water follows osmotically, and the cell bursts. The hydrogen–potassium pump is less dangerous to inhibit because it is found in only two places in the body, mostly the parietal cell, and is not essential for survival. [V31][S33]

Proton pump inhibitors bind the pump irreversibly. Omeprazole therefore reduces acid secretion by about ninety-nine per cent. Its half-life is only about one and a half hours, but the effect lasts up to seventy-two hours because the enzyme remains inactive until it is replaced or the drug is degraded at the active site. That is why a once-daily dose is sufficient despite rapid clearance. The drug only inhibits pumps that are actively secreting at the time it is present, so it is taken on an empty stomach and food is eaten about thirty minutes to an hour later to activate the pumps while the drug is available. [V22][V31][V21]

Omeprazole is used for GORD, peptic ulcer disease and states of excessive acid production, and it is first-line for peptic ulcer disease and GORD. It is on the World Health Organization's list of essential medicines and is available over the counter in some countries. Common side effects are nausea, vomiting and increased intestinal gas. Less common effects include increased GIT infections and pneumonia, because acid is a protective barrier against ingested and proliferating organisms, and long-term use is associated with increased bone fracture risk through reduced calcium and magnesium absorption. [V17][S35]

The indications carry their own safety issues. A patient may say they have GORD when they actually have Barrett's oesophagus, a pre-cancerous change of the oesophageal lining. Stopping acid suppression in that situation allows acid to keep splashing on dysplastic cells and can permit progression to oesophageal cancer. A peptic ulcer may erode into an artery and bleed into the stomach. For both reasons, a patient should not be advised to stop a proton pump inhibitor merely because the stated diagnosis sounds benign; the full history belongs to the prescriber. [V17][V9]

Pregnancy categories are a separate safety layer. In the Australian system described in the lessons, category A is the safest, with no expected adverse fetal effects, while category D is potentially harmful enough that a woman of childbearing age may need to stop the drug even if she is not planning a pregnancy; one lesson groups D and E together, while another calls D the worst category and X definitely teratogenic. Omeprazole is described as pregnancy category B3 in the module document and as category B in Australia and C in the United States in the video, which is a reminder that classification systems differ between countries and that the local system must be used. [S35][V17][V9]

Omeprazole also inhibits liver enzymes, specifically CYP2C19 and CYP2C8. That means it can raise or lower the concentration of other drugs metabolised by those enzymes. The clinical rule is not to memorise every pair but to recognise a CYP interaction and check a drug reference. Grapefruit juice is the food example: it strongly inhibits CYP3A4, which metabolises more than half of marketed medicines, and even a two-hundred-millilitre glass or one fruit can produce a clinically important effect. Inhibition can increase or decrease a drug's action depending on whether the enzyme was activating a prodrug or inactivating an active drug. Some drugs are inducers rather than inhibitors; they instruct liver cells to produce more enzyme, which lowers the concentration of other drugs. [V43][S34]

The COX example shows why enzyme inhibition can produce off-target harm. NSAIDs and aspirin inhibit COX to reduce inflammation, but COX also has protective roles in the stomach and kidney. Long-term use removes that protection and contributes to stomach ulcers and kidney damage. Omeprazole avoids that particular problem because its target enzyme is almost exclusive to the parietal cell, which is why it has a relatively favourable adverse-effect profile. [V43]

| Feature | Reversible inhibition | Irreversible inhibition |
|---|---|---|
| Bond | Usually non-covalent | Usually covalent |
| Duration | Until drug clears | Until enzyme replaced or drug degraded |
| Substrate competition | Can overcome competitive inhibition | Cannot be overcome |
| Example | Competitive acetylcholinesterase inhibitor | Omeprazole at the proton pump; aspirin at COX |
| Clinical consequence | Predictable from drug concentration | Effect outlasts half-life |

The contrast explains why omeprazole works for a day or more from a short-lived dose. [V11][V31][V9]

## 13. Neurotransmission and antiemetics

Vomiting is controlled in the brainstem, specifically the medulla oblongata. The stomach is often the source of the signal, but it is not the control centre. Four neural pathways converge on the vomiting centre, and each uses a different set of neurotransmitters. This is why one antiemetic does not work for every kind of vomiting. [V42][V12]

The four pathways are: from the GIT to the vomiting centre, used when the gut is damaged or irritated; from the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) to the vomiting centre, used when a toxin or chemical imbalance is detected; from the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, used in motion sickness; and from the cerebral cortex, used in emotional or higher-brain vomiting. The CTZ sits next to the vomiting centre but outside the blood–brain barrier, which allows it to sample blood-borne chemicals. [V12][V2]

| Pathway | Trigger | Main neurotransmitters | Preferred drug group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cerebral cortex | Emotion, anxiety, cortical damage | Poorly specified in the module | Benzodiazepines |
| Vestibular apparatus | Motion, inner-ear disturbance | Histamine 1, acetylcholine | Antihistamines that enter the brain; anticholinergics |
| GIT | Mucosal damage, irritation, chemotherapy | Serotonin 3 (5-HT3), dopamine 2 | 5-HT3 antagonists; dopamine 2 antagonists |
| Chemoreceptor trigger zone | Toxins, glucose, electrolyte or pH imbalance | Serotonin 3, dopamine 2 | 5-HT3 antagonists; dopamine 2 antagonists |

The pathway–transmitter–drug mapping is the organising scheme of the chapter. [V12][V10][V2]

Safety comes before drug selection. Repeated vomiting removes hydrogen ions, so the body can become too alkaline; the result is metabolic alkalosis, alongside dehydration and electrolyte loss. Those imbalances can produce neurological symptoms and deteriorate quickly. The cause also matters. Intestinal obstruction, appendicitis, gallstones and perforated bowel all cause vomiting and none will resolve with an antiemetic. Beyond the gut, raised glucose in a child with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes is sensed at the CTZ and can cause vomiting as the first symptom; ketones give the breath and urine a sweet, fruity smell. Kidney failure toxins, pH abnormalities and thyroid disease can also present this way. A teenager who vomits after breakfast for two days needs a blood test, not a stomach remedy. [V19][S38][S43]

To understand how antiemetics work, picture neurotransmission. An electrical impulse travels to the axon terminal, where vesicles fuse with the membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The transmitter diffuses across the narrow gap and binds receptors on the next neuron. An excitatory transmitter makes the next cell more likely to depolarise; an inhibitory transmitter makes it less likely. The strength of the signal is coded by the frequency of impulses rather than by the size of each impulse. The transmitter must then be removed almost immediately, either by an enzyme in the cleft or by a reuptake pump, so that the next signal can be distinguished from the last. [V14][S42]

A drug can interfere at any of those steps. It can block the receptor (antagonist), mimic the transmitter (agonist), inhibit the degrading enzyme, or block the reuptake pump. The antiemetics mostly belong to the first category: they are receptor antagonists for the transmitter driving that particular pathway. Their side effects usually come from blocking the same receptor in a place where it is not wanted. [V14][S42]

### Motion sickness and the vestibular pathway

The vestibular pathway uses histamine 1 and acetylcholine. A drug must therefore block at least one of those receptors, and it must cross into the brain to reach the pathway. The most effective over-the-counter options are antihistamines that enter the central nervous system, such as diphenhydramine and dimenhydrinate, and the anticholinergic hyoscine, also called scopolamine. [V12][S39]

Antihistamines that cross the blood–brain barrier cause drowsiness and uncoordination because histamine is involved in maintaining awareness in the brain. They also have some anticholinergic action. Anticholinergics block the parasympathetic “rest and digest” system, so they reduce GIT movement and secretions, including saliva, and dry mouth is a common side effect. This makes hyoscine a logical choice for a traveller who has both motion sickness and gut spasm. [V12]

Selectivity explains why loratadine is different. Loratadine is an antihistamine that stays largely outside the brain, so it relieves allergy without causing drowsiness, but it also has no effect on motion sickness. H1 receptors are not used in the other emetic pathways, so antihistamines are of little help when vomiting arises from the gut, the CTZ or the cortex. [V12]

The fishing-trip scenario makes the matching concrete. Joel has severe hay fever, is skippering the boat and never gets seasick; he should take loratadine, because a sedating antihistamine would impair the skipper. Alma has gut spasm and loose stools and occasionally gets motion sickness; hyoscine addresses both the vestibular pathway and the gut overactivity. Vivon gets motion sickness easily but has no other problem and is not skippering; diphenhydramine about an hour before departure is appropriate, with the expectation of some drowsiness. [V12]

### Chemotherapy and GIT pathways

The GIT and CTZ pathways share serotonin 3, written 5-HT3, at multiple critical points. A 5-HT3 antagonist is therefore a powerful antiemetic, and ondansetron, dolasetron and the other “-setron” drugs are especially valuable for chemotherapy-induced vomiting. Because 5-HT3 is used in few places outside these pathways, the drugs have relatively few side effects. Their main drawback is cost. [V10][S45]

Dopamine 2 is the other shared transmitter. Dopamine 2 antagonists are effective for vomiting caused by GIT irritation and, to some extent, by CTZ stimulation. Prochlorperazine and metoclopramide are the examples. They are inexpensive but produce more side effects than the 5-HT3 antagonists, because dopamine 2 is used throughout the brain and most antipsychotic drugs are dopamine antagonists. The involuntary movement disorders associated with dopamine blockade are the main concern. [V10]

The chemotherapy scenario ties the logic together. James is receiving chemotherapy, which damages the stomach and generates GIT signals, while the drugs are also sensed at the CTZ. Bismuth subsalicylate acts locally, reducing inflammation and acid, and may soothe the stomach but will not stop vomiting. Diphenhydramine treats inner-ear vomiting and will not help. Hyoscine helps motion sickness and gut spasm, not chemotherapy. Lorazepam is a benzodiazepine and is only effective for cortical emesis; it is sometimes used as an adjunct but not alone. Metoclopramide may be tried first at a fairly high dose because it is inexpensive, but the patient may not tolerate the movement side effects. Ondansetron is the most effective and best tolerated choice because 5-HT3 is central to both the GIT and CTZ pathways. [V10]

Bismuth subsalicylate deserves a separate note. It is not an antiemetic in the receptor sense: it does not act on the neural pathways. It acts directly on the gut, with local anti-inflammatory, acid-reducing and antibacterial effects, and can calm mild gastric irritation. It is not sold in Australia, so the module uses it only for awareness; it is widely available elsewhere and heavily used by people with frequent stomach irritation. [V42][S41][S37]

Benzodiazepines are the cortical option. The cortex-to-vomiting-centre pathway is involved in emotional vomiting, and the benzodiazepines ending in -azepam or -azolam are the drugs that act there. Five of the two hundred most prescribed medicines belong to that class. [V12]

One explains the learning strategy behind the quizzes; the other reviews the vomiting centre, the four input pathways and the transmitter-to-drug matching, including antihistamines and anticholinergics for motion sickness and ondansetron for chemotherapy. [V1][V2]

## Key takeaways

1. Antacids neutralise existing acid and are indicated only for occasional heartburn; chronic reflux is GORD and needs assessment, because dysplasia can progress to oesophageal cancer. [S3][V25]
2. Magnesium antacids cause diarrhoea through osmotically active magnesium chloride; aluminium antacids cause constipation and bind drugs; sodium bicarbonate adds salt; calcium carbonate causes the strongest rebound. [V44]
3. Rebound hyperacidity is homeostasis in action: lowering acid triggers a compensatory rise, and the same pattern explains diuretic tolerance. [V38]
4. Constipation is defined by the Rome criteria, not by a feeling, and laxatives come after water, fibre and psyllium. [V28][V15]
5. Osmotic laxatives show a linear dose response and are contraindicated in obstruction; most drugs, including paracetamol, do not behave this way. [V33]
6. Stimulant laxatives are for occasional use or bowel preparation and can cause rebound constipation, permanent colonic damage and electrolyte loss. [V24]
7. Mineral oil plus a detergent softener is strictly contraindicated because absorbed oil cannot be excreted and provokes a granuloma. [V40]
8. Receptors convert ligand binding into a cellular response; affinity is binding strength, potency is dose required, and selectivity is discrimination between receptors. [V18]
9. Loperamide is an opioid agonist that stays in the gut, so it slows peristalsis without analgesia, euphoria or respiratory depression at normal doses; high doses can cause obstruction and arrhythmia. [V41][V37]
10. Pharmacokinetics is ADME; first-pass metabolism, protein binding and half-life explain many clinical decisions, and alcohol is the classic zero-order exception. [V35][V34][V7]
11. Histamine 2 blockers are competitive and reach about eighty per cent acid reduction; proton pump inhibitors inhibit the pump irreversibly and reach about ninety-nine per cent, lasting up to seventy-two hours. [V13][V22]
12. Antiemetic choice follows the pathway: antihistamines or anticholinergics for vestibular vomiting, 5-HT3 antagonists for chemotherapy and GIT/CTZ vomiting, dopamine 2 antagonists for GIT irritation, benzodiazepines for cortical vomiting. [V12][V10]

## Common confusions

**Antacid, H2 blocker and proton pump inhibitor are not interchangeable.** An antacid neutralises acid that is already there. An H2 blocker reduces histamine-driven production at the receptor. A proton pump inhibitor stops the final pump. The three differ in speed, degree and duration, and only the last two are used for chronic acid disease. [V25][V13][V31]

**Affinity, potency and selectivity are three different measurements.** Affinity is how tightly a drug binds. Potency is how much drug is needed for an effect. Selectivity is how well the drug avoids other receptors. A drug can bind very tightly and still be non-selective, and a drug can be selective without being potent. [V18]

**Pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics answer opposite questions.** Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug. Receptor binding belongs to pharmacodynamics; absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion belong to pharmacokinetics. [V8]

**First-order and zero-order elimination are not the same arithmetic.** In first-order elimination a constant fraction is removed per half-life, so the amount falls 8, 4, 2, 1. In zero-order elimination a constant amount is removed per hour, as with alcohol, because the enzymes are saturated. [V7]

**Generic and brand names carry different information.** The brand is a commercial label and there can be hundreds; the generic name is the stable label on the dispenser and often contains the class suffix. Learn the generic first. [V20]

**“Contraindicated” is weaker than “strictly contraindicated”.** Contraindicated means avoid except in defined circumstances; strictly contraindicated means never combine, as with mineral oil and docusate. [V40]

**Osmotic and stimulant laxatives work by different physics.** Osmotic laxatives pull water into the lumen and show a linear dose response. Stimulant laxatives irritate the gut and increase motility and secretion, with rebound and structural damage as the price. [V33][V24]

**H1 and H2 receptors are not interchangeable.** “Antihistamine” conventionally means an H1 blocker for allergy; H2 blockers reduce acid. A patient taking an H2 blocker will not get allergy relief, and an H1 antihistamine will not heal reflux. [V26]
