Module overview: The endocrine system
The endocrine system is essentially a communication system. It depends on feedback loops of the hormones released by internal glands directly into the circulatory system that regulate distant target organs. But what happens when that communication system breaks down? This module answers that question.
This entire module contains a total of 70 minutes of video lessons plus activities that reinforce your knowledge and prepare you for the quiz.

                              ADH (vasopressin)
      Too little ADH, the water saver results in a
       condition termed diabetes insipidus.
      “Diabetes” comes Ancient Greek διαβήτης
       (diabētēs) which literally means “a passer
       through; a siphon”
      "Insipidus" comes from the Latin: in- "not" +
       sapidus "tasty" from sapere "have a taste“
      Diabetes insipidus is a disease with
       tasteless urine (in contrast to having sweet
       urine in diabetes mellitus.) Patients get
       dehydrated because they cannot save water.

                                  Oxytocin
         Derived from the Greek words meaning ‘swift birth’
         The "bonding hormone“, the “love hormone”
         Used to initiate labour, control postpartum haemorrhage,
          (promote breast milk production - no longer in humans)
         Relatively safe when used at recommended doses, and side
          effects are uncommon
         Has a half-life of about three minutes when given
          intravenously. Intranasal oxytocin has a duration of at least
          2.25 hours
         Replaces the ergot alkaloid ergometrine as the first choice in
          the uses above, but combinations are still used

Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156105_1/document/_7758236_1?view=content&state=view
Vasopressin - The
antidiuretic hormone
The material in this lesson will not be assessed in PHAR2001.
Human vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH) is a hormone synthesized from the hypothalamus. It then travels down the axon terminating in the posterior pituitary, and is released into the circulation in response to hyperosmolality (equivalent to dehydration).
ADH is the water saver, and (its first metabolite) saves the water at its last chance of being reabsorbed at the level of the kidneys just before it is excreted into the urinary bladder. As you would expect, as ADH saves water, it has the effect of increasing the blood pressure.
Diabetes insipidus
is a disorder characterised by a lack of ADH and while it is relatively uncommon, it is important to understand ADH to understand desmopressin (the treatment for diabetes insipidus) and to understand many drugs, because there are several that affect ADH. For instance, caffeine decreases ADH release and ethanol (drinking alcohol) decreases its production, both leading to their 'diuretic' effects. So let's find out more about this important hormone in this lesson.

The thyroid axis begins at the hypothalamus secretion of Thyroid releasing hormone (TRH), which incites the pituitary to release Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), which incites the thyroid gland to release free T4.

The T4 (4 molecules of iodine) released from the thyroid gland is LESS potent than the T3 (3 molecules of iodine) that has been metabolised in the liver

The main stimulus for the release of TRH from the hypothalamus is exposure to cold. In turn, thyroid hormone increases the metabolism in nearly every cell in the body, and increases the sodium-potassium ATPase pumps throughout the body, increasing the body’s heat.

One of the actions of the thyroid hormone is to increase the effects of adrenaline and noradrenaline (the catecholamines)

The thyroid gland is made to sequester the iodine from the bloodstream, and if it is not receiving adequate supply, a type of goitre or enlargement of the gland will occur.

Since the thyroid hormone uses iodine in its structure, supplementation of iodine will increase the amount of thyroid hormone produced.

 are steroidal hormones secreted by the adrenal cortex. They include:
Glucocorticoids 
        and        Mineralocorticoids
The glucocorticoids include cortisol, the major stress hormone
‘Glucocorticoids’ refers to cortisol and similar
‘Cortisol’ is the most abundant glucocorticoid
Prednisone and prednisolone are medications that act like cortisol 
Taken to decrease the immune system response (asthma, autoimmune disorders, organ transplant…)
Anabolic steroids are testosterone and its ‘analogues’ (similar in structure)
A person would not say they are on anabolic steroids
What is meant when a person says “I have had to be on steroids”?

Teriparatide is an anabolic drug, meaning that it “builds up” and in this case, it builds bone.

Teriparatide is a recumbent form of parathyroid hormone (PTH), so it acts like PTH, activating osteoblasts more than osteoclasts.

Because teriparatide acts like PTH, it decreases renal excretion of calcium by the kidneys, and increases absorption of calcium in the gut, potentially leading to hypercalcaemia, so calcium levels should be monitored.

Teriparatide is an oral medication

Teriparatide is used off label for speeding up bone fractures, especially in athletes.

There is a slight increased risk of developing bone cancer (osteosarcoma) with use of teriparatide.
