# PHAR2001 — Module 5: Analgesics and psychotropic medicines / 镇痛药与精神神经药物

AI-assisted revision synthesis of archived course material; not a verbatim lecture transcript, clinical protocol or assessment submission. Check the original readings and current unit outline. Optional material is retained; availability does not imply it is assessable.

**Medication safety:** this is educational material, not prescribing advice. Verify current authoritative medicines information before clinical use. Some source pages contain older assessment percentages or legacy statements; they are preserved rather than silently corrected.

## Pain and inflammation

Distinguish acute protective pain, chronic pain and neuropathic pain. Explain where inflammation and sensitisation enter the pathway. Combination therapy should be reasoned through mechanisms and safety rather than assuming two products always work better together.

## Non-opioid analgesia

Compare NSAID/COX-related effects with paracetamol’s role and limitations. Do not assume antipyretic or analgesic activity means equivalent anti-inflammatory activity. The module explicitly highlights the different safety context of children and older adults.

## Opioids

Link receptor action to analgesia and adverse effects, including sedation and respiratory effects. Separate acute-pain uses from the different challenges of chronic and neuropathic pain. The specific-opioid extension is marked non-compulsory in the source.

## Psychotropic and neurodegenerative conditions

The chapter includes Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, anxiety and depression. Learn the disorder/pathway before comparing medicines. Benzodiazepines and alternatives differ in intended effects and dependence-related concerns; avoid treating all sedating medicines as equivalent.

## Antidepressants and optional topics

Compare SSRIs, TCAs, SNRIs and atypical antidepressants by neurotransmitter targets and safety considerations. The source marks psychosis, specific antipsychotics and antiseizure lessons as non-compulsory; all are retained for reference.

## Revision checks

Explain why paracetamol and an NSAID are not identical; map an opioid effect to a safety concern; compare an SSRI and an SNRI; identify how age, other medicines and organ function can alter risk. Consult an authoritative current medicines reference before any clinical use.

## Mechanism and safety comparison

| Group/topic | Main study distinction | Safety focus |
|---|---|---|
| NSAIDs and paracetamol | Analgesic and antipyretic effects do not imply identical anti-inflammatory action | Check duplicate ingredients, organ-related risk and the harm of exceeding directions; poor pain relief is not a reason for uncontrolled dose escalation |
| Opioids | Receptor-mediated analgesia is accompanied by effects in other tissues | Link sedation, respiratory depression and bowel effects to action; consider cumulative effects with other sedating medicines |
| Parkinson’s / Alzheimer’s | The disorders affect different neural pathways and require different explanations | Distinguish symptom treatment from a claim to cure or halt disease; preserve dated source statements without presenting them as a current treatment review |
| Benzodiazepines and alternatives | Half-life and purpose differ across agents; buspirone is not simply another benzodiazepine | Consider accumulation, dependence and sedation. The lecturer explicitly corrects an overbroad scheduling statement in the original page |
| Antidepressants | SSRIs chiefly inhibit serotonin reuptake; SNRIs involve serotonin and noradrenaline; TCAs and atypical drugs have additional differences | Compare targets, onset expectations, interactions and adverse effects using the original materials rather than assuming every antidepressant is interchangeable |
| Psychosis and seizures | Additional receptor/neuronal mechanisms extend the chapter | Psychosis, specific antipsychotics and seizure lessons are retained as optional source material |

## Clinical-reasoning revision

Explain why an older person taking several sedating products may need a different safety assessment from a younger person taking one. Compare a medicine’s intended symptom benefit with its burden, and distinguish acute pain, inflammatory pain and neuropathic pain. These are reasoning exercises, not individual prescribing recommendations.
