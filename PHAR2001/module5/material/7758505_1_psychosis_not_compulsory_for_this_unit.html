Introduction to antipsychotics

The material in this lesson is not compulsory in PHAR2001.  

Treatment of psychosis is confusing but it is easiest to start out by understanding the dopamine pathways because treatment involves decreasing the dopamine in the brain. In the diagram below, the blue arrows depict the dopamine pathways. The mesolimbic dopamine pathway is the one that is therapeutic. People with schizophrenia have a hyperactive limbic system, and when we give dopamine antagonists, the positive symptoms (like delusions, disorganised speech and/or thought, distorted perceptions and emotions, hallucinations and the belief that one is under the control of an external agency). That is good, and that is what we want from a medication that treats psychosis.

Most of our antipsychotic medications involve decreasing dopamine in the brain, and that is good because as we decrease the dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway, the positive symptoms such as delusions are lessened. But the problem is that there are three other main pathways that use dopamine, and decreasing those pathways results in the side effects of antipsychotic medications. The mesocortical pathway, the nigrostriatal pathway, and the tuberoinfundibular pathway are all affected. Drag the pathway to the proper box.
