                                Alzheimer’s disease
 • Alzheimer’s disease is a form of dementia
   characterised by profound brain shrinkage
     • Progressive deterioration of intellectual and cognitive
       functioning.
 • Two observations that have led to the current
   therapy
     • Acetylcholine is reduced early in the course of the
       disease
     • Glutamate levels have been found to be raised
 • The acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are used to
   enhance cholinergic transmission, while the use
   of the glutamate receptor antagonist memantine
   reduces the excitotoxic effects of glutamate.

     Memantine blocks glutamate at the NMDA receptor
                 • The brain is very vulnerable to glutamate-
                   mediated overexcitation; this results in
                   excitotoxicity.
                 • Ultimately, with this overexcitation, cell integrity
                   is disrupted and the nerve cell dies.
                 • Part of the process of Alzheimer's disease may
                   have to do with this excitotoxicity from excess
                   glutamate.
                 • The psychotropic agent memantine is a selective
                   NMDA receptor antagonist.
                      • This decreases calcium influx and decreases excitation

                          Glutamate receptor antagonists
                 Memantine is indicated in the management of
                 moderate to severe Alzheimer’s disease.
                 • Mechanism of action
                      • Memantine is an NMDA receptor antagonist that
                        selectively restores the ungated receptors to the gated
                        state and inhibits prolonged calcium ion influx without
                        affecting normal glutamate neurotransmission.
                 • Common adverse effects
                      • Agitation, insomnia, drowsiness, dizziness, headache,
                        gastrointestinal disturbances and coughing.

                        Anxiolytics and hypnotics

 • Anxiolytics, minor tranquiliser and sedative are often used
   interchangeably and are described as a substance that diminishes
   the activity of the CNS. Sedatives calm a person receiving therapy,
   and moderate excitability.
 • ‘Hypnotic’ is the term used to describe a substance that induces
   sleep
     • Often difficult to distinguish
 • Many of the medicines in these categories can induce
   psychological dependence

                   Atypical antidepressants
     *Bupropion* and *Trazodone* are atypical antidepressants.
     Bupropion is a weak noradrenaline and dopamine inhibitor
     • Does not cause sexual dysfunction, weight gain or sleepiness
     • Can be used as an add-on to SSRIs
     • Is effective for reducing nicotine cravings
     Trazodone blocks the reuptake of serotonin but it also blocks the
     postsynaptic serotonin receptors of subtype 2 A which are thought to
     contribute to depression.
     • Indicated for depression, alcohol withdrawals and anxiety disorders
     • Doesn’t have anticholinergic effects

          • Schizophrenia is relatively common, affecting 1

          • Negative symptoms
             • toneless speech, vacant eyes, social withdrawal,
               anhedonia (lack of enjoyment) and apathy.

        Adverse effects of Haloperidol, Chlorpromazine and
            other first generation antipsychotic agents

     • Worsening of the negative symptoms (lack of motivation, diminished
       energy, inability to express emotions, social withdrawal)
     • Parkinsonian symptoms
     • Excess prolactin (milk production, breast development in males and
       sexual dysfunction)


     • Long term effects include the opposite of parkinsonian symptoms, that
       being tardive dyskinesia (smacking of lips, etc)

               Second generation antipsychotics
     Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics
     • *Quetiapine* (Seroquel), *Aripiprazole* (Abilify),
       *Risperidone* (Risperdal), and *Olanzapine* (Zyperxa)
     • The most important difference between the first and second
       generation antipsychotics is the tendency of the latter to
       induce fewer extrapyramidal disturbances.
     • Differences in their affinities for various central receptors may
       explain why the second generation antipsychotics are less
       prone to producing these adverse effects.

                   First generation vs second generation
                           antipsychotics: review
      • Antipsychotic agents can be classified as either typical (first
        generation) or atypical agents (second generation).
      • Typical are much more active on the D2 receptors than are the
        atypical ones.
      • The atypical agents, have less affinity for the D2 receptors, usually
        have a lower incidence of debilitating motor disturbances

        The goal is to find the right antipsychotic agent for the person: an
       agent that addresses the most disturbing symptoms without the side
        effects that may prompt the person to stop taking the medication.
