## A mechanism-first study method

Start with normal physiology, identify what changes in the disorder, then explain where the medicine acts. For each class connect mechanism to intended benefit, adverse effects, contraindications, interactions and monitoring. This module uses gastrointestinal medicines to build the foundations for later systems.

## Chemical and direct action

Antacids neutralise acid; laxatives illustrate actions that need not depend on a cellular receptor. The lessons distinguish magnesium and aluminium antacids and several laxative mechanisms. Relate the choice to the person’s bowel pattern, symptoms and safety context rather than treating all OTC agents as harmless.

## Pharmacodynamics

Explain receptors, agonists and antagonists and how drug–receptor action produces a response. Link selectivity and dose to intended and unintended effects. Use the chapter’s examples to reason from receptor action rather than memorising independent lists.

## Pharmacokinetics: ADME

Absorption determines entry, distribution movement between compartments, metabolism chemical modification and excretion elimination. Route, formulation, first-pass effects and organ function can change exposure. Distinguish the drug’s effect on the body from the body’s handling of the drug.

## Enzymes and neurotransmitters

Explain how inhibiting an enzyme differs from directly stimulating a receptor. For neurotransmitter-related gastrointestinal medicines and antiemetics, identify the relevant pathway and expected consequences. Both condensed assessable and full optional chapter branches are retained in the archive.

## Revision checks

Compare an antacid with a receptor-acting medicine; explain why a laxative may be inappropriate without assessing constipation; trace one oral dose through ADME; explain a drug interaction in terms of mechanism. ## Chapter-by-chapter comparison

| Chapter | Core mechanism or distinction | Safety and revision focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Antacids and laxatives | Neutralising gastric acid is a chemical action; laxatives may change water content or motility. Magnesium and aluminium antacids have different bowel effects. | Assess the symptom and its cause before recommending an OTC product. Chronic reflux, unexplained constipation or persistent symptoms need assessment rather than repeated symptom suppression. |
| 2 — Receptors and loperamide | Affinity describes binding; potency concerns the dose/concentration needed for an effect; selectivity concerns preference for a target. An agonist activates a receptor, an antagonist blocks agonist action, and an inverse agonist reduces constitutive activity. | Greater potency does not automatically mean a greater maximum benefit or better safety. The loperamide example illustrates why low usual systemic exposure does not make excessive doses safe. |
| 3 — Pharmacokinetics and H2 blockers | ADME describes exposure. Distribution includes protein binding; metabolism may activate a prodrug or inactivate an active drug. H2 blockade reduces histamine-mediated gastric acid secretion. | Ask how route, formulation, interactions and liver/kidney function change exposure. The source explicitly flags historical ranitidine content; do not use an old drug list as a current availability guide. |
| 4 — Enzyme inhibition and omeprazole | A proton-pump inhibitor acts on H+/K+-ATPase. The duration of an enzyme-related effect need not equal the plasma half-life. | Enzyme inhibition and induction can alter other medicines; effects on a prodrug may differ from effects on an already active drug. Do not infer that every NSAID has aspirin’s irreversible platelet action. |
| 5 — Neurotransmitters and antiemetics | Different pathways to the vomiting centre use different receptors; motion sickness and chemotherapy-related emesis are not the same pharmacological problem. | Investigate the cause and dehydration risk before choosing a symptom treatment. Preserve the distinction between the condensed required branch and the optional full chapter. |

## ADME reasoning exercise

Trace one oral medicine through the gut, circulation, liver and kidneys. At each stage ask: what fraction reaches its target; what is free versus protein-bound; is an active metabolite produced; and which route clears it? A half-life describes a fall in concentration under specified conditions, not a universal interval after which every drug stops working. Never transfer the source’s osmotic-laxative dose-response example into a rule that increasing any drug’s dose gives proportionally more benefit.
