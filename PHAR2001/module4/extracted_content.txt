Module overview
This entire module contains a total of 2.5 hours of video lessons plus activities that reinforce your knowledge.
Introduction to chapter 1
Hyperlipidaemia is the silent killer. In this chapter, we learn about how they contribute to the disease process (atherosclerosis) that leads to myocardial infarction, stroke, and loss of limbs, as well as our medications that can help prevent its development.
The objectives for this chapter include"
Define the terms hyperlipidaemia, dyslipidaemia, HDL and LDL
Describe atherosclerosis, including the risk factors, and potential consequences of plaques on the arteries
Identify and describe the therapeutic actions and major side effects of common pharmacological agents used in the treatment of dyslipidaemia or hyperlipidaemia.

                 Nicotinic acid
     Vitamin B3; (Niaspan)
        • Huge dose of nicotinic acid (1-3 grams daily)

32




        Special notes about lipid lowering drugs
       • Always ‘monitor the liver’ when giving hypercholesterolaemic drugs
          • How? The Liver Function Test (LFT, specific enzymes AST, ALT)

       • Most combinations of lipid lowering drugs are contraindicated
          • Exception to the rule: Ezetimibe can be combined with statins

       • None of the lipid normalizing drugs are ideal during pregnancy.
          • At one time statins were thought to be teratogenic but are now
            listed as pregnancy category D
          • In addition, babies need cholesterol !!!!!!!!!!
          • Dietary approaches are more suitable than drugs during pregnancy
            if that is possible (those with familial hypercholesterolemia do not
            respond to lifestyle changes very well).

Introduction to lipids
Not all lipids are ‘bad’. They perform many functions both within the body, as they act as an energy reserve, regulate hormones, transmit nerve impulses, cushion vital organs, and transport fat-soluble nutrients. They are also important in the structure of cell membranes and hormones. This lesson helps you understand more about when it may be time to treat the lipid levels in the body.

Nicotinic acid is vitamin B3 and as such, it is completely safe during pregnancy.

Nicotinic acid lowers triglycerides by up to 25%, decreases LDL, the bad transport protein by up to 30% and raises the good protein, the HDL by up to 30% but not all individuals respond to the medication.

Large amounts of nicotinic acid that are effective in normalising lipid levels cause the small blood vessels under the skin to dilate, leading to flushing, which is especially prominent in the face and ears.

If flushing is a problem, nicotinic acid is available in extended-release formulations which distribute the drug over time. Other options include titrating the dose, starting at 250 to 500mg/d, and increasing by the same amount every 2 to 4 weeks, until therapeutic dose is achieved. Aspirin 30 minutes prior to nicotinic acid administration is also effective, as the flushing is caused by a prostaglandin and aspirin decreases prostaglandin synthesis.

Nicotinic acid (in doses effective for normalising lipid levels) should never be used in diabetics, people with gout or stomach ulcers, or in people with liver disease

Nicotinic acid should never be taken with the statins or fibrates because of the increased risk of developing rhabdomyolysis. First signs of rhabdomyolysis include muscle pain and tea coloured urine.

Dyslipidaemia and HDL
Recall that we use the term dyslipidaemia for individuals who have low levels of HDL, the good protein, because that is a risk factor for developing atherosclerosis. HDL is the ‘bus’ that carries cholesterol from the tissues back to the liver, so a low amount of the HDL will yield high amounts near the blood vessels. ## ppt/slides/slide20.xml
Antiplatelet drugs: Aspirin
PGI2 from intact epithelium and TXA2 from platelets normally balance to form ideal haemostasis
This balance is disrupted when the intact epithelium is covered in atherosclerotic plaques, making the pro-platelet TXA2 dominate
The acetyl group in aspirin non competitively inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX), needed to form TXA2. 
Thromboxane (TXA2) is therefore not able to be made for the entire 9 day life of the platelet.
Balance is restored
Intact endothelium contains PGI2; prostacyclin but the endothelial cells can synthesise more enzyme while platelets cannot

## ppt/slides/slide21.xml
Aspirin as an antiplatelet drug
Low dose aspirin (75-150 mg/day) is proven effective in reducing MI and strokes from atherosclerosis
Adverse effects
Stomach ache, stomach ulcers, allergies
Q: I am healthy, and my arteries should be clean, why don’t I bleed readily when I take aspirin?
A: Aspirin doesn’t destroy platelets, it takes away one of their ‘tools’. Also, not all platelets are affected. 

## ppt/slides/slide22.xml
Clopidogrel
Clopidogrel is emerging as the preferred antiplatelet medication 
Decreases risk of heart attack and stroke
Its effect starts about two hours after intake and lasts for five days.
Common side effects include headache, nausea, easy bruising, itching, and heartburn.
Rarely causes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura.
Works by irreversibly inhibiting a receptor called P2Y12 for ADP on platelets.

Anticoagulants
Anticoagulants, commonly known as blood thinners, but do not affect the viscosity of blood

## ppt/slides/slide27.xml
Drugs that affect coagulation
Warfarin
Taken orally, absorbed quickly
Takes several days to exert its effect (there is still active vitamin K hanging around)
Affects several clotting factors
Warfarin inhibits epoxide reductase, an enzyme that recycles vitamin K back to its active form
Without active vitamin K, many coagulation factors are missing, resulting in decreased coagulation and if excessive, bleeding

Allergy, bruising, excessive bleeding
WARFARIN IS TEROTAGENIC  
(contraindicated in women of child bearing age)

From haemostasis to thrombosis: mechanisms and consequences
Haemostasis is the process of stopping bleeding. We use it every day to repair small bleeds, and it is vital to our survival. But our lifestyle has lead to us being predisposed to this process occurring in absence of a break in the blood vessel walls. In that case, a thrombus develops. Thrombi can occlude the site in which it develops or they can break off from the original site of formation to a blood vessel downstream. In order to combat the development of thrombi, we target the same process in which they develop, the platelet plug, the clotting cascade and the natural dissolving of the clot. Engage in this lesson to understand why our medications are effective.

The role of platelets in haemostasis and drug targeting
Platelets are invaluable in the process of haemostasis, and as such, most of our antiplatelet drugs will not disable the platelet altogether, but rather just take away one of the platelet's 'tools'. In order to understand our antiplatelet drugs, the most important thing to remember is that platelets are not cells, and as such, they do not contain the DNA to synthesise more 'tools'. So when we take away one of the platelet's tools that allows for optimal aggregation, that 'tool' will be disabled for the entire life of the platelet, which is 9 days.

The role of diuretics in heart failure and hypertension
The main indications for the use of diuretics are congestive heart failure and hypertension. In CHF, there is additional fluid being forced into the interstitial spaces due to increased back pressure. Relieving some of that fluid is beneficial as the heart can work more efficiently. In hypertension, recall that blood pressure is partly dependent on the amount of fluid, and as such, a reduction of fluid is likely to reduce BP - not dramatically, but reductions of 6-12 mmHg are common.
