Spironolactone and aldosterone receptor blockade

The material in this lesson will not be assessed in PHAR2001.  

Aldosterone is a mineralocorticoid that is released from the adrenal cortex and is nicknamed the salt saver. It saves salt by binding to the cells in the distal convoluted tubule.

Spironolactone binds to the aldosterone receptors and blocks those receptors, preventing the salt saving actions. It also has antiandrogenic actions, blocking some of the testosterone receptors. 

  • Although spironolactone is not as effective in reducing deaths in those individuals with CHF as compared to Loop diuretics, it is still highly rated for that.
  • For hypertension, there are normally better diuretics, but in the subset of hypertensive individuals who have excess aldosterone (1% of the population), the medication is very effective.

Potassium sparing diuretics include spironolactone and amiloride.

The potassium sparing diuretic spironolactone blocks the mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) receptor at the kidney, leading to its diuretic actions

The potassium sparing diuretic spironolactone has antiandrogenic (anti-testosterone) effects leading to its efficacy in delaying early puberty in boys, decreasing acne, decreasing hair growth in women and as a part of transgender therapy in transfeminine people.

Breast development in males, erectile dysfunction, testicular atrophy and reduced fertility are all side effects of spironolactone due to direct toxicity to the testicles.

Amiloride is another potassium sparing diuretic that works around the same area of the nephron as spironolactone but does not have the antiandrogenic effects. It is often combined with one of the potassium wasting diuretics like the loop diuretics or the thiazide diuretics.
