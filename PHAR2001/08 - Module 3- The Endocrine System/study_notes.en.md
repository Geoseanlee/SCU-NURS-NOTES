# PHAR2001 Module 3: The Endocrine System

## Overview

The endocrine system is a communication system. Glands release hormones into the bloodstream, and those hormones travel to distant organs where they bind receptors and change the target cell's behaviour. The module follows its main axes: hypothalamus and pituitary, thyroid, adrenal cortex, and the hormones governing bone. Each axis is a feedback loop, and each loop is a place where drugs replace a missing hormone, suppress an overactive gland, or block the effects of excess [S1] [V9].

The clinical relevance is direct: these hormones control metabolism, growth, salt and water balance, the stress response and bone strength. A patient on long-term corticosteroids, a patient with untreated thyroid disease and a patient with osteoporosis show the same principle: a hormone useful at physiological concentration becomes harmful when present in excess or suddenly removed. The lecture series also flags a gap: the diabetes chapter is not taught in these recordings and is deferred to the tutorial and case studies [S12] [V21].

## What you should be able to do

- Explain negative feedback using the hypothalamic–pituitary–target gland axes, and give an example of positive feedback [V3] [V5].
- Compare the posterior and anterior pituitary, and explain the actions and uses of oxytocin, ADH and growth hormone [S2] [V1].
- Explain how the thyroid axis regulates metabolism, and compare the treatment of hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism [S9] [V15].
- Explain how the adrenal axis responds to hypoglycaemia and volume loss, and name the two main adrenal steroids [S13] [V23].
- Describe the actions and adverse effects of glucocorticoids, and justify slow withdrawal after long-term therapy [S15] [S16] [V29].
- Explain calcium homeostasis and bone remodelling, and compare bisphosphonates, teriparatide and SERMs [S18] [S21].

## 1. The endocrine system as a communication network

The endocrine system is a set of glands that secrete hormones directly into the circulation so that the hormone reaches distant tissues and acts on receptors built for it. A hormone is a biochemical produced by an endocrine gland that circulates to those sites. The useful image is a molecular communicator: one organ sends a message that another is equipped to receive. Only cells carrying the matching receptor respond, so blood can carry a hormone everywhere while the effect stays selective [V3] [V4].

Two exceptions matter. The hypothalamus releases its hormones into a portal venous system running directly to the pituitary; that blood has already supplied the hypothalamus and carries the hormones straight to the pituitary rather than mixing into general circulation. The gonadal steroids are secreted into local reproductive tissue. These exceptions explain how very small amounts of hypothalamic hormone influence the pituitary without being diluted systemically [V3] [V5].

Hormones are not released at a constant rate. A pulsatile pattern is burst-like or episodic, as with gonadotropin-releasing hormone and growth hormone. A diurnal pattern follows a daily rhythm: growth hormone peaks a couple of hours after sleep begins, and cortisol peaks shortly after waking, around eight in the morning in most people. A cyclic pattern repeats over a longer cycle, as in the menstrual cycle. The pattern is part of the signal, which is why shift work alters cortisol timing and disrupted sleep in a child can disturb growth hormone and contribute to failure to thrive [V3] [V4].

Hormones are degraded fairly quickly by liver enzymes, usually becoming more water-soluble and inactive. Thyroxine is the exception described: it has a half-life of about six days and is converted to T3, which has three iodine atoms instead of four and is more active, before final inactivation. That long half-life and the conversion step explain why thyroid replacement is adjusted slowly and why the liver matters in thyroid disease [V3] [V17].

## 2. Feedback and the hypothalamic–pituitary axis

The hypothalamus is treated as the controlling organ, with a qualification: it does not control every endocrine cell, but it controls a large portion of the system. It maintains homeostasis, a stable internal environment, by receiving neural messages, hormones and cytokines, the messenger molecules of the immune system. It sends signals back out and controls the anterior and posterior pituitary directly or indirectly. That position makes it the master regulator [V5] [V6].

The portal system is the anatomical basis of that control. Small amounts of corticotropin-releasing hormone enter the portal blood and stimulate the anterior pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH reaches the adrenal cortex and stimulates cortisol, a glucocorticoid, and aldosterone, a mineralocorticoid. The same arrangement applies to thyroid-releasing hormone, which stimulates thyroid-stimulating hormone, which stimulates thyroxine release. Because the releasing hormone is delivered directly and in small amounts, control is precise rather than a matter of mass action [V5].

Negative feedback is the primary mechanism; positive feedback is less common. The menstrual cycle provides the standard example: small amounts of oestrogen exert negative feedback, medium amounts have little effect, and large amounts exert positive feedback. The distinction matters because interrupting negative feedback can make a gland overproduce, while mimicking the final hormone can shut the upstream axis down [V3] [V4].

The posterior pituitary differs structurally from the anterior pituitary. It is a neural extension of the hypothalamus. Oxytocin and ADH are made in the hypothalamus, stored in the posterior pituitary, and released when the hypothalamus signals. The posterior pituitary stores rather than manufactures them, and the two peptides are similar enough that oxytocin has some water-saving activity, while ADH has additional actions oxytocin lacks [V1] [V5] [V6].

## 3. The posterior pituitary: oxytocin and ADH

Oxytocin takes its name from Greek words meaning swift birth and appears on the World Health Organization's list of essential medicines. It is released in highest amounts during labour and suckling. During birth it participates in a positive feedback loop, and high levels around delivery also promote milk production; each episode of breastfeeding releases more oxytocin and more milk. It is therefore called the bonding or love hormone. It is released in both sexes during orgasm and contributes to positive feelings and trust afterwards, and it is partly responsible for the bond between mother and child [V7] [V8].

Oxytocin also modulates inflammation through several cytokines and can improve natural healing. Intranasal administration does not lengthen the half-life but slows absorption compared with intravenous administration, effectively lengthening exposure. Its main uses are promoting labour and breast milk production. It has superseded ergometrine as first-line therapy for initiating labour, promoting milk and controlling postpartum haemorrhage, though combinations remain because of its short half-life [V7] [V8] [S5].

ADH, also called vasopressin, is the water saver. A diuretic increases urine output, so an antidiuretic conserves water. ADH is synthesised in the hypothalamus, travels down the axon to the posterior pituitary, and is released in response to hyperosmolality, equivalent to dehydration. It acts on receptors at the kidney collecting ducts, opening water channels so water moves from forming urine into the interstitium and then capillaries. Saving water raises blood pressure, which is the physiological logic of the hormone [S6] [V9].

The ADH disorders illustrate the too-much-or-too-little rule. Too little ADH is diabetes insipidus. The name comes from Greek: diabetes means to siphon through and insipidus means tasteless, so the literal meaning is a disorder of tasteless urine. Too much fluid is siphoned out at the nephron and the person becomes dehydrated. This is not diabetes mellitus. Mellitus means sweet urine: in diabetes mellitus glucose passes through the nephron and cannot all be reabsorbed, so the urine is sweet. A patient with diabetes insipidus has a water-saving problem, not a glucose problem, and should not be labelled diabetic [V9].

Treatment is desmopressin, a synthetic replacement for vasopressin. It acts at the same site, opening the same water channels so fluid returns from pre-urine to the capillaries. Most preparations are degraded in the gastrointestinal tract, so common routes are subcutaneous, intravenous and sublingual. Desmopressin is also used for bedwetting and nocturia, and in those groups it can lower serum sodium, producing hyponatraemia. The risk is greater when the person has already lost salt through daytime sweating and then takes desmopressin, because the drug saves water without saving salt [V9] [V10].

The opposite disorder is syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion. Excess ADH produces too much water, causing hypertension, bloating and generalised oedema, and it usually results from a pituitary adenoma. The lectures note that it is uncommon and its drug treatments are not required. Two everyday substances affect the system: caffeine decreases ADH release and ethanol decreases its production, so both increase urine output. The alcohol hangover is the practical demonstration [S6] [V9] [V10].

## 4. The anterior pituitary: growth hormone

Growth hormone, or somatotropin, is the easiest anterior pituitary hormone to understand because its axis has two organs. It is anabolic, meaning it builds things up. It promotes muscle and bone growth, helps cells reproduce and increases protein synthesis. It also stimulates gluconeogenesis, mostly in the liver, so excess growth hormone raises blood glucose. It is one of six hormones that raise blood glucose, and the logic is protective: stress hormones keep glucose available so the brain, which depends on glucose, has a supply during stress [V11].

The axis runs from hypothalamic releasing hormone to anterior pituitary growth hormone, which feeds back negatively on the hypothalamus. Deficiency in childhood can profoundly affect final height. Many cases are idiopathic, but inadequate or disrupted sleep can contribute because growth hormone peaks a couple of hours after sleep begins. In adults, deficiency is usually caused by a pituitary tumour, is fairly rare, and produces reduced quality of life and energy. Somatotropin is the treatment and should be reserved for true deficiency. When taken by athletes who are not deficient, it enlarges every internal organ except the brain; an enlarged heart causes cardiovascular complications [V11] [V12] [S7].

Excess produces gigantism before the epiphyseal plates close and acromegaly afterwards. Excess at any age increases morbidity and lowers life expectancy. After the plates close, internal organs enlarge along with the nose, hands and head. A synthetic somatostatin analogue is the drug of choice, and because most cases are caused by a pituitary tumour, surgical excision is usually part of management [V11] [V12].

## 5. The thyroid axis and thyroid hormone

The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis has three organs. The hypothalamus secretes thyroid-releasing hormone into the portal system, where it acts on pituitary cells to release thyroid-stimulating hormone. TSH enters the circulation and signals the thyroid to release thyroid hormone. There is some baseline secretion, but the main stimulus begins at the hypothalamus. The main trigger is cold, and physiological and psychological stress also increase it. The design logic is that the body can raise the metabolic rate of nearly all cells in order to warm itself [V15] [V16].

Thyroid hormone acts on nearly every cell. It increases metabolic rate, increases sodium–potassium ATPase activity, and generates heat as a by-product. It increases cell sensitivity to catecholamines, so adrenaline and noradrenaline produce larger effects on heart rate and contractility, and it increases the speed of skeletal muscle contraction. It is important in growth and development, and a child born without thyroid hormone who is not detected and treated early develops intellectual disability [V15].

The thyroid sequesters iodine from blood flowing through it. When iodine is scarce the gland enlarges to capture as much as possible, producing a goitre; when the cause is dietary deficiency it is endemic goitre, and iodine supplementation prevents and treats it. The lecture corrects a common slip: the gland undergoes hyperplasia, an increase in cell number, more than hypertrophy, an increase in cell size, though both occur. Excess iodine can temporarily reduce thyroid function while cells adapt [V15] [S9].

Many drugs affect thyroid hormone or its release, so when a patient shows signs of hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, check the medication history before blaming the thyroid; thyroid function is assessed by blood test, and specific levels are not assessed in this introductory unit [V15] [V16]. The liver connection matters because T4 is converted to the more active T3 there, so liver function is relevant to thyroid status [V17].

## 6. Hypothyroidism and its treatment

Hypothyroidism occurs in about five per cent of women and becomes more common with age; it is less common in men. Present at birth, it is congenital hypothyroidism, or cretinism, usually from a missing protein or gene needed for hormone production. Children in Australia are tested at birth, and treatment is synthetic thyroid hormone for life [S10] [V17].

Endemic goitre is the iodine-deficiency form, treated with iodine supplementation. The disorder the module emphasises is Hashimoto's disease, an autoimmune destruction of the gland. Certain genes increase susceptibility, so there is a familial predisposition, and women are affected far more often. Autoantibodies target thyroid cells and destroy the gland slowly, so the disease is often not recognised early or is misdiagnosed as depression or chronic fatigue syndrome. One of the first signs is often a hard goitre that is not painful on palpation. Selenium, low-level laser therapy and gluten-free diets have been suggested to slow progression but are not necessarily proven [V17] [V18].

When Hashimoto's progresses to clinical hypothyroidism, treatment is levothyroxine, a synthetic T4, aiming to restore hormone to the normal range. Adverse effects often come from giving slightly too much, so the dose starts low and is adjusted with monitoring. Some patients still report hypothyroid symptoms after the dose is normalised, and the lecture treats this like subclinical hypothyroidism. In subclinical disease the person has slightly low function, reduced energy and a more sedentary lifestyle, which lowers metabolism further and leads to weight gain. The extra weight can begin a spiral that includes fatty liver disease; because T4 is converted to T3 in the liver, the fatty liver is part of the same metabolic picture. One therapy is unlikely to reverse all of that, so the plan may combine brisk walking, support for liver function and attention to other maintaining factors [V17] [V18].

Some patients never regain the ability to convert T4 to T3. In that case liothyronine, synthetic T3, may be considered; it is not routine, but some patients need it. Replacing the hormone is not always the same as restoring the pathway, because the hormone may need conversion and the converting organ may also be unwell [V17].

## 7. Hyperthyroidism and its treatment

The polar opposite of hypothyroidism is hyperthyroidism, and its most common specific disorder is also autoimmune: Graves' disease. The difference from Hashimoto's is the antibodies' behaviour. In Hashimoto's they destroy thyroid cells; in Graves' disease the immunoglobulins mimic thyroid-stimulating hormone, binding the thyroid and activating thyroxine release. The patient's own body drives the gland, and the hypothalamus has little influence: however much releasing hormone falls, the antibody continues to stimulate T4 production [V19] [V20].

Graves' disease causes a diffuse goitre and exophthalmos, protrusion of usually both eyes. Other forms of hyperthyroidism are not associated with exophthalmos. Thyroid hormone increases catecholamine sensitivity, so adrenergic signs are prominent; propranolol or another sympatholytic beta blocker is used alongside other therapy. Carbimazole or propylthiouracil is usually prescribed before radioactive iodine. Both inhibit the enzyme needed for hormone synthesis, and propylthiouracil also inhibits conversion of T4 to T3, so both hormones fall. The drawback is that the gland stores hormone and these drugs do not affect stored hormone, so the response is not immediate [V19] [V20] [S11].

Radioactive iodine, iodine-131, is taken up by the thyroid and destroys thyroid cells. The aim is to reduce the number of active cells, but treatment often destroys too many, producing hypothyroidism. Radioactive iodine is therefore a trade: it controls the overactive gland at the cost of creating a need for lifelong replacement [V19] [V20].

## 8. Diabetes mellitus and glucose-lowering therapy

The module's treatment of diabetes is limited and should be stated plainly. Diabetes mellitus means sweet urine: glucose rises in blood, passes through the nephron, and cannot all be reabsorbed, so glucose appears in urine. This differs from diabetes insipidus, where the defect is water conservation rather than glucose handling. The lectures also note that glucocorticoid therapy can raise glucose enough for the patient to be considered to have diabetes mellitus, often resolving when the drug stops, though physical changes may persist, especially if the drug was taken during childhood. Growth hormone and other stress hormones raise glucose by the same protective logic [V9] [V11] [V27].

The recorded module does not teach insulin, metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists or DPP-4 inhibitors. The lecturer states that diabetes is presented in the tutorial and case studies rather than this chapter [S12]. The recorded diabetes portion is missing from the corpus, which is a source gap.

> **Source gaps:** The module does not provide detail on insulin preparations, insulin regimens, or the mechanisms, adverse effects and cautions of metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists or DPP-4 inhibitors. The background below is for orientation only and is not examinable from this module.

**Background (orientation only).** Insulin lowers blood glucose and is replaced when endogenous secretion is insufficient. Metformin is a biguanide that reduces hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity. Sulfonylureas stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. SGLT2 inhibitors block renal glucose reabsorption and increase urinary glucose loss. GLP-1 receptor agonists augment incretin signalling, and DPP-4 inhibitors slow breakdown of endogenous incretins. These statements are general orientation, not claims from this module's sources; use the tutorial and case studies for assessed detail.

## 9. The adrenal axis and the stress response

The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys. They are small, about thumbnail-sized and about four grams each, but have an extensive blood supply. The cortex is the outer layer and secretes steroid hormones: aldosterone, a mineralocorticoid that saves salt; cortisol, a glucocorticoid that saves glucose; and androgens, including testosterone and analogues, with the cortex supplying a significant portion of the androgens present in females. The medulla is part of the autonomic nervous system and produces noradrenaline [V25] [V31].

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis is the stress axis. When the hypothalamus registers a stressor it releases corticotropin-releasing hormone into the portal system, stimulating the anterior pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH finds receptors on the adrenal cortex and prompts release of glucocorticoids such as cortisol and mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone. Cortisol then feeds back on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary to suppress further releasing hormone and ACTH. The system is a panic button: the hypothalamus responds to many stressors with the same package rather than tailoring a response [V23] [V25] [V29].

Two stressors show why this is the stress axis. Hypoglycaemia threatens the brain, which uses glucose for energy, and glucocorticoids restore glucose through the actions described next. Dehydration or hypovolaemic shock threatens circulating volume, and mineralocorticoids save salt, with water following salt. The axis therefore covers both the fuel problem and the volume problem. The objectives name spironolactone and fludrocortisone as the drugs to know [S13] [V23] [V25].

Mineralocorticoid excess produces too much salt and water, so the patient is bloated, hypertensive and volume-overloaded. Deficiency produces the opposite: salt and water are not retained and hypovolaemia develops. Spironolactone treats oedema from heart failure, liver disease or kidney disease. It is an aldosterone receptor antagonist: it binds the receptor and prevents aldosterone from binding, acting as an anti-salt-saver. As salt is wasted, water is wasted too, making it a diuretic. Fludrocortisone is named in the objectives, but the recorded lectures do not develop its pharmacology [S13] [V25] [V29].

## 10. The actions of the glucocorticoids

Cortisol is the main glucocorticoid and is released in response to almost any stress. The lectures describe six actions that raise glucose, although the recording itself names five; the sixth is not identified in the transcript. One is stimulation of protein breakdown. Breaking down protein provides energy that spares glucose, helping restore glucose levels, but the same catabolism produces thin skin and easy bruising when levels are high. A patient given a corticosteroid cream for haemorrhoids and told to use it for about seven days and stop may forget and continue; the steroid then breaks down skin and blood vessels and the condition worsens [V27] [V28].

A second action is stimulating hunger, and a third is storing energy in fat pads in places more accessible during prolonged stress. A fourth is inhibiting almost every arm of the immune system. This is not incidental: the stressor may itself be a type 1 hypersensitivity reaction, and a severe reaction can cause hypovolaemia, so suppressing immune and inflammatory responses is part of surviving that emergency. No other class suppresses immune and inflammatory responses as powerfully. A fifth action is a mineralocorticoid effect: they save some salt, and salt saving saves water [V27] [V28] [S15].

That breadth is why glucocorticoids are useful drugs. They are used for anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressant effects in severe asthma and autoimmune conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus. The clinical skill is separating the wanted suppression of inflammation from the unwanted effects on metabolism, skin, bone and salt balance [V27] [V35] [S15].

## 11. Corticosteroid adverse effects and withdrawal

Long-term corticosteroid therapy produces a recognisable pattern. Skin becomes thin and bruises easily. Fat is redistributed to the face, shoulder pads and central abdomen. Muscle protein is broken down. Blood glucose rises, sometimes to the point of diabetes mellitus. Bone is lost, and osteonecrosis, death of a portion of bone, can occur. Yeast infections become more likely because immune defences are suppressed. The collection of changes caused by excess corticosteroid is Cushing's syndrome, and when treatment causes it, it is iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome [V27] [V29] [V30].

The mechanism behind withdrawal is the negative feedback loop. Cortisol normally feeds back on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, suppressing corticotropin-releasing hormone and ACTH. When a patient takes a glucocorticoid for a long time, the exogenous hormone provides such strong negative feedback that the hypothalamus and pituitary effectively stop secreting their own releasing hormone and ACTH. The adrenal cortex, deprived of ACTH, stops producing cortisol. If the drug is stopped abruptly, the patient has a cortisol shortage. This is secondary adrenal insufficiency because the fault is in the regulatory axis, not the cortex. Primary adrenal failure at the cortex is Addison's disease [V29] [V30].

The consequence is serious because a person can live only a few days without cortisol. Long-term treatment must therefore be withdrawn very slowly, giving the patient's own axis time to resume secretion. This is the chapter's most important safety point: the drug is not simply stopped when the original condition improves. 

Prednisone is a prodrug converted to prednisolone, its active metabolite, in the liver, so patients with liver problems receive prednisolone directly. Prednisolone is a synthetic derivative of cortisol. Spironolactone is the aldosterone antagonist used as a diuretic. Prednisone and prednisolone are glucocorticoids, while spironolactone blocks a mineralocorticoid receptor and is not a glucocorticoid [V29] [V30].

## 12. Bone remodelling, calcium and osteoporosis

Bone is living tissue continually broken down and rebuilt. The osteoclast is the bone-destroying cell. It is very large and multinucleated, formed when several cells fuse and retain their nuclei; the same category includes giant cells that destroy microbes too large for ordinary phagocytes. Osteoclasts break down old bone and release calcium and phosphorus into blood. The osteoblast is the bone-building cell; it secretes products that allow minerals to precipitate back into bone. Bone mass is built up for the first thirty years and declines afterwards, so a good peak bone mass matters later [V35].

Osteoporosis means porous bone. It is too little bone: the proportion of mineral is often correct and the bone is not necessarily softer, but there is less of it, so it is thinner and more fragile. Because it affects vertebrae, it causes loss of height. Women are affected much more than men. Oestrogen is a more potent stimulator of bone growth than testosterone, and it falls sharply at menopause. Smoking, inactivity, a diet high in phosphorus from soft drinks, low calcium, low vitamin D and corticosteroid therapy all contribute; weight-bearing exercise stimulates osteoblastic activity and is protective [V35] [V36].

Calcium must be kept in a narrow range for bone and for cardiac muscle contraction. Two hormones keep it in range. Parathyroid hormone is released from the parathyroid glands when blood calcium falls. It raises calcium in four ways: increasing release from bone, increasing intestinal absorption, decreasing renal excretion, and increasing synthesis of active vitamin D in the kidney. Active vitamin D is a steroid hormone required for calcium absorption; without it, almost no calcium is absorbed. Calcitonin is released when calcium rises and stimulates osteoblasts to deposit calcium into bone [V37] [V38].

The bone disorders form a useful contrast. Osteoporosis is too little bone with correct mineralisation. Osteomalacia is the adult form of rickets; both involve too little mineralisation, so bone softens. In children the rapidly growing bones are affected first and the legs bow; in adults the presentation is more often pelvic abnormality. Osteonecrosis is death of a portion of bone, usually from loss of blood supply, and corticosteroid treatment is the main cause. Paget's disease involves increased destruction and formation, often in one area such as the forehead, but the rebuilt bone is weaker because remodelling is disordered [V37] [V38].

## 13. Drugs for bone disease

Oral bisphosphonates are first-line therapy for osteoporosis. They prevent loss of bone density, are the most commonly prescribed drugs for osteoporosis, and reduce fractures in postmenopausal women when taken preventively. They incorporate into bone and encourage osteoclasts to undergo apoptosis, a form of programmed cell death that does not leave toxic debris. Oral bisphosphonates end in -dronate, and alendronate is the example. Their limitation is that they do not increase new bone formation; they decrease destruction. They carry a very slight increased risk of osteonecrosis of the jaw and have been associated with severe bone, joint or musculoskeletal pain [V39] [V40] [S21].

Teriparatide is the only anabolic treatment for osteoporosis, meaning it builds bone rather than only slowing loss. It is a recombinant form of parathyroid hormone, produced by altering DNA, and is expensive; it is on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme. Because it acts like parathyroid hormone, it increases calcium uptake from the gut and reduces renal calcium excretion, so the patient is susceptible to hypercalcaemia, which can have cardiovascular effects. Calcium monitoring is therefore needed. It also carries a risk of osteosarcoma, a bone cancer, and must be injected. It is not approved for speeding fracture healing, but athletes have used it to accelerate bone remodelling after injury [V39] [V40].

Selective oestrogen receptor modulators, or SERMs, include tamoxifen and the -oxifene drugs. Tamoxifen was launched as a breast cancer treatment because it antagonises oestrogen binding in breast tissue, but it was found to benefit bone. At bone it acts like oestrogen on oestrogen receptors, preventing bone loss and inhibiting osteoclastic activity. This tissue-specific behaviour defines a SERM: antagonist in one tissue, agonist in another [V39].

Drug therapy sits alongside weight-bearing exercise, a calcium-rich diet, less phosphorus-rich food such as soft drinks, and smoking cessation [V39].

## Key takeaways

- Hormones are molecular messages; only cells with the matching receptor respond, and the hypothalamic portal system is an exception to general circulation [V3] [V5].
- Negative feedback is the default control mechanism; positive feedback is the exception, illustrated by the large-oestrogen surge [V3].
- Osteoporosis is too little bone with normal mineralisation; bisphosphonates reduce resorption, teriparatide builds bone, and SERMs act like oestrogen at bone [V35] [V39].
- ADH conserves water at the collecting duct; desmopressin replaces it in diabetes insipidus and can cause hyponatraemia when used for bedwetting [S6] [V9].
- Long-term glucocorticoids suppress the HPA axis; abrupt withdrawal causes secondary adrenal insufficiency, so the dose must be tapered [V29].
- The recorded module does not cover insulin or oral hypoglycaemic drugs; those are deferred to the tutorial and case studies [S12].

## Common confusions

**Diabetes insipidus is not diabetes mellitus.** Insipidus means tasteless and describes dilute urine from ADH deficiency. Mellitus means sweet and describes glucose spilling into urine. The physiology and treatment are unrelated [V9].

**Glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid are not interchangeable.** Glucocorticoids mainly defend glucose; mineralocorticoids mainly defend salt and water. Cortisol has some mineralocorticoid activity, which is why it causes salt retention as well as hyperglycaemia [V23] [V27].

**Prednisone and prednisolone are not unrelated drugs.** Prednisone is a prodrug converted in the liver to prednisolone, its active form, so liver disease is a reason to prescribe prednisolone directly [V29].

**Addison's disease and secondary adrenal insufficiency differ.** Addison's is primary failure at the adrenal cortex. Steroid withdrawal produces secondary insufficiency because the hypothalamus and pituitary have been suppressed and no longer drive the cortex [V29].

**Osteoporosis, osteomalacia and osteonecrosis are different lesions.** Osteoporosis is too little bone; osteomalacia is under-mineralised bone; osteonecrosis is dead bone from loss of blood supply [V37].

**"Steroids" is ambiguous.** In common speech it may mean anabolic steroids such as testosterone, but in this module it usually means anti-inflammatory glucocorticoids such as prednisone and prednisolone [V25].

**Hypertrophy and hyperplasia are not synonyms.** Hypertrophy is larger cells; hyperplasia is more cells. The iodine-deficient thyroid undergoes mainly hyperplasia, though both occur [S9].
