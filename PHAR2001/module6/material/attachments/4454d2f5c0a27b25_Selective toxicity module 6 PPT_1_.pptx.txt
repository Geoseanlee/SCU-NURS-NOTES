MECHANISM OF ACTION OF ANTIMICROBIAL AGENTS 
The ‘ideal’ antimicrobial agent will, after entering a person’s body, move to the site of infection, destroy the pathogenic microbe (or at least facilitate its destruction), then be eliminated from the body without affecting the structure or function of human body cells. To achieve this aim, it must attack and disable some microbial process or structure not present in humans. This is known as the principle of selective toxicity. Indeed, there are many differences between human cells and microbes to exploit (see Figure 70.1). 
The mechanism of action of any antimicrobial agent can be classified according to the way in which it exploits these differences. The four categories by which antimicrobial agents work are described here. 
Inhibition of cell wall synthesis 
Most bacteria possess a cell wall to protect them from the osmotic influences of the external environment. As these microbes divide to produce progeny, new cell walls are synthesised. Certain antimicrobial agents interrupt the formation of the cell wall in these progeny, leaving them vulnerable to outside influences. As a result, the cell ruptures and the microbes perish. 
Penicillins, 
cephalosporins
, 
vancomycin
 and bacitracin 
are examples of medicines that work in this way. These are relatively nontoxic antimicrobial agents, as human cells do not possess a cell wall. 
Interference with metabolic processes 
These medicines are structurally similar to para-
aminobenzoic
 acid (PABA), a component of folic acid. The latter substance is an essential participant in nucleic acid synthesis. Without folic acid, the bacteria cannot produce the proteins necessary for growth. Antimicrobial agents with this action inhibit the synthesis of folic acid by competition with PABA or incorporate themselves in the structure of folic acid, thus interfering with the metabolic reactions in which it would normally participate. The selectivity of these medicines derives from the fact that many microbes must synthesise their own folic acid, whereas humans can use folic acid present in their diet. The 
sulfonamides
 and trimethoprim are examples of medicines with this mechanism of action (see Chapter 71). 
Inhibition of protein synthesis 
There are two locations at which such medicines can act—either at the site of protein synthesis (the ribosome) or within the nucleus by preventing the synthesis of nucleic acids (inhibiting either DNA replication or RNA synthesis), thereby blocking transcription. Proteins are essential to repair and growth. As new proteins need to be manufactured before cell division, inhibition of this process halts microbial population growth. Examples of medicines that act in this way are the 
tetracyclines
 (e.g. doxycycline), aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamicin) and macrolides (e.g. erythromycin). These medicines exploit structural differences between human and bacterial ribosomes or enzymes involved in nucleic acid synthesis. However, at higher doses they can be quite toxic to human cells, which also need to synthesise proteins. 
Disruption or destabilising the microbial cell membrane 
Antimicrobial agents that act through this mechanism affect membrane transport into and out of the microbe. This change in cellular permeability is incompatible with survival. 
Colistin
 is an example of such medicines. Many of the antifungal agents also act by this mechanism (see Chapter 78). These medicines are more toxic systemically than the previous group, as some human cells (e.g. erythrocytes) are particularly vulnerable to their action.

 are the unicellular organisms and cannot be seen with naked eye. There is no particular method of cell division, they simply divide simply by binary fission in which cell divides into two daughter cells. They do no have proper nucleus within the cell but the genetic material is attached to the cell membrane in an irregular form. They are found everywhere like top of the mountains, rivers, on land and in ice. Bacteria have the property of living in extreme weathers like extreme cold and extreme heat. They are able to live long because they become inactive for a long period of time.
Bacteria play an important role in the environment. Many dead materials are decomposed by bacteria. .

The role of viruses
 is less clear, but there is no doubt that evolution is influenced by viruses. Specifically
, the site of action is usually (but not always) one of the ribosomal subunits, where proteins are manufactured. 
 selective toxicity of these medicines against microbial cells arises out of 
differences between the structure of prokaryote (microbial) and eukaryote (mammalian) ribosomes. 
However, at higher doses these 
antibacterials
 inhibit mammalian ribosomal processes as well.
 Protein synthesis inhibitors tend to have bacteri

 properties at standard therapeutic doses. AMINOGLYCOSIDES 
The prototype aminoglycoside was streptomycin— 
historically, the next major antibacterial identified after penicillins and the first isolated from a Streptomyces mould. 
Today, it is rarely used clinically in Australia and New Zealand. The clinically important aminoglycosides are 
framycetin
, gentamicin, tobramycin, neomycin, 
paromomycin
 and 
amikacin
. 
Paromomycin
 is only available through the Special Access Scheme in Australia. 
Mechanism of action 
Even though they act by inhibiting protein synthesis (affecting the smaller ribosomal subunit) and, therefore, microbial growth, their potency at therapeutic dose levels affords them bactericidal properties. 
The spectrum of activity of most of these medicines is broad, effective against a wide range of Gram-negative bacteria (
making them important in the treatment of serious Gram-negative sepsis), staphylococci and, to a lesser degree, streptococci. The aminoglycosides are often used when microbes are found to be resistant to cheaper and less toxic antibacterial agents. 
Common adverse effects 
The toxicity of the aminoglycosides is well noted. 
The three body sites seriously affected by these medicines and the toxic effects observed are 
the kidneys (nephrotoxicity), 
the 
eighth cranial nerve (ototoxicity) and the neuromuscular junction (neuromuscular paralysis). 
These effects can be minimised by measuring peak and trough serum drug levels to ensure that they conform to the desired range, as well as monitoring the person for altered renal and vestibular functions. The neuromuscular effects can be avoided through slow intravenous administration. 
Clinical considerations 
The absorption of aminoglycoside antibacterial agents from the gastrointestinal tract is negligible, so 
for systemic effectiveness these medicines must be administered 
parenterally
.
 The exception is neomycin, which can be administered orally for bowel sterilisation before surgery. Neomycin is also available in combination with other antibacterial agents and/or a corticosteroid for topical use. The use of 
amikacin
 is usually reserved for situations in which resistance to the other aminoglycosides is apparent. 
Paromomycin
 is administered after completing treatment with metronidazole or 
tinidazole
. Thus, it is not used alone for intestinal disease. 
Paromomycin
 is taken with or after meals to reduce the incidence of drug-related diarrhoea. 
Once-daily dosing of aminoglycosides is currently thought to be as effective as more frequent dosing, and is generally recommended for individuals with normal renal function. If treatment is likely to continue for less than 48 hours, drug concentration monitoring is not required in people with normal renal function. However, for treatment longer than 48 hours, drug concentration and creatinine clearance should be checked every three to five days in clinically stable people, and daily in people with some form of clinical instability, especially those individuals with impaired renal function. A baseline creatinine clearance is determined for all people before treatment.
 
 
A number of strategies could be implemented to prevent aminoglycoside toxicity. Dose frequency should be once daily and certainly no more than 12-hourly, except in specified conditions, such as infective endocarditis or osteomyelitis. Gentamicin can be administered with 
benzylpenicillin
 to treat serious 
enterococcal
 infections, such as endocarditis. Duration of therapy should be relatively short and generally not more than 7–10 days. Cochlear toxicity can be monitored using audiometry testing every one or two weeks during prolonged treatment. Ensuring adequate hydration can help to prevent nephrotoxicity. If the renal function deteriorates, the aminoglycoside dosage may need to be adjusted or an alternative antibacterial agents considered. 
TETRACYCLINES 
Mechanism of action 
Tetracyclines
 are broad-spectrum, bacteriostatic antibacterial agents. As with the aminoglycosides, the mechanism of action is directed at the smaller ribosomal subunit. 
Demeclocycline
 is the only natural member of the tetracycline group used clinically, as all the others (tetracycline, 
lymecycline
, 
tigecycline
, doxycycline and minocycline) are either semisynthetic or synthetic derivatives. 
Tigecycline
 is a derivative of minocycline and is referred to as a member of the 
glycylcycline
 antibacterial agents. All the 
tetracyclines
 have a similar spectrum of activity and are effective against the Plasmodium spp. that cause malaria (see Chapter 76), many Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, 
rickettsiae
 and spirochaetes, and some amoebae. In terms of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, many strains have become resistant to the action of the 
tetracyclines
 and, therefore, culture and sensitivity tests are recommended. Tetracycline itself is not marketed in Australia, but it is available through the Special Access Scheme, if needed. 
Demeclocycline
 may be available in Australia through the Special Access Scheme. Similarly, the injection form of doxycycline is only marketed through the Special Access Scheme. 
Lymecycline
 is only available in New Zealand. 
Common adverse effects 
The most commonly observed adverse effects associated with the 
tetracyclines
 are gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, anorexia, abdominal pain), hypersensitivity reactions (skin rashes, discolouration of the nails, photosensitivity) and superinfection. This last effect is usually associated with prolonged treatment with 
tetracyclines
, and can be avoided by administering the antifungal agent nystatin in combination with tetracycline. Gastric irritation, 
oesophagitis
 and, more rarely, oesophageal ulceration, have been reported during doxycycline therapy. 
Clinical considerations 
Many of the 
tetracyclines
 are poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration; however, the resultant blood levels are still therapeutically effective. One of the major problems associated with the 
tetracyclines
 is their ability to bind to free divalent and trivalent ions, especially calcium (Ca2+), in the gut and blood. In the gut, this property results in less of the medicine being absorbed. This lack of absorption is especially problematic when 
tetracyclines
 are administered with food or 
nonsystemic
 antacids containing salts of aluminium, calcium and magnesium. The only exceptions are doxycycline and minocycline, which are very lipid-soluble 
tetracyclines
 whose absorption is not impaired by the presence of food in the gut. Antacids and preparations containing iron will decrease the absorption of all 
tetracyclines
. If these preparations need to be taken, they should be administered more than two hours after giving a tetracycline. Thus, 
tetracyclines
 should generally be taken one hour before or two hours after meals. 
The effect of binding calcium ions in the blood is that less calcium is stored in bones and teeth. This lack of calcium storage is the main reason why 
tetracyclines
 are not recommended for children under 8 years old or for women in the latter half of pregnancy. Newly forming teeth become permanently discoloured and poorly enamelled. Indeed, in children who have discoloured teeth as a result of exposure to tetracycline, you should be able to see the borderline separating affected and normal growth. If this line is close to the gums, then the exposure was later in tooth development. If it is closer to the crown, then exposure was early in development. Bone growth may also be affected. 
To reduce the risk of gastric and oesophageal reactions, it is recommended that tetracycline tablets or capsules be administered with a full glass of water and that the person not lie down for two hours after taking the medicine. The latter recommendation minimises drug contact with the oesophageal region. Ingestion just before bedtime should, therefore, be avoided. 
As 
tetracyclines
 may cause photosensitivity, people are advised to wear protective clothing when outside and to use sunscreen. The specific agent known as tetracycline is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and, therefore, should be given on an empty stomach and in high
 
 
doses to obtain an adequate response. Unfortunately, these actions tend to increase the gastric irritation produced by tetracycline. 
People should be informed that 
tigecycline
 therapy may make them dizzy. They need to tell their doctor if they develop abdominal pain. Due to possible hypersensitivity with 
tetracyclines
, 
tigecycline
 should be used cautiously in these people. 
Tigecycline
 may prolong the prothrombin time and activated partial 
thromboplastin
 time, and cause thrombocytopenia in people with bleeding disorders. During long-term therapy with 
demeclocycline
, renal function should be monitored, especially in individuals with heart failure or liver cirrhosis, due to its effect on electrolyte and fluid balance. 
MACROLIDES
Erythromycin, clarithromycin and 
roxithromycin
 are macrolide antibacterial agents. Azithromycin is closely related, both chemically and pharmacologically, to the macrolides and is grouped with them. 
Mechanism of action
The macrolides specifically affect the function of the larger microbial ribosomal subunit without reacting with the human ribosome. This accounts for their relatively low human toxicity.
The spectrum of activity of this group resembles that of penicillin G; however, culture and sensitivity tests are recommended because resistance to previously sensitive strains has been demonstrated. Compared with erythromycin, the other medicines are less active against Gram-positive bacteria and more effective against Gram-negative bacteria, especially H. 
influenzae
. Azithromycin is very effective in the treatment of toxoplasmosis and may be more effective against Legionella and Chlamydia. Azithromycin, clarithromycin and 
roxithromycin
 also have a lower potential for hepatotoxicity than orally administered erythromycin. Because of their spectrum of activity, the macrolides are an important alternative for people with an allergy to penicillins or other related antibacterial agents. 
Common adverse effects
Unpleasant gastrointestinal disturbances and superinfection are common adverse reactions. The frequency of these adverse reactions is reduced when another medicine from this group besides erythromycin is used. 
Clinical considerations
The macrolides can be administered either orally or 
parenterally
. The newer medicines are more acid-stable than erythromycin. Some forms of erythromycin must be 
enterically
 coated for oral administration due to its acid-
lability
. Erythromycin has a bitter taste, which is somewhat reduced by the enteric coating; however, paediatric forms (granules, dulcets and suspensions) are flavoured to make them more palatable.
Macrolides are useful alternatives to penicillins and 
cephalosporins
 for individuals who are allergic to these medicines. Erythromycin and 
roxithromycin
 are best absorbed when taken about an hour before or two hours after meals. However, if nausea occurs they may be taken with food.
Administration of erythromycin or clarithromycin may cause prolongation of the Q-T interval and development of serious ventricular dysrhythmias. Rapid intravenous administration of erythromycin can cause the development of serious dysrhythmias, so it is important that the infusion rate not exceed 1–5 mg/mL over at least 60 minutes. Erythromycin and clarithromycin should not be administered with other medicines that have a tendency to prolong the Q-T interval, such as amitriptyline, haloperidol, 
amiodarone
 and 
pimozide
. Prolongation of the Q-T interval can lead to life-threatening cardiac rhythms, such as torsades de pointes.
It is also important to monitor for hepatic dysfunction during long-term or high-dose erythromycin therapy. Discontinue erythromycin therapy if severe hepatic dysfunction occurs, as demonstrated by changes in liver enzyme levels. 
STREPTOGRAMINS
Two members of the 
streptogramin
 antibacterial group, 
quinupristin
 and 
dalfopristin
, are available in Australia and New Zealand in a combined preparation. 
Mechanism of action
The 
streptogramins
 target protein synthesis at the ribosome. Each of these drugs induces a bacteriostatic action, but when used together the effect becomes a bactericidal action. They are indicated for intravenous administration in MDR S. Aureus or VRE infections when other antimicrobials are inappropriate. These medicines may also be useful in treating other 
multiresistant
 bacteria, such as Streptococcus and selected Gram-negative bacteria. 
Common adverse effects
Common adverse effects include reactions at the injection site, such as inflammation, pain and oedema. Systemic adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and rashes.
 
 
This combined preparation is administered intravenously every eight hours, and is prone to causing peripheral vein irritation. This effect can be reduced by using large infusion volumes of at least 1 L or if the combined preparation is infused through a central vein rather than through a peripheral vein. The preparation is not compatible with sodium chloride and should be administered with 5% glucose. Monitor the blood count periodically to determine whether thrombocytopenia or anaemia occurs. Arthralgia, myalgia and thrombophlebitis are common problems that can adversely affect people. Dosing every 12 hours or reducing the dose may help to resolve these problems. 
OXAZOLIDINONES 
Linezolid is the sole representative of the 
oxazolidinones
 available in this region. It, too, is recommended for use in MDR S. aureus and VRE infections. Linezolid is formulated for both oral and parenteral administration. 
Mechanism of action 
Linezolid acts to inhibit protein synthesis at the ribosome. However, its mechanism of action is different from that of other antibacterial agents. It binds to one of the subunits and prevents the two subunits from joining together. Subunit joining is essential for translation to occur. 
Common adverse effects 
Common adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, altered taste, abnormal liver function tests and superinfection with Candida 
albicans
. A reversible 
myelosuppression
 has also been observed, particularly when treatment is prolonged for more than 10–14 days. 
Clinical considerations 
Linezolid reversibly inhibits monoamine oxidase. People should avoid consuming foods that are rich in 
tyramine
 (e.g. mature cheese, yeast extracts) when taking linezolid as they may experience a rise in blood pressure. The mechanism by which 
tyramine
 and monoamine oxidase inhibition can interact to induce a hypertensive state is described in the chapter on antidepressant agents (see Chapter 36). Similarly, it should not be administered concurrently with monoamine oxidase inhibitor antidepressants. Due to its ability to increase synaptic concentration of serotonin, linezolid should not be administered with pethidine due to the potential for serotonin toxicity to occur. 
Long-term treatment of more than 21 days should be avoided to prevent the development of linezolid-resistant enterococci. This medicine is considered only if other 
antibacterials
 have failed, or they are inappropriate due to allergy or resistance. If linezolid treatment continues beyond 14 days, people should have their complete blood counts monitored each week. They should also be monitored for optic or peripheral neuropathy, especially if treatment is to be continued for more than 28 days.

inactivators
 of the larger ribosomal subunit 
The 
lincosamides

The prototype aminoglycoside was streptomycin—

the

MECHANISM OF ACTION OF ANTIMICROBIAL AGENTS 
The ‘ideal’ antimicrobial agent will, after entering a person’s body, move to the site of infection, destroy the pathogenic microbe (or at least facilitate its destruction), then be eliminated from the body without affecting the structure or function of human body cells. To achieve this aim, it must attack and disable some microbial process or structure not present in humans. This is known as the principle of selective toxicity. Indeed, there are many differences between human cells and microbes to exploit (see Figure 70.1). 
The mechanism of action of any antimicrobial agent can be classified according to the way in which it exploits these differences. The four categories by which antimicrobial agents work are described here. 
Inhibition of cell wall synthesis 
Most bacteria possess a cell wall to protect them from the osmotic influences of the external environment. As these microbes divide to produce progeny, new cell walls are synthesised. Certain antimicrobial agents interrupt the formation of the cell wall in these progeny, leaving them vulnerable to outside influences. As a result, the cell ruptures and the microbes perish. 
Penicillins, 
cephalosporins
, 
vancomycin
 and bacitracin 
are examples of medicines that work in this way. These are relatively nontoxic antimicrobial agents, as human cells do not possess a cell wall. 
Interference with metabolic processes 
These medicines are structurally similar to para-
aminobenzoic
 acid (PABA), a component of folic acid. The latter substance is an essential participant in nucleic acid synthesis. Without folic acid, the bacteria cannot produce the proteins necessary for growth. Antimicrobial agents with this action inhibit the synthesis of folic acid by competition with PABA or incorporate themselves in the structure of folic acid, thus interfering with the metabolic reactions in which it would normally participate. The selectivity of these medicines derives from the fact that many microbes must synthesise their own folic acid, whereas humans can use folic acid present in their diet. The 
sulfonamides
 and trimethoprim are examples of medicines with this mechanism of action (see Chapter 71). 
Inhibition of protein synthesis 
There are two locations at which such medicines can act—either at the site of protein synthesis (the ribosome) or within the nucleus by preventing the synthesis of nucleic acids (inhibiting either DNA replication or RNA synthesis), thereby blocking transcription. Proteins are essential to repair and growth. As new proteins need to be manufactured before cell division, inhibition of this process halts microbial population growth. Examples of medicines that act in this way are the 
tetracyclines
 (e.g. doxycycline), aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamicin) and macrolides (e.g. erythromycin). These medicines exploit structural differences between human and bacterial ribosomes or enzymes involved in nucleic acid synthesis. However, at higher doses they can be quite toxic to human cells, which also need to synthesise proteins. 
Disruption or destabilising the microbial cell membrane 
Antimicrobial agents that act through this mechanism affect membrane transport into and out of the microbe. This change in cellular permeability is incompatible with survival. 
Colistin
 is an example of such medicines. Many of the antifungal agents also act by this mechanism (see Chapter 78). These medicines are more toxic systemically than the previous group, as some human cells (e.g. erythrocytes) are particularly vulnerable to their action.

Viruses do not fit the normal description of life. 
Perhaps it is better to think of viruses as infectious agents that enter cells and interfere with their normal function. A virus itself is not a cell, but a strand of either DNA or RNA, which has a protein coat and sometimes a lipid envelope. There is no cell membrane or cytoplasm. When a virus particle, a 
virion
, enters a cell, it uses the host cell enzymes to synthesise more of its nucleic acid and the components of its 
extranucleic
 structures. The viral nucleic acid can code for specific viral enzymes. A virus employs at least some of the existing metabolic machinery in the host cell for its own use. It is, therefore, very difficult to come up with a medicine that can destroy the virus without affecting the host cell. The process of infecting a host cell, viral synthesis and release of new viruses is represented in Figure 77.1. 
Most antiviral agents target the differences that exist between viral nucleic acids or enzymes, and those of the human host. Common sites of antiviral drug action are represented in Figure 77.2.

HIV INFECTIONS 
The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), is a retrovirus and is caused mainly by two viral strains, HIV-1 and HIV-2. HIV-1 is found worldwide, whereas HIV-2 infections are commonest in parts of Africa and India. 
A 
retrovirus
 is a single-stranded 
RNA virus
 that stores its nucleic acid in the form of an 
mRNA
 genome 
Once inside the host cell 
cytoplasm
, the virus uses its own 
reverse transcriptase
 enzyme to produce 
DNA
 from its 
RNA
 genome, the reverse of the usual pattern, thus 
retro
 (backwards). 
This new DNA is then 
incorporated
 into the host cell 
genome
 by an 
integrase
 enzyme, at which point the retroviral DNA is referred to as a 
provirus
. The host cell then treats the viral DNA as part of its own 
genome
, translating and transcribing the viral genes along with the cell's own genes, producing the proteins required to assemble new copies of the virus. It is difficult to detect the virus until it has infected the host. At that point, the infection will persist indefinitely.
The antiviral agents used in the suppression of HIV are mostly effective against the HIV-1 strain
. 
The virus infects the CD4 cells (a group of helper T lymphocytes that possess the CD4 membrane receptor), thus decreasing the effectiveness of the cell-mediated immune systems. 
Two of the most  important drug groups used to suppress HIV are reverse transcriptase inhibitors and HIV protease inhibitors. The viral enzymes reverse transcriptase and protease are more susceptible to AIDS medicines than are human enzymes.
 Humans do not have the reverse transcriptase enzyme; however, these enzyme inhibitors can slightly affect other enzymes involved in human nucleic acid metabolism.

Antimetabolites
The antimetabolite 
flucytosine
 
is used to treat various systemic fungal infections, including those involving 
C. 
albicans
. Some fungi are more susceptible than others, and resistance has been reported.
Mechanism of action
Flucytosine
, like some of the antivirals, is a 
prodrug
. 
As an antimetabolite it is 
converted in fungal cells 
into its active form, 5-fluorouracil (a substituted pyrimidine), and incorporated in RNA, making it defective. 
Mammalian cells cannot activate 
flucytosine
. The medicine is 
fungistatic
,
 is usually given by intravenous infusion and is well distributed in all body compartments. 5-Fluorouracil and other antimetabolites are used in the management of some cancers (see Chapter 80). 
Flucytosine
 capsules are not marketed in Australia but they may be obtained through the Special Access Scheme. 
Common adverse effects 
As it is specific to fungi, 
adverse effects are few. 
Nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea are seen occasionally. 
Flucytosine
 is excreted unchanged by the kidneys, and care must be taken in people with renal insufficiency, as blood levels may reach toxic levels due to an increase in half-life. 
Clinical considerations
Flucytosine
 is available as an 
injection preparation
. As resistance to 
flucytosine
 may develop rapidly and it has 
a narrow spectrum of action
, 
it is used in combination with another antifungal agent. Monitor the blood count, and liver and renal functions, at baseline and then regularly during treatment.
Concentration monitoring of 
flucytosine
 is important in renal impairment when used in conjunction with amphotericin. For individuals who do not have renal impairment, the dose interval of therapy for 
flucytosine
 is every six hours. However, the dose interval is progressively increased with increasing severity of renal impairment to reduce the risk of haematological adverse effects, such as anaemia, 
leukopoenia
 and agranulocytosis. 
Flucytosine
 should be kept at 15–25 °C. It forms fluorouracil at temperatures above 25 °C and it can precipitate when kept below 15 °C.

A joint action of two drugs in such a manner that one supplements or enhances the action of the other to produce 
an effect greater than that which may be obtained with either one of the drugs
 in equivalent quantity or produce effects that could not be obtained with any safe quantity of either drug, or both.

Chemotherapy —  drugs with selective toxicity
The word ‘chemotherapy’ first used in the early 1900s 
The first chemotherapeutic agent was arsphenamine, an arsenic compound discovered in 1909 and used to treat syphilis. 
Later followed by sulphonamides and penicillin. 
Some authors still describe chemotherapy as “an agent that has selective toxicity against an invader (a virus, a bacterium, a parasite or a cancerous cell), and causes little damage to the normal cells of the host organism”.

## ppt/slides/slide22.xml
Microscopy, culture and sensitivity (MC&S) 
Microscopy: The sample can be viewed under a microscope within minutes of arriving at the laboratory. This enables a quick initial report, such as ‘Gram positive cocci seen’, to be telephoned to the clinician if necessary. Combined with an accurate clinical picture, this may be enough to initiate targeted treatment.
Culture: A sample from the original culture is grown to create a pure sample to enable further identification of an organism.
Sensitivity: A pure culture is seeded onto an agar plate containing discs saturated with various antibiotics. If the organism grows up to a disc it is resistant to that antibiotic; if there is a clear zone around the disc, it is susceptible.

## ppt/slides/slide101.xml
Non-azole sterol biosynthesis inhibitors
Mechanism of action
Terbinafine and butenafine disrupt ergosterol biosynthesis, leading to an inhibition of fungal cell wall synthesis. 
Oral treatment can cure onychomycosis (fungal infection of the nails), in as little as 12 weeks. 
Common adverse effects
Liver problems can occur with terbinafine, and gastrointestinal upsets occur in about 10 per cent of treated people.
