# PHAR2001 — Module 6: Selective toxicity and polypharmacy / 选择性毒性与多重用药

**Medication safety:** this is educational material, not prescribing advice. Verify current authoritative medicines information before clinical use. Some source pages contain older assessment percentages or legacy statements; they are preserved rather than silently corrected.

## Selective toxicity

Explain how a medicine targets a pathogen or abnormal cell while trying to limit harm to the host. Selectivity is relative, not a promise of zero adverse effects. Distinguish bacterial, fungal, viral and malignant-cell targets.

## Antibiotics

Start with bacterial structure and the process being targeted. The full chapter compares cell-wall, protein-synthesis, DNA and metabolic targets. Relate appropriate use to the organism and indication, and explain resistance and stewardship rather than treating antibiotics as a remedy for every infection.

## Antifungal and antiviral medicines

Compare fungal targets with viral replication. Antiviral therapy depends on the virus and the stage or process being inhibited. The compulsory and full versions cover these topics at different depth; both branches are preserved.

## Anticancer chemotherapy

Link loss of growth regulation to treatment goals and collateral effects on normal tissue. Distinguish the aim of targeting malignant cells from the assumption that all rapidly dividing cells can be spared.

## Polypharmacy reflection

The final reflective lesson asks students to examine possible over-reliance on medicines. Review the indication, intended benefit, interactions, cumulative burden and the person’s goals; do not infer that the correct response is to stop prescribed medicines without clinical review.

## Revision checks

Contrast an antibacterial and an antiviral target; explain relative selective toxicity; identify two routes to unnecessary medicine burden. Keep the source’s 43-minute compulsory pathway distinct from the additional full-chapter material.

## Targets and selective toxicity

| Target/group | Mechanism highlighted in the full chapter | Why toxicity/selectivity still matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial cell wall | Beta-lactam antibiotics include penicillins and cephalosporins; beta-lactamase can interfere with the active ring | Resistance mechanisms and the role of an inhibitor such as clavulanic acid must be explained, not treated as a guarantee against all resistance |
| Bacterial proteins, DNA or metabolism | Additional antibiotics affect different essential bacterial processes; the source uses folate synthesis as a metabolic example | Bacteriostatic and bactericidal describe different effects; antimicrobial choice still depends on the clinical context |
| Fungal membrane | Ergosterol-related targets help distinguish fungi from human cells | Fungal and human cells share many features, so selectivity is incomplete and adverse effects remain important |
| Viral replication | Antivirals interrupt virus-specific stages or processes; the full branch discusses herpes, HIV and hepatitis | Antibacterial mechanisms cannot simply be applied to viruses; the source warns that treatment standards change |
| Malignant cells | Chemotherapy targets abnormal growth and related cellular processes | Normal tissues can also be harmed; intended selectivity does not mean cancer-only effects |

## Required versus extension material

The source provides a condensed compulsory pathway and a fuller branch with additional antibiotic and antiviral lessons. Both are saved. Study status follows the source labels rather than file order. In the final polypharmacy reflection, ask what each medicine is for, whether the benefit remains relevant, how products interact and who should review the regimen; do not turn reflection into unsupervised cessation.
