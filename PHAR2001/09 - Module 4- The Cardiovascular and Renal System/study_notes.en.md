# PHAR2001 Module 4 — The Cardiovascular and Renal System

## Overview

Module 4 follows the blood through four connected problems: lipid accumulating in the arterial wall, pressure rising inside the artery, a clot forming in the artery or vein, and salt and water being retained by the kidney. Each chapter builds the physiology first and then places the drug classes onto it, so every medicine is learned as an intervention at a named step rather than as a list of indications. [S1][S10][S21][S27]

Blood pressure is cardiac output times peripheral resistance; the kidney sets blood volume through sodium handling; the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system links the two; and platelets, clotting factors and fibrin build the thrombus that a ruptured plaque can trigger. Lipid lowering sits upstream because LDL drives the plaque. [V17][V19][V21][V36]

## 1. Pressure, flow and resistance

Arterial pressure is not a single number. The heart ejects intermittently, so pressure peaks in systole and falls while the heart rests in diastole. In the brachial artery and aorta the pulse pressure is wide. As blood travels toward the arterioles the pulse is damped, and by the capillaries pressure is almost constant, below 40 mmHg and falling toward 20 mmHg. [V17]

Mean arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance. Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke volume, so two people can share an output with very different rates and volumes. Peripheral resistance depends on vessel length, blood viscosity and vessel radius. Length and viscosity are not easily changed by drugs; weight loss helps because adipose tissue adds kilometres of vessel to perfuse, and dehydration raises viscosity slightly. Radius is the variable that matters. [V19]

The arterioles are the resistance vessels because they carry smooth muscle and are innervated by the sympathetic nervous system. A small reduction in their radius produces a large fall in flow: halving the diameter of a vessel reduces flow to about one-sixteenth. That is why a drug which dilates arterioles lowers pressure so effectively, and why a plaque that halves an artery's diameter causes such a severe distal deficit. [V19][V5]

Physical factors such as arterial elasticity and lumen width cannot be reversed once atherosclerosis is established, so therapy acts on the physiological factors instead. [V17]

## 2. Short-term and long-term regulation

Short-term regulation is neural and fast. On standing, gravity shifts blood away from the head; baroreceptors detect the change, sympathetic outflow rises, adrenaline and noradrenaline act on alpha-1 receptors to constrict arterioles, and on beta-1 receptors to raise heart rate and contractility. Pressure is restored within seconds. [V21]

Long-term regulation is hormonal and renal. A sustained fall in pressure, or sympathetic stimulation at the kidney, makes the juxtaglomerular apparatus release renin. Renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which is inactive; angiotensin-converting enzyme in the lung converts it to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts arterioles powerfully, signals the posterior pituitary to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH), and signals the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone. ADH conserves water at the collecting duct; aldosterone conserves sodium, and water follows the sodium. The result is a slower but more durable increase in pressure. [V21][V22]

Local mediators adjust flow without waiting for a reflex. Nitric oxide and prostacyclin from intact endothelium dilate the vessel and oppose platelet aggregation, while thromboxane A2 from platelets constricts and promotes aggregation. Autoregulation lets organs such as the kidney constrict the afferent arteriole when pressure is high, though it works poorly at very low pressure. [V21]

## 3. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system

The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system is the body's single control point for raising pressure, because one enzyme release recruits several amplifying mechanisms at once. Renin is secreted when the juxtaglomerular apparatus senses reduced pressure or receives sympathetic stimulation. It cleaves angiotensinogen, an inactive circulating precursor, into angiotensin I. Angiotensin I has little activity of its own. When it passes through the lung it is converted to angiotensin II, the active hormone. [V27][S17]

Angiotensin II raises pressure in at least five ways. It is a potent vasoconstrictor of arterioles, it stimulates ADH release so water is retained, and it stimulates aldosterone release so sodium is retained and potassium excreted. The module notes that further mechanisms exist within the same cascade. Because all of these effects depend on the same cascade, blocking either the converting enzyme or the angiotensin II receptor lowers pressure through several mechanisms simultaneously. [V27][V22]

Aldosterone deserves separate attention because it explains both a drug class and a diuretic class. It is a mineralocorticoid released from the adrenal cortex and nicknamed the salt saver. It binds to cells in the late distal convoluted tubule and the first part of the collecting duct, where it activates sodium–potassium ATPase pumps. Sodium is reabsorbed and potassium is secreted in exchange. [S32][V55]

| Sensing | Juxtaglomerular apparatus | Releases renin when pressure falls or sympathetic tone rises |
| Conversion 1 | Renin acting on angiotensinogen | Produces inactive angiotensin I |
| Conversion 2 | Angiotensin-converting enzyme in the lung | Produces active angiotensin II |
| Vascular action | Angiotensin II on arterioles | Potent vasoconstriction |

## 4. The nephron and the logic of diuretics

A diuretic is a substance that increases urine production and therefore water loss. Apart from the osmotic diuretics, every diuretic in this module works by promoting sodium loss; the sodium remains in the tubule as an osmotically active particle and holds water with it. [V46][V50]

The kidney is small but metabolically expensive. Both kidneys together receive about 22% of cardiac output, and each contains on average about 1.2 million nephrons, the functional units. Renal pyramids number from seven to eighteen per kidney. Cortical nephrons sit near the surface; juxtamedullary nephrons run deep into the medulla and deposit osmotically active particles there. [V48]

Filtration begins at the glomerulus. Blood enters by the afferent arteriole; cells and proteins are retained because they cannot be reabsorbed; fluid, electrolytes and glucose pass into the proximal tubule as filtrate. The afferent and efferent arteriolar diameters can be adjusted to change glomerular pressure and therefore filtration rate. [V48]

Each segment then has a distinct job. The proximal tubule reclaims nearly all filtered glucose and has no diuretic target in this module. The loop of Henle pumps salt into the medulla, building the osmotic gradient that ADH later uses to reclaim the last of the water at the collecting duct. The distal convoluted tubule reabsorbs sodium chloride through a sodium–chloride symporter. The late distal tubule and collecting duct are where aldosterone acts. Any drug acting at one segment can disturb sodium, potassium, calcium and acid–base balance, because each segment contributes to overall electrolyte homeostasis. [V50][V52][V55]

The two main indications are congestive heart failure and hypertension. In heart failure, raised back pressure pushes fluid into the interstitial spaces; removing some of it lets the heart work more efficiently. In hypertension, reducing volume lowers pressure modestly, by about 6–12 mmHg. [S29]

Counselling matters. Patients often drink less because they dislike frequent toileting. That drop in intake triggers aldosterone and ADH release, so fluid is retained and oedema can worsen. Diuretic therapy assumes normal fluid intake. [V50]

## 5. Thiazide diuretics

Thiazides and thiazide-like diuretics are the most widely used diuretics. Hydrochlorothiazide is the standard thiazide; metolazone is a thiazide-like agent. They are mild on their own, but a small dose added to another diuretic or antihypertensive produces a disproportionate increase in diuresis. [S30][V52]

Their target is the sodium–chloride symporter in the distal convoluted tubule. Blocking it leaves sodium chloride in the lumen, water follows osmotically, and sodium is excreted. The increased sodium load reaching the late distal tubule and collecting duct drives sodium–potassium exchange, so potassium is lost in the urine. That is the physiological reason for thiazide-induced hypokalaemia, and it also explains why the deficit is corrected by adding an ACE inhibitor, an ARB or a potassium-sparing diuretic. [V52]

|---|---|

Thiazides raise uric acid, so they are used sparingly in people susceptible to gout. They also raise the risk of type 2 diabetes and increase lipid levels, and are contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation. Conversely, because they raise serum calcium and stimulate osteoblasts, they may be used in osteoporosis even without hypertension or oedema. [V52]

## 6. Loop diuretics

Loop diuretics are the most potent class and are the preferred diuretic in congestive heart failure. Furosemide acts at the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, where it blocks the sodium–potassium–chloride symporter. Because that segment normally reabsorbs a large fraction of filtered sodium and also handles calcium and magnesium, loop diuretics produce a much larger diuresis than thiazides and waste more electrolytes. [S31][V54]

The site explains the adverse effect pattern. Sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium all remain in the lumen, so hypokalaemia, hypocalcaemia and hypomagnesaemia can occur, along with dehydration if the diuresis is excessive. Uric acid rises, so gout is a contraindication. These drugs are reserved for situations needing rapid or large-volume fluid removal, and patients require close monitoring of potassium, fluid status and electrolytes. [V54]

A thiazide is mild and useful in hypertension; a loop diuretic is powerful and useful in decompensated heart failure. Neither is interchangeable with the other. [V52][V54]

## 7. Potassium-sparing diuretics

Thiazides and loop diuretics are potassium-wasting diuretics. The potassium-sparing group opposes that loss by acting at the late distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct, where aldosterone normally drives sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion. There are two mechanisms: aldosterone receptor antagonists such as spironolactone, and direct sodium channel blockers such as amiloride. Both reduce sodium reabsorption at that site, so water is excreted, but potassium is retained. [V55][V56]

The predictable adverse effect is hyperkalaemia, which is dangerous in renal impairment or when the drug is combined with an ACE inhibitor, an ARB or potassium supplements. Spironolactone has additional effects because it blocks androgen receptors as well as aldosterone receptors. In women this can cause dysmenorrhoea; in men it can cause breast development. Those effects are not explained by diuresis and are a common reason for stopping the drug. [V55][S32]

Spironolactone is highly regarded in heart failure, although it reduces deaths less than loop diuretics. In hypertension it is not usually the best choice, but it is very effective in the small subset with excess aldosterone, about 1% of the population. Combining a potassium-sparing with a potassium-wasting diuretic increases efficacy and reduces the potassium disturbance of either drug alone. [S32][V55]

| Class | Site | Main electrolyte effect | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thiazide | Distal convoluted tubule | Potassium loss, calcium retention | Hypertension, mild oedema |
| Loop | Thick ascending limb | Potassium, calcium and magnesium loss | Heart failure, rapid diuresis |
| Potassium-sparing | Late distal tubule and collecting duct | Potassium retention | Heart failure, hyperaldosteronism, combination therapy |

## 8. Hypertension

In Australia hypertension is defined as a blood pressure of at least 140/90 mmHg. The ideal is 120/80 mmHg or lower, and 115/75 mmHg is suggested for younger people because pressure tends to rise with age. Readings above 120/80 but below 140/90 are labelled prehypertensive. That label matters: a meta-analysis of 18 studies including almost half a million patients found that prehypertensive people had a 55% higher risk of cardiovascular disease and a 71% higher risk of stroke than people below 115 mmHg. [V23][V24]

Primary, or essential, hypertension has multiple contributing factors: genetics, a diet high in animal fat and salt, obesity and inactivity. Secondary hypertension has one identifiable cause, such as an adrenaline-secreting tumour, and removing it restores normal pressure. [V23][V24]

Hypertension damages its own control system. High pressure injures the endothelium, injured endothelium accelerates atherosclerosis, atheroma narrows the lumen, and narrowing raises pressure further. The spiral also damages target organs, particularly the retina and the glomeruli, and can produce an aneurysm. In an aneurysm the wall bulges and the pressure inside the bulge rises rather than falls, increasing the risk of rupture. [V23]

Lifestyle is first-line: weight loss, a diet rich in fruit, vegetables, whole grains and fish, low salt, low saturated and trans fat, reduced alcohol, and exercise. Medication is added when lifestyle fails or cannot be maintained, because untreated pressure shortens life. [V23][V32]

## 9. Alpha-1 blockers and the ABCD framework

The antihypertensives are remembered as the ABCD classes. A includes alpha-1 blockers, alpha-2 agonists, ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers; B is beta blockers; C is calcium channel blockers; D is diuretics, especially thiazides. [S15]

Alpha-1 blockers end in `-azosin`; prazosin and doxazosin are the examples. Alpha-1 receptors sit on peripheral blood vessels, the prostate and the urinary sphincter. Sympathetic stimulation normally constricts those vessels and tightens the sphincter, which helps in fight-or-flight but makes urination harder. Blocking the receptor dilates arterioles, opens pre-capillary sphincters and lowers peripheral resistance. It also relaxes the prostate and sphincter, so urinary flow improves in benign prostatic hyperplasia, although the hyperplasia is not cured. [V25][S16]

The characteristic adverse effect is orthostatic, or postural, hypotension. On standing, gravity pools blood in the legs; a healthy person compensates by reflex vasoconstriction within about a second. Alpha-1 blockade prevents that compensation, so pressure falls, causing dizziness and syncope, with a risk of falling and fracture. [V25][V26]

Alpha-1 blockers are no longer preferred for hypertension: they lower pressure less than ACE inhibitors or ARBs and cause more adverse effects. Their best place is the patient who has both hypertension and benign prostatic hyperplasia. [V25][V26]

## 10. ACE inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitors end in `-pril`; ramipril is the example. They inhibit the enzyme that converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II, so the whole downstream cascade is reduced: less vasoconstriction, less ADH, less aldosterone and less angiotensin II activity. ARBs end in `-sartan`; candesartan is the example. They block the angiotensin II receptor on target organs, so angiotensin II may still be produced but cannot act. Both classes are first-line for hypertension and both are indicated in congestive heart failure. [V27][V28][S17]

The ACE inhibitor cough is the clearest adverse effect explained by a second function of the target enzyme. Angiotensin-converting enzyme also breaks down bradykinin, a potent inflammatory mediator. Inhibiting the enzyme raises bradykinin, and in sensitive patients that produces a chronic dry cough. The cough can persist for three months after the drug is stopped, so a patient switched to an ARB may blame the new drug while the old effect is still fading. [V27][V28]

Both classes raise serum potassium because reduced aldosterone means less potassium excretion. Most diuretics do the opposite, so a thiazide is often combined with an ACE inhibitor or ARB; the effects partly cancel, but potassium still needs monitoring. Both are pregnancy category D and contraindicated in pregnancy. [V27][V28]

Candesartan also has a use in prehypertension. Treating people whose pressure sits between 120 and 140 mmHg reduced their risk of progressing to hypertension, and the benefit persisted for a couple of years. Candesartan is available in fixed-dose combination with a thiazide or thiazide-like diuretic, which adds antihypertensive effect and offsets the rise in potassium. [V27]

## 11. Calcium channel blockers

Calcium channel blockers interrupt the movement of calcium through calcium channels. Both cardiac muscle and vascular smooth muscle need intracellular calcium to contract, so partial blockade reduces contractility and vascular tone. They are indicated in hypertension and in coronary artery disease. The class has no common suffix because members act in slightly different ways. [V29][S19]

Amlodipine is among the ten most prescribed medicines in Australia; nifedipine is the calcium channel blocker most often prescribed in pregnancy. Amlodipine is frequently combined in a fixed-dose tablet with an ACE inhibitor, an ARB, a thiazide or a statin. [V29][V31]

Peripheral oedema is one of the most frequent adverse effects, occurring in about 11% of users and more often in women. The mechanism follows from vasodilation: as arterioles dilate, vessel permeability rises, and fluid moves into the interstitial space. Combining amlodipine with an ACE inhibitor reduces the oedema because the ACE inhibitor increases sodium and water excretion. [V29]

Amlodipine is pregnancy category C and is not generally used in pregnancy. Nifedipine is also category C but is often recommended in pregnancy. Methyldopa is the exception discussed here: pregnancy category A, used for pregnancy-induced hypertension and pre-eclampsia. It is an alpha-2 agonist, which looks paradoxical because sympathomimetics usually raise pressure; alpha-2 receptors restrain sympathetic outflow, so activating them lowers it. Methyldopa causes depression, anxiety and impaired concentration, so it is rarely used outside pregnancy. [V29][V31]

## 12. Beta blockers

Beta blockers reduce blood pressure by blocking adrenaline at beta receptors in the heart, lowering heart rate and contractility. First-generation agents are non-selective; second-generation agents such as atenolol are beta-1 selective; third-generation agents such as carvedilol block both beta and alpha receptors. [S15][V30][S18]

Atenolol is still widely prescribed and is indicated for hypertension and certain cardiac conditions, but it is no longer first-line for most hypertension. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers and thiazides are more effective with fewer adverse effects. Carvedilol remains second-line because of its long list of adverse effects, despite its broader receptor blockade. Better alternatives were identified more than ten years before prescribing habits changed, which is why first-line therapy needs periodic review. [V30][S18]

## 13. Combining antihypertensives

The first-line classes are ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers and thiazides. Treatment starts with one at the lowest dose. When it loses effect, adding a second first-line class is generally better than maximising the first, because the combination gives a larger reduction with fewer adverse effects. Pressure has several independent contributors, so one drug addresses one contributor while a combination addresses several. A small dose of thiazide added to an ACE inhibitor or ARB therefore works well: the diuretic reduces volume while the RAAS drug reduces vascular tone and offsets potassium loss. [V32][V33]

## 14. Haemostasis and thrombosis

Haemostasis is the normal stoppage of bleeding; thrombosis is the same process occurring without a vessel break. A thrombus is a blood clot. In the arterial system it can cause a stroke, a myocardial infarction or infarction of another organ; in the venous system it most often forms in the deep veins of the legs and can break off as a pulmonary embolism. [V36][S22]

Haemostasis has four steps. First, the damaged vessel and adjacent tissue release vasoactive substances that constrict the vessel and slow bleeding. Second, platelets form a plug. Third, the clotting cascade produces fibrin, an insoluble mesh that stabilises the plug. Fourth, fibrinolysis dissolves the clot once repair is complete. [V36]

Platelets are fragments, not cells. A megakaryocyte breaks up into one to four thousand platelets, each of which has lost its nucleus and cannot synthesise new proteins. Everything a platelet needs must be packaged before release: actin and myosin to pull the plug together, mitochondria to make ADP, and granules containing cyclooxygenase, the enzyme that converts arachidonic acid to thromboxane A2. Because the platelet cannot make more enzyme, a drug that destroys cyclooxygenase disables that tool for the platelet's whole life, about nine days. [V36][S23]

Platelets are attracted to injury by a change in surface charge and by von Willebrand factor. Intact endothelial cells in the neighbourhood release antiplatelet prostaglandins and nitric oxide, which balance thromboxane A2 and keep aggregation local. In severe atherosclerosis there are few intact endothelial cells, so the balance tips toward aggregation and antiplatelet therapy becomes useful. [V36]

Arterial and venous clots differ in composition. Arterial clots are platelet-rich; venous clots are fibrin-rich with very few platelets. That difference predicts therapy: antiplatelet drugs work in arteries but are ineffective in venous thrombosis, while anticoagulants work in both and are used mainly for veins. [V36][V38]

## 15. Antiplatelet drugs

Aspirin has been available longest. It lowers the incidence of myocardial infarction and improves survival in patients with complicated atherosclerotic lesions. Its acetyl group inhibits cyclooxygenase non-competitively and essentially irreversibly, so thromboxane A2 production falls and platelet aggregation is reduced. The effect lasts the life of the platelet, which is why blood collected within about ten days of an aspirin dose has its platelets discarded. [V38][S23]

Aspirin does not destroy the platelet; it removes one tool. Some platelets in a plug may not have been exposed, so some thromboxane A2 remains, and the drug is best understood as rebalancing pro-aggregatory and anti-aggregatory signals. The antiplatelet dose is much lower than an analgesic dose, about 75–150 mg daily. [V38]

Clopidogrel is replacing aspirin as the preferred antiplatelet drug. It is an ADP receptor antagonist and is selective for the platelet ADP receptor, so it does not block other ADP receptors in the body. ADP released from platelet mitochondria normally changes platelet shape and starts aggregation; blocking the receptor removes that second tool. Clopidogrel can be combined with aspirin for additional benefit because the combination disables two separate activation pathways. [V38][V39]

## 16. Anticoagulants

Anticoagulants are nicknamed blood thinners, but they do not reduce blood viscosity; the term is a misnomer. They act on the clotting cascade, making fibrin formation more difficult. They prevent both arterial and venous clots, although their main use is venous. [V40]

Warfarin is the prototype. Several clotting factors require vitamin K, and as vitamin K participates in those reactions it is oxidised and cannot be reused until vitamin K epoxide reductase recycles it. Warfarin inhibits that enzyme, so the pool of usable vitamin K falls, fewer clotting factors are produced, and clotting is slowed. Warfarin does not eliminate clotting factors entirely, because some clotting is needed to survive everyday minor bleeding. [V40][V41]

Warfarin is difficult to use. Its effect varies greatly between individuals: one patient may be stable on 2 mg daily while another needs 13 mg, and the difference does not track body weight or sex. Patients need frequent INR tests to stay between excessive clotting and excessive bleeding. Many drugs and dietary substances interact, and because vitamin K comes from vegetables, patients are told not to change their vegetable intake abruptly. Warfarin is highly teratogenic, pregnancy category X, and is not recommended even for women of childbearing age who are not planning pregnancy. [V40]

Rivaroxaban is an oral anticoagulant becoming first-line. It has fewer adverse effects and a much shorter interaction list than warfarin. It is pregnancy category C and is used to treat deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, to prevent clots in atrial fibrillation, and after hip or knee surgery. [V40][V41]

Enoxaparin is a low-molecular-weight heparin given by injection. It has predictable absorption, bioavailability and distribution, so it needs no monitoring. It is often substituted for warfarin before travel or surgery, when time zones and diet make warfarin unpredictable. It is pregnancy category C but considered the safest anticoagulant in pregnancy because it does not cross the placenta. It is used for unstable angina with aspirin, for deep vein thrombosis, and as prophylaxis in bedridden and surgical patients. [V40][V41]

## 17. Fibrinolytics

Fibrinolysis is the final step of haemostasis. Plasminogen is converted to plasmin, a proteolytic enzyme that digests the fibrin mesh and dissolves the clot. Fibrinolytic drugs exploit this step, and because they act on a clot that already exists, they are not preventive therapy. They are used during myocardial infarction or pulmonary embolism. [V42][S25]

Streptokinase is the drug to know. It is the most widely prescribed fibrinolytic in the world, with good efficacy and relatively good safety. It is less effective than alteplase, but it causes less intracranial haemorrhage. It is of bacterial origin, so allergic reactions are common, and it must not be given a second time within at least six months. Its therapeutic index is very narrow, meaning the gap between a helpful dose and a harmful one is small. [V42][V43]

## 18. Lipids and lipoproteins

Triglycerides store energy, phospholipids build cell membranes, and cholesterol is the precursor for steroid hormones and bile acids. Problems arise when excess lipid is carried in the wrong lipoprotein. [V1][S2]

Hyperlipidaemia means too much lipid in the blood. Dyslipidaemia is the broader term and is the precise word when the abnormality is a low level of high-density lipoprotein rather than a high level of lipid. The two are often used interchangeably, but a patient with low HDL and normal total cholesterol has dyslipidaemia, not hyperlipidaemia. [V1][V2]

Lipoproteins are transport proteins, not cholesterol itself. LDL carries cholesterol and triglyceride out to the tissues, where it can contribute to plaque; HDL carries cholesterol back to the liver, which can excrete it in bile and faeces. The therapeutic target is therefore low LDL and high HDL, not simply a low total cholesterol. [V1]

Dyslipidaemia may be primary or secondary. Primary disease arises from single or multiple gene mutations that impair triglyceride or LDL clearance or cause underproduction or excessive clearance of HDL; it often responds poorly to diet and lifestyle. Secondary disease is acquired, and it responds well to a healthy diet low in saturated fat, more physical activity, weight loss and stopping smoking, all of which also raise HDL. [V1][V2]

## 19. Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis literally means hardening of the arteries, from the Greek word for hard. The artery does not become usefully hard; it becomes brittle and loses the flexibility that normal arterial flow requires. It is a chronic inflammatory condition that produces fibrosis, scarring and narrowing, and it underlies about half of all deaths in westernised societies. [S3][V3]

Risk factors usually coexist. High LDL and low HDL are direct risks. Hypertension damages the endothelium and initiates scarring. Smoking damages endothelial cells directly. Turbulent flow injures endothelium, and damage is consistently greater at arterial bifurcations. Toxins, including the high glucose of diabetes, bacterial toxins, viruses and immune reactions, add further injury. A xanthoma under the eyelid may signal hypercholesterolaemia but does not reliably predict the amount of atherosclerosis. [V3][V4]

The process begins with endothelial injury. Chronic inflammation follows, and macrophages engulf the lipid carried by LDL. The macrophage becomes a foam cell and accumulates in the subendothelial space. Foam cells and lipid form fatty streaks, fibrosis develops, and the plaque narrows the lumen. The body attempts to wall off the inflamed area with scar tissue, which is why the lesion becomes fibrous. [V3][V4]

A plaque can remain stable for years or become fragile and rupture. When it ruptures, the thrombus can occlude the vessel at that point or break off as an embolus and lodge downstream in a smaller vessel. Statins stabilise plaques and reduce that rupture, which is one reason their benefit exceeds what LDL lowering alone would predict. [V5][V7]

The site of the plaque determines the syndrome. Cerebral arteries produce stroke, or dementia when narrowing develops slowly. Coronary arteries produce myocardial infarction. Peripheral arteries produce decreased libido, mesenteric occlusion, renal artery stenosis or gangrene of the toes. When narrowing develops slowly, collateral vessels can grow around the occlusion and partly preserve flow. [V5][V6]

## 20. Lipid-lowering drugs

Statins are the HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors; every member ends in `-statin`, and atorvastatin and simvastatin are the Australian examples. They inhibit the liver enzyme that synthesises cholesterol, so they reduce production rather than absorption and do not depend on dietary fat intake. They are particularly suited to familial hypercholesterolaemia, in which LDL is overproduced regardless of diet. [V7][V8][S5]

Statins also reduce mortality, increase longevity and reduce atherothrombotic events by more than LDL lowering alone would explain. Their anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic and plaque-stabilising actions appear separate from lipid lowering. Plaque stabilisation prevents the sudden expansion and rupture that causes many acute myocardial infarctions. [V7][V8]

The serious adverse effect is rhabdomyolysis, a rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle that can cause acute kidney failure. It occurs in about four people per 10,000 treated and is much more likely when a statin is combined with another lipid-lowering drug, which is why statins are usually used as monotherapy. Statins are pregnancy category D and contraindicated in pregnancy and liver disease. Baseline liver function tests and periodic monitoring are required because hepatocytes release ALT when they are injured. [V7][V15][V16]

Ezetimibe inhibits absorption of dietary cholesterol, lowering hepatic cholesterol stores and increasing clearance from the blood. Total cholesterol, triglycerides and LDL fall, and HDL usually rises when it is used alone. It is well tolerated, may cause diarrhoea, especially with a statin, and is the one lipid-lowering drug that combines successfully with others; fixed-dose statin–ezetimibe tablets are available. [V9][V10][S6]

Bile acid sequestrants bind bile acids in the small intestine so they cannot be reabsorbed, and the cholesterol-rich bile is excreted. The liver compensates by making more LDL, which can negate much of the benefit. The resins require large daily doses, up to about 20 g, are unpleasant to drink, cause constipation, bind acidic drugs, and bind fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. They are used for chronic diarrhoea and Clostridium difficile infection because they bind bacterial toxins. [V9][V10]

Nicotinic acid is vitamin B3 but is used at 1–3 g daily, about 200 times the amount needed to prevent deficiency. It lowers triglycerides by about 25%, lowers LDL and raises HDL by about 30%. It is contraindicated in liver disease, diabetes, gout and peptic ulcer. Gout is a risk because it reduces uric acid excretion; the ulcer risk follows from direct gastric irritation, which also causes nausea, diarrhoea and heartburn. Flushing is prostaglandin-mediated, appears within about 30 minutes, and can be severe enough to cause hypotension. Slow titration, taking it with meals and prior aspirin reduce flushing, although aspirin may worsen gastric symptoms. Tolerance usually develops within about a month. Nicotinic acid is contraindicated with statins or fibrates because the combination increases the risk of rhabdomyolysis. [V11][V12][S7]

Fibrates end in `-fibrate`; fenofibrate is the example. They were the first lipid-normalising drugs and are now largely superseded. They lower triglycerides and raise HDL, but often raise LDL as well, so they are reserved for people with low HDL. Fenofibrate also reduces the risk of amputation in type 2 diabetes with peripheral artery disease by about 37%. Adverse effects include gallstones, with sudden right upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder blade, nausea and vomiting, and rhabdomyolysis, especially with a statin or nicotinic acid. [V13][V14][S8]

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Statins | Inhibit HMG-CoA reductase | LDL falls; HDL little changed | Rhabdomyolysis, hepatotoxicity | Pregnancy D, liver disease, interactions |
| Ezetimibe | Blocks dietary cholesterol absorption | LDL, triglycerides fall; HDL rises | Diarrhoea | Combines with statins |

The table compares mechanism, lipid effect and harm for the five classes. [V7][V9][V11][V13][S5][S6][S7][S8]

All lipid-lowering drugs can harm the liver, so a baseline liver function test is required and ALT is the most useful marker of hepatocyte injury. None is ideal in pregnancy, because the fetus needs cholesterol for rapid cell division and steroid hormone production; diet and lifestyle should be tried first, but familial hypercholesterolaemia may still require drug treatment. [V15][V16]

## Key takeaways

- Blood pressure equals cardiac output times peripheral resistance; output equals heart rate times stroke volume; arteriolar radius is the most powerful controllable factor. [V19]
- Short-term pressure control is neural and baroreceptor-driven; long-term control is renal and hormonal through renin, ADH and aldosterone. [V21]
- RAAS blockade lowers pressure through vasodilation, reduced ADH, reduced aldosterone and reduced angiotensin II activity at the same time. [V27]
- Diuretics work by leaving sodium in the tubule; the segment determines whether potassium, calcium and magnesium are lost or retained. [V50]
- Thiazides act at the distal convoluted tubule, loop diuretics at the thick ascending limb, and potassium-sparing diuretics at the late distal tubule and collecting duct. [V52][V54][V55]
- ACE inhibitor cough is caused by bradykinin accumulation, not by angiotensin II; ARBs avoid it. [V27]
- Alpha-1 blockers cause orthostatic hypotension because they block the reflex vasoconstriction needed on standing. [V25]
- Antithrombotics are placed by step: antiplatelets at the platelet plug, anticoagulants at the clotting cascade, fibrinolytics at clot dissolution. [V36][V38][V40][V42]
- Arterial clots are platelet-rich and respond to antiplatelet drugs; venous clots are fibrin-rich and require anticoagulants. [V36]
- Statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis and stabilise plaque; ezetimibe is the exception that combines safely with other lipid drugs. [V7][V9]
- Rhabdomyolysis is the shared danger of statins, fibrates and high-dose nicotinic acid, and the risk rises with combination therapy. [V7][V11][V13]

## Common confusions

Hyperlipidaemia and dyslipidaemia are not identical. Hyperlipidaemia is excess lipid in the blood; dyslipidaemia also covers a low HDL, which is a risk even when total cholesterol is normal. [V1]

Blood thinners do not thin blood. Anticoagulants slow the clotting cascade and have no effect on viscosity. [V40]

Arterial and venous clots are not treated the same way. Arterial clots contain many platelets and respond to aspirin or clopidogrel; venous clots contain fibrin and few platelets, so antiplatelet drugs are ineffective and anticoagulants are required. [V36][V38]

Potassium-sparing does not mean potassium-safe. These drugs cause hyperkalaemia, particularly with an ACE inhibitor, an ARB or renal impairment. [V55]

A thiazide and a loop diuretic are not interchangeable. The thiazide is mild and retains calcium; the loop diuretic is potent and wastes calcium, potassium and magnesium. [V52][V54]

A persistent cough after switching from an ACE inhibitor to an ARB may still be the old drug's effect, because bradykinin-related cough can take up to three months to resolve. [V27]
