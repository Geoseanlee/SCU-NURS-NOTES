# PHAR2001 Module 6 — Medications with Selective Toxicity

## Overview

Module 6 is organised around one idea: a drug can damage an invading organism or a cancer cell while leaving the patient's own cells comparatively intact. That property is selective toxicity, and it separates this module from the earlier ones, where a medicine usually acts on a human receptor or enzyme and the therapeutic and adverse effects arise from the same tissue. [V1] The compulsory portion covers the principle and general antibacterial material; the longer optional chapter adds specific antibiotics, antifungals, antivirals and anticancer chemotherapy. [S1]

Antibiotics disrupt normal flora, select resistant bacteria and provoke hypersensitivity. Antifungals are limited by the closeness of fungal and human cells, antivirals by the way viruses hide inside host cells, and anticancer drugs by the fact that they attack proliferation itself. Knowing where the selectivity comes from explains both the benefit and the characteristic harm of each group. [V4][V6][V8][V10]

## What you should be able to do

By the end of this module you should be able to:

- Explain selective toxicity as a comparison between microbial and host structures, and name the difference each class exploits. [V1][V2]
- Distinguish antiseptics, disinfectants and sterilants, and explain why only antiseptics are applied to skin. [V2]
- Define bactericidal and bacteriostatic action, narrow and broad spectrum, and time-dependent and dose-dependent killing. [V2]
- Compare the four antibacterial target areas: cell wall, folate, protein synthesis and DNA. [S9][S10][V14][V15]
- Explain how beta-lactamase and incomplete courses produce resistance, how culture and sensitivity guides a change of antibiotic, and predict the adverse effects and counselling that follow from disruption of the normal flora. [S9][V4]
- Compare azoles, allylamines and polyenes by target, route and toxicity, and explain why liver and kidney function are monitored. [V6][V16]
- Explain how antivirals exploit docking, fusion, integration and release, and why latency limits cure. [V8][V17][V18]
- Relate the side effects of anticancer chemotherapy to the normal tissues that divide rapidly. [V10][V19]

## 1. The principle of selective toxicity

Selective toxicity means that a drug harms the pathogen or cancer cell much more than the patient's cells. Most medicines in earlier modules bind a receptor or inhibit an enzyme belonging to the patient, which is why their wanted and unwanted effects often come from the same action. Anti-infective drugs instead attack a microbial structure or process that human cells do not possess or do not use in the same way. [V1]

The clearest example is the bacterial cell. Bacteria are prokaryotes: single-celled organisms without a membrane-bound nucleus. Human cells are eukaryotes, and most bacteria also have a cell wall, which human cells do not. Penicillins and cephalosporins attack wall synthesis, so the bacterium loses the structure that holds it together against osmotic pressure. Human cells, which have a membrane but no wall, are not directly affected. [V2][V4]

Antifungal therapy is harder. Fungal cells are eukaryotic, like human cells, so there are far fewer differences to exploit. The principal difference taught here is the membrane sterol: human membranes contain cholesterol, while fungal membranes contain ergosterol. Most antifungals target ergosterol or its synthesis, which gives useful selectivity but not the wide margin of a bacterial cell wall. Toxicity therefore tends to affect the liver and kidneys. [S4][V6]

Viruses present a different problem. A virus is not a cell. It has no cell membrane and no cytoplasm, only a protein coat, sometimes called a capsid or envelope, surrounding a strand of RNA or DNA. It must enter a host cell and use that cell's machinery to replicate, so the usable targets are virus-specific steps such as docking, fusion, integration and release rather than a structure of its own. [V8]

Cancer is the limiting case, because the malignant cell is the patient's own cell. Anticancer drugs usually stop cells progressing through the cell cycle. That arrests the tumour, but it also stops every normal tissue dividing rapidly at the same time. Selective toxicity here is a difference in rate of division, not in species, and the adverse effects are predictable from that fact. [V10][V19]

| Difference exploited | Example | Consequence for selectivity |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial cell wall, absent in human cells | Penicillins and cephalosporins | High selectivity; wall loss causes osmotic lysis |
| Bacterial folate synthesis | Sulphonamides | Bacteriostatic control, with liver toxicity and hypersensitivity still possible |
| Fungal ergosterol in place of cholesterol | Azoles, allylamines, polyenes | Useful but narrow margin; liver and kidney toxicity |
| Virus-specific replication steps | Antivirals acting on docking, fusion, integration, release | Selective in principle; latency prevents elimination |
| Rapid cell division | Anticancer chemotherapy | Low selectivity; marrow, gut, skin and hair are damaged |

The table collects the five target differences that organise the module. [V2][S10][V6][V8][V10]

The word chemotherapy was first used in the early 1900s for an arsenic compound used to treat syphilis, a bacterial infection. [V2]

## 2. The vocabulary of antimicrobial therapy

Antiseptics, disinfectants and sterilants are distinguished by where they are used and how complete their action is. An antiseptic is the only one applied to the body, and it is used topically. A solution of about 70% alcohol or about 6% hydrogen peroxide reduces the microbial population and is applied to skin before a procedure such as taking blood; cetrimide is an example. A sterilant destroys all life forms on a surface, including spores. A disinfectant is a sterilant that is slightly less effective, perhaps killing 99% of microorganisms. [V2]

Bactericidal and bacteriostatic describe what the drug does to the bacteria. The suffix -cidal means killing; -static means holding in check. Cell wall agents are usually bactericidal: damage to the wall lets fluid pour into the cell because of the osmotic difference across it, and the bacterium bursts. Bacteriostatic drugs keep the population from growing while the immune system gains control. [V2]

Spectrum describes the range of organisms a drug can affect, not its strength. A narrow-spectrum antibiotic may be extremely effective against a small range, and may be chosen because it targets a feature that a resistant organism has developed. A broad-spectrum antibiotic covers a wide range, often both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. [V2]

Time-dependent and dose-dependent antibiotics differ in what determines killing. A time-dependent antibiotic needs a certain time above a minimum concentration; raising the concentration further does not kill faster, it only adds host toxicity. A dose-dependent antibiotic responds to a higher dose with a quicker effect, so the aim is high peaks in the blood. [V2]

Synergism means two drugs together produce a greater effect than either alone. The lecture illustrates it with blood pressure drugs that lower pressure by 4 mmHg and 5 mmHg separately but by about 15 mmHg together. Antibiotic combinations are used for the same reason: two agents acting by different mechanisms can produce a rapid, profound effect when one alone would work slowly or not at all. Speed matters because slow killing gives bacteria time to develop resistance mutations. [V2]

## 3. Bacteria as a drug target

Antibacterial drugs are grouped by mechanism because the grouping makes the set easier to remember and reconstruct in an examination. The module mentions grouping by time and dose dependence, but mechanism organises the specific agents into four target areas: cell wall synthesis, DNA function, protein synthesis and metabolic processes such as folate synthesis. [S9][S10][V14]

The bacterial cell wall is the most attractive target because it has no human counterpart. A bacterium lives with a large osmotic difference across its surface, so the wall prevents it from swelling and bursting. Interfering with wall synthesis, or destabilising the wall once made, is incompatible with bacterial life, which is why wall-active drugs tend to be bactericidal. [V2][V4]

## 4. Cell wall inhibitors: penicillins and cephalosporins

The cell wall inhibitors act while the bacterium is reproducing. The bacterium cannot make the wall it needs, so the progeny cannot make the wall required to survive. The two classes named in the module notes are the penicillins, recognised by the suffix -cillin, and the cephalosporins, recognised by the prefix cef- or ceph-. Both contain a beta-lactam ring, the active portion of the molecule. [S9]

Resistance commonly arises through an enzyme. Bacteria synthesise beta-lactamase, which attacks the beta-lactam ring before the drug reaches the wall. A beta-lactamase inhibitor such as clavulanic acid can be given with the beta-lactam antibiotic to protect the ring. [S9]

Cephalosporins have a wider spectrum of activity and a longer half-life than penicillins. They also have a different adverse-effect profile, with effects on the gastrointestinal tract and kidneys, and cross-reactivity between beta-lactam classes is a prescribing consideration. A patient with a documented penicillin allergy may therefore react to a cephalosporin. [V14]

Hypersensitivity is the signature harm of penicillins. Because most antibiotics were originally derived from toxins produced by fungi, the immune system can recognise them, and penicillin can produce any of the four types of hypersensitivity reaction. About 10% of people given penicillin have some reaction, and about 0.01% have life-threatening anaphylaxis, a type I response at its most extreme. Patients at risk also have a contraindication to a number of other specific antibiotics. [V4]

The lecture stresses the importance of identifying someone with a true allergy rather than a simple intolerance, because a person who develops these allergic-type reactions has a contraindication to a number of other antibiotics. [V4]

## 5. Folate inhibitors: the sulphonamides

Sulphonamides, including sulphamethoxazole, block the bacterium's ability to synthesise folic acid, which it needs to reproduce. The drug does not kill outright; it is bacteriostatic, holding the population in check while the immune system gains control. This is the clearest example of a drug targeting a metabolic pathway rather than a structural component. [S10]

The important adverse effects are serious liver toxicity and hypersensitivity reactions. Because the drug is only suppressive, the host's immune system still has to clear the infection. The combination of a narrow therapeutic target and hepatic harm is a reminder that selective toxicity is never absolute: the drug can injure the liver regardless of its antimicrobial mechanism. [V15]

## 6. Protein synthesis inhibitors

The protein synthesis group includes tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides and lincosamides. The module names these classes and their characteristic harms rather than requiring the detailed ribosomal binding site of each. The shared principle is that bacterial protein synthesis differs enough from human protein synthesis to be a usable target, but each class still carries its own pattern of toxicity. [V15]

Tetracyclines bind calcium, which explains their best-known harm: tooth discolouration in children. [V15]

Aminoglycosides carry two named toxicities: ototoxicity, meaning damage to hearing or balance, and nephrotoxicity, meaning kidney damage. [V15]

Macrolides are alternatives for people allergic to beta-lactams. That place in therapy matters: the macrolide is not simply another broad-spectrum drug, it is the substitute when the first-line beta-lactam cannot be used. Lincosamides are listed with this group, and the module does not develop their individual profile. [V15]

## 7. DNA and RNA targets

The antibacterial lectures describe DNA unwinding as their nucleic acid target; RNA enters the module through viruses, whose genome is a strand of RNA or DNA. [V14][V15][V8]

Fluoroquinolones prevent bacterial DNA from unwinding, which stops the processes that depend on separating the two strands. The module groups this with the other ways of killing bacteria rather than with the protein synthesis inhibitors. Selectivity depends on the bacterial DNA process differing sufficiently from the human counterpart. [V14][V15]

Nitroimidazoles are effective against certain parasitic infections. They sit at the edge of the antibacterial chapter because the module uses them to show that the same selective-toxicity logic extends beyond bacteria to other invading organisms. [V15]

## 8. Resistance

Resistance is selection, not a deliberate change by the bacterium. Bacteria replicate at an enormous rate, and spontaneous mutations arise continuously. An antibiotic creates conditions in which a mutation that protects the organism becomes an advantage, so that organism survives and proliferates, and all of its progeny carry the same protection. [V4]

Underdosing and poor compliance accelerate this. If a patient stops an antibiotic before recovering, the microorganisms have all been exposed, and the ones beginning to make molecular changes that protect them continue to make those changes. The progeny of those survivors are then resistant. The lecture treats this as a direct consequence of incomplete exposure rather than a separate phenomenon. [V4]

Beta-lactamase is the concrete example. The enzyme attacks the beta-lactam ring before it can affect the wall, and the answer is to combine the antibiotic with a beta-lactamase inhibitor such as clavulanic acid. This is resistance overcome at the level of the formulation rather than by changing the target. [S9]

Combination therapy is the other strategy. Two antibiotics acting by different mechanisms can produce a synergistic effect, kill more quickly and reduce the opportunity for resistant mutants. The lecture links this to the earlier definition of synergism: rapid killing deprives the population of the time it needs to accumulate protective mutations. [V2]

Culture and sensitivity is the definitive guide. A broad-spectrum antibiotic may be started first, but once the organism and its sensitivities are known the patient should be changed to the most effective agent. The urinary tract infection is the worked example: empirical broad-spectrum treatment first, then a switch when the laboratory result arrives. Prescribing an antibiotic to which the organism is proven sensitive is one practical measure the module lists for limiting resistance. [V4]

Superinfection is a separate problem from acquired resistance, although the two are linked. A superinfection is the unchecked growth of a pathogenic microorganism until it constitutes a further infection, and bacterial superinfections are often resistant because they arise while the patient is already taking an antibiotic. [V4]

## 9. Antibiotic harm and counselling

The overview lesson frames the chapter around side effects, resistance and antibiotic selection. [S3]

Antibiotic therapy disrupts the natural flora on the skin, in the gastrointestinal tract and in the vagina. That flora is a mixed community of bacteria and fungi whose toxins keep each other in check. Killing a large part of the bacterial population removes that restraint and allows pathogenic bacteria and fungi to proliferate. [V4]

The most common consequence is antibiotic-associated diarrhoea. It is especially severe when antibiotics allow an overgrowth of Clostridium difficile, whose toxins injure the gut lining. The first treatment step is to discontinue the antibiotic therapy. [V4][V5]

The diarrhoea raises a counselling point that is easy to get wrong. If toxins cause the diarrhoea, an antidiarrhoeal such as loperamide is not appropriate, because the toxins have to be expelled. Suppressing the natural process can have serious consequences. [V4]

Fungal overgrowth is more common than bacterial superinfection. Candida can appear in the mouth or vagina, and in an immunocompromised patient it can extend through the intestines. It is common enough that some women are advised to take a prophylactic antifungal alongside the antibiotic they need. [V4]

Hypersensitivity is the third major harm, and the penicillin figures above show why every course begins with an allergy history. The practical counselling set is therefore: take the antibiotic as prescribed and for the full course, because stopping early selects resistance; expect that fungal infection may need separate treatment; and remember that an antidiarrhoeal is not appropriate when toxins cause the diarrhoea. [V4]

## 10. Antifungal agents

Fungi are diverse, with more than one million species identified. They are part of the normal flora, and two mechanisms keep them in check: the immune system, and competition from other organisms, including bacteria whose toxins suppress fungal growth. When control fails, the resulting infection is a mycosis. [V6][S4]

Mycoses are divided into four groups by the tissue involved. A systemic or deep mycosis is almost invariably fatal if untreated; the causative agent usually lives in soil, enters through the respiratory tract and disseminates through the body. A subcutaneous mycosis is also usually fatal without treatment, remains fairly common in tropical countries, enters through a wound and is disfiguring because it infects living tissue. A cutaneous mycosis involves the epidermis, hair or nails, and athlete's foot is the example; the fungus lives on the dead or dying top layers and does not go deeper. A superficial mycosis affects the outermost layers of the epidermis or hair. [V6]

Opportunistic infection is a separate category. Candida albicans normally resides in the gastrointestinal tract and vagina as part of the flora. Broad-spectrum antibiotics, immunosuppression, or severe diabetes with high blood sugar alter the environment in favour of fungal growth, and Candida can get out of control. [V6]

The main difference from the human cell is the membrane sterol: cholesterol in human cells, ergosterol in fungal cells. Azoles such as clotrimazole and allylamines such as terbinafine inhibit an enzyme needed for ergosterol synthesis; they also reduce the enzymes that metabolise hydrogen peroxide, so peroxide accumulates to toxic levels inside the fungal cell. Polyenes such as amphotericin B and nystatin bind ergosterol in the membrane and disrupt normal membrane permeability. [S4][V6]

| Class | Drugs named | Target and mechanism | Route and use | Main adverse effects and cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Azoles | Clotrimazole, fluconazole, ketoconazole | Inhibit an enzyme in ergosterol synthesis; peroxide accumulates | Clotrimazole topical; fluconazole oral or IV; ketoconazole cream or shampoo | Liver and kidney toxicity with systemic use; topical steroid combinations thin the skin |
| Allylamines | Terbinafine | Inhibit ergosterol synthesis | Oral, used as a last option for nail infection | Class concern is liver and kidney toxicity; monitor |
| Polyenes | Amphotericin B, nystatin | Bind ergosterol and disrupt membrane permeability | Nystatin oral suspension or tablet, 0% absorbed | Nystatin stays in the gut; systemic polyenes carry toxicity risk |

The table groups the antifungals by target and route. [V6][V16]

Clotrimazole is the most effective topical agent for onychomycosis. It can eliminate the fungus in up to 80% of cases, compared with about 50% for ciclopirox, but it must be used for about six months because the infected nail has to grow out. Oral terbinafine, which inhibits sterol synthesis, is the last-resort option and carries the same long commitment. [V6]

Topical clotrimazole is often supplied with a corticosteroid. The steroid thins the skin, and because it gives rapid relief people tend to continue the combination long after the antifungal alone would be appropriate, which eventually causes more harm than good. [V6]

Systemic antifungals can damage the liver and kidneys, so liver function tests, urea, electrolytes and creatinine are checked, and a full blood count may be added to confirm that the patient is coping. Adequate hydration is encouraged, and other nephrotoxic or hepatotoxic substances are avoided. With a nephrotoxic drug this means avoiding non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and aspirin; with a hepatotoxic drug it means avoiding alcohol and paracetamol. [V6][V7]

## 11. Antiviral agents

Knowledge of viruses was limited until the HIV scare of the mid-1980s, and antiviral pharmacology has advanced faster than any other area since. First-line therapy is expected to change before a student reaches clinic, so the module teaches principles and one representative drug for each main virus rather than a long list. [V8][S5][S13]

A virus is an infectious agent, not a cell. The complete infectious particle is the virion. It consists of a protein coat, called a capsid, sometimes surrounded by an envelope, and inside is a strand of RNA or DNA. It has no cell membrane and no cytoplasm. To replicate, it docks onto a host cell, fuses with it, injects its genetic material, then uses the host to make new viral particles, which eventually kills the cell. [V8]

Attachment is specific. Proteins on the virion surface must match proteins on the host cell surface, so viruses that infect epithelial cells, such as the wart viruses, cannot infect a T lymphocyte, and HIV, which infects T lymphocytes, cannot infect epithelial cells. Antivirals target docking, fusion, integration of viral genetic material into host DNA, or release of new particles. [V8]

A single remaining virus can restart the infection, and some viruses become dormant inside cells. Varicella zoster causes chickenpox first and shingles later because it hides in the dorsal root ganglion, where the cell bodies of peripheral nerves sit; when stress weakens immune control, the virus can reactivate. HIV hides in immune cells. [V8]

Influenza is the worked example of a release inhibitor. Oseltamivir is a neuraminidase inhibitor: it prevents the virus from detaching from the host cell, so it cannot go on to infect other cells. Treatment must start within 24 hours of symptom onset because the virus replicates rapidly early in the illness. Oseltamivir may also be used as prophylaxis in people who cannot tolerate the influenza vaccine. [V17]

HIV uses reverse transcriptase and integrase to integrate its genetic material into host DNA. Combination therapy is essential, because a single drug allows the virus to mutate and become resistant. Herpes viruses hide in the nervous system and reactivate during stress, producing cold sores or shingles; acyclovir selectively targets viral DNA replication, and early treatment prevents severe complications. [V18]

The hepatitis viruses have been transformed by the newest direct-acting antivirals, which can achieve high cure rates with minimal side effects. They are not suitable for pregnant women. The pace of change is why the module emphasises principles over memorised regimens. [V18]

## 12. Anticancer chemotherapy

Cancer is a set of cells that have begun to grow without regulation. Normal cells are tightly regulated so that they do not proliferate out of control; when that control is lost, cancer can develop. Healthy cells are programmed to die, but cancer cells do not, so they keep passing through the cell cycle, increase in number and crowd out normal cells. [S6][V10]

The most common way an anticancer drug works is to stop cells progressing through the phases of that cycle. If the tumour cells do not mutate into a form that escapes the drug, tumour growth is arrested. The problem is that normal cells are cycling at the same time. Epithelial cells are constantly replaced, the intestinal lining regenerates every five to seven days, hair is continually renewed, and the bone marrow produces red cells, white cells and platelets. All are hit by a drug that indiscriminately attacks rapidly dividing cells. [V10]

Skin and epithelial effects include weak skin, easy bruising and easy bleeding. Patients should avoid accidental knocks, be careful with sharp objects such as knives, and use an electric shaver rather than a razor. Gastrointestinal effects include nausea, pain and anorexia. Here anorexia means a distaste for food, not anorexia nervosa, the disorder in which a person chooses not to eat. Food makes the patient feel ill, and nutritionists therefore suggest avoiding favourite foods during treatment, because the nausea can create a lasting aversion after treatment ends. [V10]

Reduced red cells produce anaemia, which develops over weeks and presents as tiredness; patients need advice on coping with fatigue and on rising from a seated position. Reduced white cells reduce immune defence, so patients should avoid large groups, avoid people who are unwell, avoid contact with animal manure and report any infection immediately. Reduced platelets increase bleeding risk, which compounds the skin and gut effects, so patients should avoid razors, use stool softeners to reduce bleeding risk in the gut, and avoid rectal thermometers because they can damage the rectal lining. [V10][V11]

| Drug | Target | Characteristic harm | Counselling focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doxorubicin | Dividing cells, including bone marrow | Dilated cardiomyopathy; marrow suppression | Understand cardiomyopathy risk; infection and bleeding precautions |
| Cisplatin | Dividing cells, including bone marrow | Hearing loss; marrow suppression | Understand hearing loss risk; infection and bleeding precautions |
| Tamoxifen | Oestrogen receptors at cancer-prone sites | Blocks oestrogen action at those sites | Understand that it prevents division rather than killing cells |

The table lists the three anticancer drugs named in the module and the harms attached to each. [V19]

Tamoxifen works differently. It is a selective oestrogen receptor modulator: it blocks oestrogen at sites prone to cancer development while allowing oestrogen effects elsewhere. It prevents cancer cells dividing without causing cell death. [V19]

## 13. Polypharmacy: the reflective chapter

The final chapter is a reflective piece about over-reliance on medications, and it has no notes or slides. [S15]

The documentary material makes the scale concrete. Up to 40% of elderly people take five or more medications every day, Each year over 140,000 Australians are hospitalised because of problems with their medicines, and adverse drug reactions are described as probably the fourth or fifth greatest killer after heart disease, cancer and stroke. [V20]

The reason is structural as well as clinical. Patients may see different doctors and use different pharmacies, and no single person oversees the whole list. Clinical trials test individual drugs, exclude people on multiple medications, recruit young healthy volunteers and run for short periods, so the long-term combination is never tested. The drug burden index estimates total exposure to high-risk drugs from the number of agents and the dose, and a Medicare-funded home medicines review lets an accredited pharmacist review all of a patient's medicines at home. [V20]

Adding a drug is easier than reviewing or stopping one, and stopping can produce improvement. The artistic reflection that closes the unit makes the same point about fear, trust and the difficulty of deciding which medicine is still earning its place. [S15][V20][V21]

**Source gaps:** The module does not give doses, half-lives, or detailed pharmacokinetics for the drugs it names, and it does not develop the ribosomal or enzymatic binding sites of most antibacterial classes. The note therefore stays at the level of mechanism, target and named adverse effect. Background beyond that level is not examinable from this module.

## Key takeaways

- Selective toxicity is a property of the drug-host pair, not of the drug alone. [V1]
- Bacteria offer a cell wall and prokaryotic organisation; fungi are eukaryotic and share sterol biology, so antifungals are less selective; viruses are not cells; cancer cells are the host's own cells. [V2][V6][V8][V10]
- Antiseptics are for skin, disinfectants and sterilants for surfaces; bactericidal kills, bacteriostatic holds in check; spectrum is range, not potency. [V2]
- Penicillins and cephalosporins are beta-lactams that block cell wall synthesis; beta-lactamase destroys the ring and clavulanic acid protects it. [S9]
- Sulphonamides are bacteriostatic folate inhibitors; tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides and lincosamides block protein synthesis; fluoroquinolones block DNA unwinding; nitroimidazoles cover certain parasites. [S10][V15]
- Resistance arises by mutation and selection, and is accelerated by underdosing and incomplete courses; culture and sensitivity, synergy and narrow-spectrum reserve all limit it. [V4][V2]
- Antibiotics disrupt flora, causing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, Clostridium difficile colitis and Candida overgrowth; antidiarrhoeals are inappropriate because toxins must be expelled. [V4]
- Antifungals target ergosterol synthesis or bind ergosterol; systemic use requires liver and kidney monitoring and avoidance of other nephrotoxic or hepatotoxic drugs. [V6]
- Antivirals target docking, fusion, integration or release; latency means cure is often impossible, and timing is critical for influenza. [V8][V17]
- Anticancer drugs stop the cell cycle, so marrow, gut, skin and hair are damaged; doxorubicin adds cardiomyopathy and cisplatin adds hearing loss; tamoxifen modulates oestrogen receptors instead. [V10][V19]

## Common confusions

**Bactericidal versus bacteriostatic is not a ranking of strength.** The suffixes describe the action: -cidal means killing, usually by destabilising the wall so the cell bursts, while -static means holding the population until immunity catches up. A bacteriostatic drug can be the correct choice for a specific infection. [V2][S10]

**Broad spectrum does not mean more effective.** Spectrum is the range of organisms covered. A narrow-spectrum drug may be more effective against its target and is often reserved for resistant organisms. [V2]

**A superinfection is not the same as resistance.** Resistance is a property of an organism that survives a drug; a superinfection is a new, additional infection that arises during treatment, often with an organism that is resistant because it grew while the antibiotic was present. [V4]

**Anorexia in cancer care is not anorexia nervosa.** Here anorexia means a distaste for food caused by treatment; anorexia nervosa is a disorder in which the person chooses not to eat. The counselling differs completely. [V10]

**Fungi are eukaryotes, so antifungal selectivity is not antibacterial selectivity.** The cholesterol-versus-ergosterol difference is real but narrow, which is why systemic antifungals are monitored through liver and kidney function. [V6]
