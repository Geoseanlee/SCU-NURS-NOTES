# PHAR2001 Module 5 — Analgesics and Psychotropic Drugs

## Overview

Module 5 has two halves that meet in the nervous system. The first is analgesia: how injured tissue generates pain and where each analgesic class interrupts the signal. The second is psychopharmacology: drugs that change mood, thought, perception and behaviour. Both groups look as though they act in the brain, but most analgesics act along the pathway that carries pain, while psychotropic drugs act on the neurotransmitters that shape mood and perception. [S1][S12]

The clinical weight is large. Twelve of the top 100 most prescribed medicines are antidepressants, and 20% of Australians live with chronic pain, now regarded as a disorder in its own right. [S1][S2] A nurse who knows where a drug acts can predict whether it will help this pain and which adverse effect to watch for. [V1][V2]

## What you should be able to do

By the end of this module you should be able to:

- Explain why pain is protective, and distinguish acute, subacute and chronic pain by the changes each produces along the pathway. [S2][V1]
- Trace a pain impulse from peripheral neuron to cortex, and locate the gate where opioids, cannabinoids and antidepressants act. [V4]
- Explain how prostaglandins and cytokines produce pain and fever, and where NSAIDs interrupt synthesis. [V3][V36]
- Compare NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors and paracetamol by mechanism, indication, main adverse effect and caution. [S5][V6]
- Explain each major opioid adverse effect from the receptor or organ responsible, and justify naloxone use. [S9][V10]
- Distinguish nociceptive from neuropathic pain, and justify why an opioid is a poor choice for neuropathic pain. [S11][V14]
- Compare SSRI, SNRI, TCA, MAOI and atypical antidepressants by mechanism and adverse-effect burden. [S18][V26][V28]
- Relate typical and atypical antipsychotics to the four dopamine pathways, and predict extrapyramidal, prolactin and metabolic effects. [S21][V30][V32]

## 1. Pain as a protective mechanism

The International Association for the Study of Pain describes pain as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience. Impulses travel from the tissues to the spinal cord, thalamus and sensory cortex; the emotional component is generated in the limbic system, which is why pain can be distressing out of proportion to the visible injury. [V1]

Pain protects tissue. A person with longstanding diabetes may not feel a cut, a blister or early infection, and may lose a foot as a result, yet still have severe burning pain in the feet from damaged nerves. Loss of protective sensation and neuropathic pain therefore coexist. [V1][V14]

Pain is also subjective. A rugby player can fracture a bone and keep playing, then notice the pain when the game ends; the same person may find a blood test very painful because of earlier experience with needles. Two patients with the same injury can respond very differently to the same analgesic, so treatment is matched to the individual as well as to the type of pain. [V1]

Acute pain begins suddenly and usually follows tissue damage plus inflammation. When it persists it enters the subacute phase, the phase that matters most, because aggressive treatment here can prevent the changes that make pain chronic. In chronic pain the body's own analgesic system becomes less effective, neuronal cell bodies undergo inflammatory change that increases transmission, and the neurotransmitters used more often have a much longer half-life, so each message lasts longer. [V1][S2]

## 2. The pain pathway and the gate

A painful stimulus is detected by the peripheral sensory neuron, the first-order neuron, whose cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglion. It carries the impulse to the spinal cord and synapses with a second-order neuron, which ascends to the thalamus; from there the signal passes to the cortex and limbic system. The synapse between the two neurons is the gate, where the signal can be amplified or suppressed before it reaches consciousness. [V4]

A-delta fibres are myelinated and carry sharp, acute pain; unmyelinated C-fibres carry dull, aching pain. This is why a stubbed toe is sharp first and throbs afterwards, and why rubbing an injury helps: touch input competes with pain input at the gate, so the pain message is partly overridden. [V4]

The body has its own analgesic system. Descending pathways release endogenous opioids and other transmitters onto the gate, reducing transmission. Opioid drugs mimic these transmitters, cannabinoids act at the same region, and tricyclic antidepressants strengthen descending inhibitory control. This is why opioids and antidepressants can relieve pain without repairing tissue. [V4][V14]

| Site | Drug class | Action there |
|---|---|---|
| Injured tissue | NSAIDs, COX-2 inhibitors | Less prostaglandin synthesis, so less peripheral sensitisation |
| Peripheral nerve ending | NSAIDs | Lower propagation of impulses read as pain |
| Dorsal horn gate | Opioids, cannabinoids, TCAs, SNRIs | Less transmission to the second-order neuron; stronger descending inhibition |
| Brain and limbic system | Opioids | Reduced perception and emotional component |

Combinations act additively when they hit different points, which is why the table is also a guide to rational pairing. [S4][V4][V36][V14]

## 3. Inflammation, prostaglandins and fever

Inflammation is the local reaction to cell injury: capillaries dilate, leukocytes move in, and the area becomes red, hot and painful. It occurs in the interstitial space and sensitises nerve endings, so a normally harmless stimulus becomes painful. [V3]

Chemical mediators do the work. Mast cells use cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) to make prostaglandin D2, which starts and sustains inflammation. Macrophages use the same enzyme to make prostaglandin E2, which binds neurons and increases impulse propagation, producing pain. Blocking COX-2 therefore reduces both the inflammatory cascade and the neural signalling experienced as pain. [V36]

Fever is the same process projected to the brain. Inflammatory cytokines act locally, travel to the bone marrow to increase immune cell production, and in sufficient quantity cross the blood–brain barrier and act on the hypothalamus. There, COX-2 makes prostaglandin E2, raising the thermoregulatory set point; the hypothalamus then raises metabolic rate and triggers shivering, both generating heat. NSAIDs inhibit COX-2 at both sites, so the set point returns toward normal. [V36]

Fever is not simply an error to suppress. Higher temperature makes it harder for bacteria to survive and increases the immune response, so a modest fever may be allowed to run while cold compresses protect people susceptible to convulsions. Very high fever, or moderate fever in someone with a convulsion history, is treated promptly. [V36]

Corticosteroids sit at the top of the anti-inflammatory hierarchy. Prednisone produces a profound reduction in inflammation but carries significant adverse effects, which is why it is reserved for conditions that need that power. Non-steroidal agents act further down the same cascade, on the enzymes that make inflammatory prostaglandins. [V3][S3]

## 4. NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) reduce pain and inflammation by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase enzymes that produce inflammatory prostaglandins. Ibuprofen, aspirin and naproxen are the module's examples. They act at the site of injury rather than in the brain, so they suit pain with a peripheral inflammatory component. [S5][V36][V5]

Therapeutic effect and harm come from the same mechanism. COX-1 produces prostaglandins that protect the stomach lining and prostaglandins that maintain renal blood flow. Inhibiting COX-1 removes that protection, so the stomach becomes vulnerable to peptic ulceration and the kidney to toxicity, especially when renal blood flow is already marginal. Chronic use is also associated with hypertension and myocardial infarction. [V5]

Aspirin shares the analgesic and anti-inflammatory mechanism but not the same cardiovascular signal: the lecture notes that aspirin does not carry the increased myocardial infarction risk seen with other NSAIDs. Celecoxib and parecoxib were designed to spare COX-1 and therefore the stomach and kidney. They succeeded, but carry a high risk of serious cardiovascular complications and are no more effective than traditional NSAIDs, so the intended advantage did not produce a safer drug overall. [V5]

| Group | Target | Benefit | Main harm | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional NSAID (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) | COX-1 and COX-2 | Analgesia, anti-inflammatory, antipyretic | Peptic ulcer, renal toxicity, hypertension, myocardial infarction (aspirin excepted) | Lowest effective dose for the shortest time |
| COX-2 inhibitor (celecoxib, parecoxib) | COX-2 preferentially | Analgesia with less gastric irritation | Serious cardiovascular events | Avoid with cardiovascular risk |
| Paracetamol | Central, not fully understood | Analgesia and antipyresis | Hepatotoxicity in overdose | No anti-inflammatory effect |

The table compares the groups. [V5][V6]

## 5. Paracetamol

Paracetamol is called acetaminophen, and in some countries Tylenol, which matters because overseas clients may not recognise the Australian name. Unlike the NSAIDs it has no anti-inflammatory effect. It reduces fever and relieves pain, but the relief is only about half of what an NSAID produces. Its exact mechanism is not understood; the current view is that it acts centrally rather than at inflamed tissue. [S6][V6]

Guidelines place paracetamol first-line for mild to moderate pain, although some researchers question how well it works. First-line is not a claim of potency; it reflects a favourable safety profile at therapeutic doses, which is why exceeding the dose is so dangerous. [V6]

A weak analgesic does not become stronger with a larger dose. Increasing the dose adds liver toxicity rather than analgesia, and paracetamol toxicity is the leading cause of acute liver damage in Australia, and a leading cause in the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada. Half-life varies between people, patients who get no relief take more, many products combine paracetamol with an opioid so the opioid can be escalated without the patient realising paracetamol is rising too, and the drug is a common means of deliberate self-harm. [S6][V6]

Toxicity is insidious because the patient may feel well before liver failure appears, and the window for intervention is narrow. [V6]

## 6. Opioids: receptors, benefits and harms

Opium is the dried exudate of the opium poppy and contains morphine and codeine. Until the 1960s a preparation called paregoric was sold for diarrhoea, cough and pain, and was also used to settle children at bedtime. [S8]

An opiate is a natural alkaloid of the poppy or a natural opioid of the body. An opioid is the wider term for natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic drugs with opium-like activity. Narcotic originally meant any psychoactive sleep-inducing substance, then came to mean opioids, and is now used loosely, often for illegal drugs; the precise term is narcotic analgesic. [V8]

The lecture names four opioid receptors: mu, delta, kappa and epsilon. Mu and kappa are most involved in pain, followed by delta. Each opioid binds with different affinity, which is why the class varies in euphoria, respiratory depression and constipation. [V8][S10]

The World Health Organization analgesic ladder, produced in the 1980s, steps from an over-the-counter agent for mild to moderate pain, to a weak opioid such as codeine for moderate to severe pain, to a strong opioid such as morphine or fentanyl for severe pain. The ladder is still taught, but opioids are often not the best choice, particularly in chronic non-cancer and neuropathic pain, and for bone pain a peripherally acting analgesic may work better. Opioids remain valuable for extreme pain, some cancer pain, cough suppression and diarrhoea. [V8][V9]

Tolerance develops unevenly, and that is the core safety problem. After a few weeks the body removes opioid receptors from the cell surface and reduces endogenous opioid production, so the same injury produces more pain. Tolerance to analgesia develops, but tolerance to constipation and respiratory depression does not develop to the same degree. The patient needs more drug for pain while remaining fully exposed to the effects that can kill. Euphoria drives escalating use and dependence. [V8][V12]

| Adverse effect | Mechanism | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Nausea and vomiting | Stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the medulla | A leading reason for non-adherence; ondansetron or prochlorperazine act there |
| Constipation | Gut opioid receptors slow peristalsis, so more water is absorbed | Does not resolve with tolerance; prevent it |
| Respiratory depression | Reduced brainstem sensitivity to carbon dioxide, dose-dependent | The effect that causes death; monitor breathing |
| Pruritus | Histamine release with cutaneous vasodilatation | Reassure; distinguish from allergy |
| Pinpoint pupils | Pupillary constriction | Reduces night vision; helps identify overdose |
| Urinary and biliary spasm | Constriction of ureteric and biliary smooth muscle | Caution in gallstones and urinary retention |
| Dependence and withdrawal | Receptor downregulation after prolonged use | Less dangerous than alcohol withdrawal, but can cause cardiac abnormalities and severe psychological disturbance |

The table links each effect to its cause. [V10][V11][S9]

Naloxone is the opioid antagonist used to reverse overdose and saves many lives each year. One limitation matters: naloxone displaces the opioid from the receptor but does nothing to active metabolites, and some morphine metabolites are neuroexcitatory, so reversal of the parent drug does not guarantee a settled patient. [V10][V12]

## 7. Specific opioids and the morphine yardstick

Morphine is the main active component of the opium poppy, the first opioid isolated, and the reference against which every other opioid is measured. Its half-life is about two to four hours. It is available by several routes, including controlled-release forms whose use in chronic non-cancer pain is limited. [V12]

The reference dose is 10 mg intramuscular morphine in the lecture [V12]; the printed chapter uses 10 mg IV as the reference [S10]. This note follows the lecture (IM). Because morphine undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism, an oral dose of 30 mg is needed to match it. Most other opioids are not metabolised so extensively, so the oral-to-parenteral gap is smaller. [V12]

Oxycodone has less first-pass metabolism and a longer half-life. About 15 to 20 mg orally matches 30 mg of oral morphine, and it is generally less addictive. Where addiction or constipation is a concern, oxycodone is combined with naloxone; the naloxone reduces euphoria and constipation while most analgesia is retained. [V12]

Codeine is a weak opioid and is much better absorbed than morphine, so the equipotent parenteral dose is about twelve times the morphine dose while the oral dose is about seven times. It is supplied combined with paracetamol and with NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen. About 10% of people lack the enzyme that converts codeine to morphine, which explains the patient who reports no relief. Codeine has abuse potential, and since early 2018 Australian products have required a prescription. [V12]

Fentanyl is a potent synthetic opioid with rapid onset and short duration. Tramadol binds opioid receptors and also inhibits serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake. As taught, it produces little euphoria and does not develop tolerance or dependence, has lower respiratory depression, and is useful for moderately severe pain but not neuropathic pain. Because of its serotonergic action it is contraindicated with SSRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, monoamine oxidase inhibitors and warfarin. Methadone is given to people dependent on heroin or morphine; it is itself addictive but produces weaker withdrawal, and because it binds the receptor strongly, a later dose of heroin cannot attach. [V12]

| Opioid | Strength relative to morphine | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Morphine | Reference (10 mg IM = 30 mg oral) | Short half-life; active neuroexcitatory metabolites |
| Oxycodone | 15–20 mg oral ≈ 30 mg oral morphine | Less first-pass metabolism, longer half-life, less addictive |
| Codeine | About one-seventh orally, one-twelfth parenterally | Prodrug; 10% of people cannot convert it |
| Fentanyl | About 100 times | Rapid onset, short duration |
| Tramadol | Not given as a morphine multiple | Also inhibits serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake |
| Methadone | Not given as a morphine multiple | Used for opioid dependence; strong receptor binding |

The table gathers the comparisons the lecture makes against morphine. [V12][V13]

## 8. Pain in children and older adults

Children and older adults both metabolise and excrete drugs less efficiently, so a standard adult dose stays in the body longer and produces a larger effect. Each group has an additional problem. [S7]

In children the first problem is assessment: pain, anxiety and other needs can produce similar behaviour, so pain is easy to underestimate. The second is dosing. Dose does not scale linearly from adult weight. If a 100 kg adult takes 100 mg, a 10 kg child does not take 10 mg, and applying that arithmetic can cause serious harm or death. Paracetamol is the most commonly used analgesic in children. NSAIDs and aspirin carry the risk of Reye's syndrome in a child with a viral infection, and codeine is used with caution. [S7][V7]

In older adults the problem is accumulation plus interaction. Polypharmacy is common, renal function is reduced, and NSAIDs are particularly risky because the kidney depends on prostaglandin-mediated blood flow that is already marginal. Older patients are also more sensitive to sedative and postural effects. [S7][V7]

For both groups, start low, review the response, and never borrow an adult dose. [V7]

## 9. Chronic and neuropathic pain

Chronic pain is longstanding pain that has produced lasting change along the pathway. Neuropathic pain is pain caused by damage to the nerve fibres themselves, so they send incorrect signals. The distinction matters because drugs that work for nociceptive pain often fail here. [V14][S11]

Three examples anchor the concept. Phantom limb pain occurs because the brain regions that received input from the amputated limb remain and generate burning, aching pain for a limb that is gone. Diabetic neuropathy occurs because high glucose destroys peripheral nerves; protective sensation is lost while burning pain develops. Post-herpetic neuralgia follows shingles: varicella zoster virus survives chickenpox in the dorsal root ganglion and reactivates in one dermatome when immunity falls. [V14][V15]

The change that converts acute into chronic pain is called wind-up, and it involves all three orders of neurons. Cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglion become inflamed and initiate impulses they should not initiate. The pathway uses more glutamate, which has a much longer half-life than substance P, so each message lasts longer. Inhibitory interneurons between the first- and second-order neurons become fewer, so the gate is held open and more pain signal passes with less braking. [V14]

Opioids are generally ineffective because the problem is not excess nociceptive input at an intact gate but a reorganised pathway with reduced inhibition. Tricyclic antidepressants are more effective: they leave serotonin and noradrenaline in the cleft longer, strengthen descending inhibition, close the gate and appear to normalise some pathway changes. SNRIs work similarly, and some SSRIs can be used at a higher dose. [V14]

Amitriptyline and nortriptyline are the tricyclics found most useful. Carbamazepine blocks sodium channels and allows chloride to flow back into the neuron; gabapentin and pregabalin act at the GABA receptor. More than 10% of people taking the anticonvulsants experience dizziness and drowsiness. Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist. Treatment should begin early, because delay allows further deregulation. [V14]

## 10. Depression and antidepressants

Depression becomes a disorder when profound sadness persists for months rather than following a recognisable loss. The common links are genetic vulnerability and severe stress, and postpartum depression was one of the first stress-linked forms recognised. The biogenic amine hypothesis proposes that serotonin and/or noradrenaline are too low in mood-related areas, so treatment raises one or both. [S17][V24][V25]

The hypothesis does not explain the time course. Raising neurotransmitter levels should work quickly, yet antidepressants take several weeks. Receptors must adapt, which takes time, and long-standing depression may produce permanent change, so some patients need treatment for years or for life. [V24]

| Class | Mechanism | Examples | Main adverse effects | Key caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SSRI | Blocks serotonin reuptake | Sertraline (Zoloft), escitalopram (Lexapro), fluoxetine (Prozac) | Headache, drowsiness, dizziness, insomnia, anxiety, dry mouth, nausea, sexual dysfunction | Suicide risk under 18; taper slowly; do not combine with other antidepressants |
| SNRI | Blocks serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake | Duloxetine (Cymbalta), venlafaxine (Effexor) | Similar to SSRI but more frequent; higher suicide risk | Also useful in chronic and neuropathic pain |
| TCA | Blocks serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline | Anticholinergic effects, sedation, sexual dysfunction, cardiac stimulation | Contraindicated in cardiovascular disease and epilepsy |
| MAOI | Inhibits monoamine oxidase | None named in the lecture | Affects all monoamines; food and drug interactions | Last resort |
| Atypical | Multiple sites | Bupropion, trazodone | Varies by drug | Profile depends on the individual agent |

The table compares the classes. [S18][V26][V28]

SSRIs are first-line. They block the serotonin transporter so serotonin stays in the cleft and inhibits the next neuron for longer. Onset is four to six weeks, and early effects are not the final effect. Common effects include headache, drowsiness, dizziness, insomnia, dry mouth, nausea and sexual dysfunction. They must not be combined with other antidepressants, and must be tapered rather than stopped, because abrupt withdrawal raises suicide risk. [V26][S18]

Tricyclics remain important when SSRIs fail and are particularly effective in chronic pain, neuropathic pain and fibromyalgia. Amitriptyline also blocks histamine H1 receptors, causing sedation and explaining why it is taken at night. Its anticholinergic action produces dry mouth, blurred vision, difficulty urinating and constipation in a high proportion of patients, and sexual dysfunction occurs in about 7%. Because it increases serotonin and noradrenaline it stimulates the heart and is contraindicated in cardiovascular disease; the same stimulation makes it unsafe in epilepsy. It is not first-line because it is less well tolerated. [V26][V27]

SNRIs block both transporters and share the pain benefit of the tricyclics. They cause somewhat more adverse effects than SSRIs and carry a higher suicide risk. [V28][S19]

Atypicals do not fit the other classes. Bupropion weakly inhibits noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake, helps nicotine cravings and withdrawal, and can be added when response to a typical antidepressant is incomplete. It does not cause sexual dysfunction, weight gain or sleepiness, so it suits patients troubled by those effects. Trazodone blocks serotonin reuptake and postsynaptic 5-HT2A receptors, and antagonises histamine H1, which explains its sedation; it lacks anticholinergic effects, is favoured in older patients, and can increase libido. [V28][V29]

Monoamine oxidase inhibitors were the first antidepressants and are now rarely used. They inhibit the enzyme that degrades serotonin, noradrenaline, dopamine, adrenaline and histamine, so effects are widespread, and the same enzyme normally stops tyramine from food reaching toxic levels, which is the source of the food interactions. [V24]

Bipolar disorder alternates depression with mania. Mania is not severe depression; it is elevated arousal and energy with reduced need for sleep, pressured talk, racing thoughts and overindulgent behaviour. Lithium carbonate is one of the most effective mood stabilisers and works better for mania. It has a narrow therapeutic index. It blocks ADH binding at the kidney, causing dehydration, and is itself nephrotoxic, so fluid intake must be maintained. It can cause parkinsonism, confusion, gastrointestinal upset, polyuria, vertigo, weight gain and cardiac dysrhythmias. [V28]

## 11. Anxiety and benzodiazepines

Anxiolytics reduce anxiety and hypnotics induce sleep, and the categories overlap because many anxiolytics help sleep and the reverse is also true. Benzodiazepines are the most commonly prescribed members; their names usually end in -zolam or -pam, a reliable way to recognise the class. [S15][V20]

More than 75% of brain neurons are interneurons, and the inhibitory ones release GABA. GABA interneurons are concentrated in the limbic system, especially the amygdala, which governs fear and anxiety. Excitatory input, usually glutamate, admits sodium and calcium and moves the cell toward threshold. GABA opens chloride channels, and chloride entry makes the cell more negative and further from threshold. Benzodiazepines bind the GABA-A receptor and increase the affinity of GABA for its own site; they do not act like GABA. More chloride flows, the cell is harder to depolarise, and activity falls throughout the brain, most importantly in the fear circuits. [V20][V21]

The class differs mainly by onset and half-life. Rapid onset with a short half-life suits sleep; a longer half-life suits anxiety; a very long half-life suits withdrawal and alcohol withdrawal. Diazepam has a half-life of about 50 hours and is available orally, intramuscularly and as a suppository; it accumulates in liver disease and in older patients. Alprazolam has a half-life of about 11 hours. [S15][V20]

Benzodiazepines are relatively free of direct toxicity, but can cause respiratory depression severe enough to require ventilation. Flumazenil reverses them, but its action is short, so repeated doses may be needed while a long-acting benzodiazepine is cleared. Long-term use is associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, and the drugs are addictive and often abused. In Australia, flunitrazepam and alprazolam are Schedule 8 and the others Schedule 4. Rebound anxiety can occur after more than about a week of use. [S15][V20][V21]

| Option | Mechanism | Onset | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Benzodiazepine | Potentiates GABA at GABA-A | 20–60 minutes | Fast, reliable | Dependence, respiratory depression, dementia risk |
| Buspirone | Partial agonist at serotonin receptors | 2–4 weeks | No addiction or respiratory depression | Generalised anxiety only, not panic |
| Hydroxyzine | First-generation antihistamine, H1 blockade | Not stated in the lecture | Useful in anxious children | Long half-life in adults, next-day drowsiness, avoided in older adults |
| Clonidine | Alpha-2 adrenergic agonist | Not stated in the lecture | Alpha-2 is a "negative feedback" receptor, parasympathetic-like | Not first-line in this lesson |

The table compares the alternatives the module names. [V22][V23][S16]

Buspirone is not a benzodiazepine. It has no addiction or respiratory depression potential, but works only in generalised anxiety, not panic anxiety, and evidence in social phobia is limited. It takes two to four weeks, compared with 20 to 60 minutes for a benzodiazepine. Hydroxyzine is a first-generation antihistamine used mainly for children too anxious to sleep; its half-life is about seven hours in children and much longer in adults, producing next-day drowsiness, and it is not recommended in older adults because of accumulation and dementia risk. Clonidine, an alpha-2 agonist, lowers sympathetic outflow. Many antidepressants also have anxiolytic effects, which is why they are preferred when long-term treatment is needed. [V22][V23]

## 12. Antipsychotics and the dopamine pathways

Psychosis is a loss of contact with reality, with hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thought and speech, distorted perception and emotion, and behaviour that can be bizarre or purposeless. Schizophrenia is the most common form, affecting about 1% of the population and more than 20% of homeless people in Australia; it usually appears between 16 and 21, and is associated with genetics, adverse intrauterine conditions and psychotropic drug use before about 22. The name means splitting of the mind, but it does not mean multiple personality: the split is between incoming stimuli and the response to them. [V30][V31]

Symptoms are divided into positive and negative. Positive symptoms are added experiences: delusions, disorganised speech and thought, disordered perception and emotion, and hallucinations. Negative symptoms are losses: toneless speech, social withdrawal, lack of enjoyment and apathy. Positive symptoms respond better to treatment. [V30]

Four dopamine pathways explain both benefit and harm. The mesolimbic pathway is overactive in schizophrenia and generates positive symptoms, so blocking dopamine there is therapeutic. The mesocortical pathway is already underactive and generates negative symptoms, including loss of motivation and social withdrawal, so blocking it makes those worse. The nigrostriatal pathway is part of the extrapyramidal motor system; reducing dopamine there produces parkinsonism, and excess produces hyperkinetic movement. The tuberoinfundibular pathway controls prolactin release, and dopamine normally inhibits prolactin, so blocking it raises prolactin, driving milk production, breast development in males and sexual dysfunction. [V30][V31]

| Pathway | Normal function | Effect of D2 blockade |
|---|---|---|
| Mesolimbic | Overactive in psychosis | Positive symptoms improve |
| Mesocortical | Motivation and cognition; underactive in psychosis | Negative symptoms may worsen |
| Nigrostriatal | Extrapyramidal movement control | Parkinsonism: rigidity, tremor, difficulty starting and stopping |
| Tuberoinfundibular | Dopamine inhibits prolactin | Prolactin rises: galactorrhoea, gynaecomastia, sexual dysfunction |

The table is the core prediction tool for antipsychotic adverse effects. [V30][V32]

Typical, or first-generation, antipsychotics block D2 receptors across all four pathways about equally. Haloperidol is high potency and produces more extrapyramidal symptoms; chlorpromazine is low potency, has less D2 affinity so fewer extrapyramidal effects, but blocks alpha-1 adrenergic receptors causing orthostatic hypotension, acetylcholine receptors causing anticholinergic effects, and histamine H1 receptors causing sedation and weight gain. Tardive dyskinesia is a late complication of haloperidol and chlorpromazine, caused by dysregulation of the basal ganglia; it produces tongue thrusts, lip smacking and exaggerated blinking and is often permanent. [V32][S20][S21]

Atypical, or second-generation, antipsychotics block D2 more transiently and dissociate quickly, allowing more normal dopamine transmission, and they also block serotonin 5-HT2A, which lowers extrapyramidal effects and improves negative symptoms. Quetiapine, aripiprazole, risperidone and olanzapine are the examples. They are not interchangeable, because each binds other receptors to a different degree. Olanzapine binds 5-HT2C strongly and causes weight gain and high glucose; quetiapine and olanzapine block H1 and cause drowsiness; risperidone binds alpha-1 and causes orthostatic hypotension, and also binds D2 strongly, giving it the highest extrapyramidal and prolactin effects in the group. Published ratings place aripiprazole fourth best for weight gain, fifth for extrapyramidal effects, best for prolactin and fifth for sedation, so it suits a patient whose main concern is prolactin elevation. [V32][V33]

Choice is therefore made by side-effect profile matched to the patient, not by generation alone. First-generation drugs are used less often and none is in the top 300 most prescribed. [V32]

## 13. Counselling that follows from the mechanism

For paracetamol, the dose ceiling protects the liver and more will not relieve more pain. Ask about combination products so the patient is not unknowingly doubling the dose. [S6][V6]

For NSAIDs, the rule is the smallest effective dose for the shortest time, because gastric, renal and cardiovascular harm scale with exposure. Ask about ulcer history, kidney disease and cardiovascular risk before recommending an over-the-counter NSAID. [V5]

For opioids, warn that constipation persists when analgesia fades, because tolerance to constipation does not develop. Nausea usually settles and can be treated with an antiemetic acting at the chemoreceptor trigger zone. Caution in gallstones and urinary retention, because opioids constrict biliary and ureteric smooth muscle. Dependence can occur within weeks, and stopping abruptly causes withdrawal. [V10][V8]

For antidepressants, set the expectation of four to six weeks, warn about early suicidal ideation in patients under 18, and insist on gradual tapering. [V26]

For antipsychotics, discuss the specific effects predicted by the receptor profile, because adherence fails when the patient is not prepared for them. [V32]

## Key takeaways

- Pain is protective, subjective and stage-dependent; the subacute phase is the window in which chronic change can be prevented. [V1]
- First-order neurons carry the impulse to the dorsal horn, second-order neurons ascend to the thalamus, and the gate between them is where opioids, cannabinoids and antidepressants act. [V4]
- Prostaglandin E2 sensitises peripheral neurons and raises the hypothalamic set point, so COX-2 inhibition reduces both pain and fever. [V36]
- NSAIDs harm the stomach and kidney by removing protective prostaglandins; COX-2 inhibitors traded gastric risk for cardiovascular risk. [V5]
- Neuropathic pain is driven by nerve damage and wind-up, so tricyclics, SNRIs and anticonvulsants outperform opioids. [V14]
- Antidepressants block reuptake of serotonin and/or noradrenaline, but response takes four to six weeks and stopping must be gradual. [V26]
- Antipsychotic effects are predicted by the pathway blocked: mesolimbic gives benefit, nigrostriatal gives extrapyramidal effects, tuberoinfundibular gives hyperprolactinaemia. [V30][V32]

## Common confusions

**Weak analgesic does not mean safe to escalate.** Paracetamol is first-line for mild to moderate pain yet is the leading cause of acute liver injury; first-line refers to safety at the correct dose, not to potency. [S6][V6]

**Opiate and opioid are not synonyms.** Opiate refers to natural alkaloids; opioid is the umbrella term for natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic drugs with opium-like action. [V8]

**Neuropathic pain is not just severe nociceptive pain.** The fibres are damaged and the pathway reorganised, so an opioid that works after surgery may fail in diabetic neuropathy. [V14]

**Schizophrenia is not split personality.** The splitting refers to a mismatch between incoming stimuli and the response, not to multiple identities. [V30]

**Atypical does not mean free of extrapyramidal effects.** Risperidone binds D2 strongly and produces the most extrapyramidal effects and prolactin elevation among the atypicals. [V32]
