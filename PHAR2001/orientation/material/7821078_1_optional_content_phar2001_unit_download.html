How can I use GenAI safely?
Using GenAI can be helpful for your learning, but only if you use it safely and responsibly. Safe
use means protecting your privacy, checking accuracy, and making sure GenAI supports (not
replaces) your learning.
Your Responsibilities
   Protect your data: Never enter personal details, assessment questions, or sensitive SCU
   information into GenAI tools. Assume anything you type could be stored or reviewed.
   Check accuracy: GenAI can make mistakes, especially in technical areas like engineering
   materials. Always verify explanations, formulas, or examples against your lecture notes,
   labs, or trusted resources.
   Respect assessment rules: Follow the GenAI use level set for each assessment. Using
   GenAI in prohibited tasks counts as academic misconduct.
   Own your learning: Use GenAI to practise, test, or revise — but the real understanding
   must come from you.
Reduce Your Risk
   Use Microsoft Copilot with your SCU login where possible. It offers better security
   under SCU’s enterprise agreement.
   Check tool policies before using external GenAI. Look for how your data is stored,
   whether it trains the model, and if you can delete it.
   Minimise what you share: Use made-up or anonymised data (e.g. fictional material
   strengths in practice problems).
   Avoid full uploads: Don’t paste whole assignments or lab reports into GenAI. Work with
   short sections or sample problems instead.
   Delete history: Turn off chat history/model training where possible and clear
   conversations when finished.
   Be transparent: Keep a brief note of how you used GenAI, and acknowledge it if required
   in your assessments.

Gastrointestinal System
Module 1 lays down the foundation for your understanding of
medications, your ability to use them safely and your ability to
streamline your studies in Pharmacology. Using one of the easiest
bodily systems, the gastrointestinal tract/system (GIT), we explore pharmacokinetics,
pharmacodynamics and other foundational information. This is a very important module that
is twice the size of the average module in this unit. The week 2 quiz on Friday of week 2 tests
your knowledge of the module.
Module overview


Module 1: The basics of Pharmacology
Hi All,
Module 1 explores most of the important basics of Pharmacology to give you a solid
foundation for your understanding of medications in the future. Normally, the foundational
information in pharmacology is the most difficult (and sometimes boring) material.
But in this module, we use the medications of the gastrointestinal system/tract (GIT) to
explore these difficult topics, making it actually fun. We start out with the easiest of
medications - those that cannot get absorbed and work by chemical action or by direct
action. (Normally, our medications work on cellular receptors or affect enzymes, but the first
topic/chapter introduces you very slowly.) Next, Topic/Chapter 2, we explore cellular
receptors, looking at what they are, what they do and how our drugs can affect them. This is
where we get into the topic of pharmacodynamics. The third topic/chapter starts introducing
you to pharmacokinetics, the movement of drugs through the system, involving absorption,
distribution, metabolism and excretion. We look at enzymes and how we can affect them with
our medications in Topic/chapter 4. Finally, in Topic/chapter 5, we look at neurotransmitters
and the most difficult concepts in the GIT, which have to do with our antiemetics.
If you want to love pharmacology the way I do, please pay close attention to this first
module. I teamed up with a partner in this module to make sure that you can actually picture
the processes that will form the basis of your understanding of pharmacology. Unfortunately,
while all of the videos in this unit are of relatively high quality, this module will have a
significantly higher level of annimation than subsequent modules, but by then you will have
developed the ability of picturing how our medications work !
Enjoy! Regards,
Ken

Affinity potency selectivity
Understanding affinity, potency and selectivity
Why is it that medication A, which works in the same way as Medication B can be so much
stronger (more potent)? While there can be many reasons, one of the most common has to
do with the strength of the molecular bond of the medication to its given receptor.
Most often, a medication that affects cellular receptors binds to its receptor and then lets go
shortly after. Then it binds to another receptor of the same type, and lets go... But what if the
drug bound to the receptor and it really takes a lot more time to get it off that receptor (it had
a much greater affinity to the receptor)? As you can imagine, many more messages will be
sent to the cell. So the drug's affinity can affect its potency.
This is another really important lesson as we learn three very important concepts, affinity,
potency and selectivity.

Metabolism of drugs
The importance of the liver
The liver is the site of most metabolism of drugs. As soon as most drugs are absorbed and
begin the distribution process, our body wants to excrete the drug as quickly as possible. And
since the kidneys are the main site of excretion of most drugs, the main goal of metabolism of
drugs is to make the drug more water soluble and easy to excrete. We want that drug out of
there ASAP
Interestingly, as drugs go through the liver, many different scenarios are possible, including
that an inactive pro-drug can become an active drug or an active drug can become an
inactive drug. This lesson explains the basics of the third principle of Pharmacokinetics, the
metabolism of drugs, in a way that you can apply the information to any medication.

Neurotransmitters
What are neurotransmitters?
Neurons communicate with the adjacent neuron using a neurotransmitter that excites or
inhibits the next neuron. But we need to get that neurotransmitter back out of the area very
quickly by a number of methods, so that there is not constant excitation or inhibition at the
next neuron. Our medications that affect neurotransmission can target the receptors on the
neurons, they can mimic the neurotransmitter, or they can target the ways in which we get the
neurotransmitters out of the synaptic cleft. The antiemetics all target the receptors on the
neurons. In other words, they are receptor antagonists for the neurotransmitters involved in
emesis.

Divisions of the ANS
The two divisions of the ANS
The two divisions of the autonomic nervous system are the Sympathetic Nervous System
(SNS) and the Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS). The SNS and PNS have, for the most
part, opposite effects because the autonomic nervous system helps maintain homeostasis.
So, if our heart was beating too slow for the conditions at the time, our cells would start
getting too little oxygen, lactic acid would start to accumulate and homeostasis would be
disrupted. But that does not happen because the SNS comes to the rescue, sending out a bit
of adrenaline and homeostasis is maintained.

   Sympathomimetics are drugs that mimic the sympathetic nervous system, like adrenaline

   nervous system agonists.

   It blocks acetylcholine and is often also called an anticholinergic drug or an antimuscarinic
   drug.

Vasopressin - The antidiuretic hormone
The material in this lesson will not be assessed in PHAR2001.
Human vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (ADH) is a hormone synthesized from
the hypothalamus. It then travels down the axon terminating in the posterior pituitary, and is
released into the circulation in response to hyperosmolality (equivalent to dehydration).
ADH is the water saver, and (its first metabolite) saves the water at its last chance of being
reabsorbed at the level of the kidneys just before it is excreted into the urinary bladder. As
you would expect, as ADH saves water, it has the effect of increasing the blood pressure.
Diabetes insipidus is a disorder characterised by a lack of ADH and while it is relatively
uncommon, it is important to understand ADH to understand desmopressin (the treatment for
diabetes insipidus) and to understand many drugs, because there are several that affect ADH.
For instance, caffeine decreases ADH release and ethanol (drinking alcohol) decreases its
production, both leading to their 'diuretic' effects. So let's find out more about this important
hormone in this lesson.

B/2: Specific antibiotics
Specific Antibiotics 1
Our antibiotics can be grouped in many different ways. For instance, one grouping has them
as time dependant versus dose dependent. But one of the most useful ways to group them is
by mechanism of action. While we will not need to know the mechanism by which various
types of antibiotics work, it is a convenient grouping that ultimately makes it easier to get a
complete picture of each type of antibiotic. In this first lesson about specific antibiotics, we
will look at the antibiotics that inhibit cell wall synthesis. This happens as the bacteria are
reproducing, and the prodigy will be unable to make the cell wall needed to survive.
Antibiotics that inhibit the cell wall synthesis include the penicillins (suffix: cillin) and the
cephalosporins (prefix: cef or ceph). These contain a β (beta)-lactam ring that is the active
portion of the drug. Bacteria often develop resistance to β-lactam antibiotics by synthesizing
a β-lactamase, an enzyme that attacks the β-lactam ring before it can adversely affect the
cell wall. To overcome this resistance, β-lactam antibiotics can be given with β-lactamase
inhibitors such as clavulanic acid.
