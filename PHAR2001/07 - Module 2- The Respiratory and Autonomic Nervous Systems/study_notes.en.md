# PHAR2001 Module 2 — The Respiratory and Autonomic Nervous Systems

## Overview

Module 2 builds the pharmacological vocabulary you will reuse for the rest of the unit. It begins with the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the involuntary control network that adjusts the heart, airways, gut, pupils, bladder and blood vessels to meet the body's needs at a given moment. It then separates the ANS into sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions, maps each division onto its neurotransmitters and receptor subtypes, and uses that map to explain why adrenergic and cholinergic drugs produce their intended effects and their adverse effects. [S1] [S3]

The second half applies the same logic to the respiratory system. Upper respiratory disorders such as allergic rhinitis and the common cold are treated with antihistamines and decongestants. Lower respiratory ventilation disorders such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are treated with bronchodilators, inhaled and oral corticosteroids and, where mucus is thick or copious, expectorants or mucolytic agents. The organising idea is that a drug's action and its side effects both follow from the receptor it binds and the organ in which that receptor sits. [S1] [S3] [S27] [S28]

## What you should be able to do

- Describe the general properties of the ANS: the functions it regulates, its two-neuron pathway, its neurotransmitters and the enzymes that degrade those transmitters in the synaptic cleft.
- Compare sympathetic and parasympathetic effects on the heart, pupils, gastrointestinal tract including salivation, lungs, blood vessels and urinary bladder.
- Name the structures affected by alpha 1, beta 1 and beta 2 receptors, and predict the effect of an agonist and an antagonist at each.
- Recognise the alternative names for sympathomimetic, sympatholytic, parasympathomimetic and parasympatholytic drugs.
- Explain the actions, interactions and adverse effects of adrenaline, phenylephrine, dobutamine, salbutamol, dopamine, phentolamine, labetalol, prazosin, doxazosin, metoprolol and atenolol.
- Distinguish direct-acting from indirect-acting cholinergic agonists, and explain the uses of bethanechol, pilocarpine, neostigmine, donepezil and atropine.
- Describe the structure and function of the lungs, including the pulmonary immune response, and the pathophysiology of allergic rhinitis and asthma.
- Compare expectorants with mucolytic agents, and explain when an antitussive is appropriate and when it is unsafe.

## 1. Where the autonomic nervous system sits

The nervous system is easiest to learn as a nested hierarchy. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system has an afferent (sensory) portion carrying information inward and an efferent (motor) portion carrying commands outward. The efferent portion splits into the somatic nervous system, which is largely voluntary, and the autonomic nervous system, which we control very little and which regulates organs such as the heart and gut. [S3] [V3]

A structural difference explains much of the pharmacology. The somatic pathway is a single neuron from the CNS to the effector, releasing acetylcholine at a nicotinic receptor on the neuromuscular junction. The autonomic pathway uses two neurons in series: a preganglionic neuron synapses in a ganglion, and a postganglionic neuron runs to the effector. In the sympathetic division the ganglion lies close to the CNS; in the parasympathetic division it lies close to, or within, the target organ. [V3] [S3]

The transmitters differ by division. The somatic and parasympathetic systems use acetylcholine. The sympathetic system uses adrenaline and noradrenaline. Acetylcholine is split by acetylcholinesterase within about three milliseconds, leaving fragments with far less activity. Noradrenaline is broken down by catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) and monoamine oxidase (MAO). Blocking acetylcholinesterase prolongs acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, which is how the indirect cholinergic drugs work. [V3] [S23]

## 2. Sympathetic versus parasympathetic effects

The sympathetic division is the fight-or-flight system. Imagine being chased: breathing becomes fast and deep because the airways dilate; the heart beats harder and faster; the mouth dries; gastrointestinal secretions and motility fall; peripheral vessels constrict so blood is diverted to the heart, lungs, brain and large muscles; the urinary sphincters tighten; the pupils dilate; and the liver shifts to make glucose available. Heart rate, contractility and vasoconstriction together drive blood pressure up. [V5]

The parasympathetic division is the rest-and-digest system and is close to the mirror image. Pupils constrict, the heart slows and contractility falls, gastrointestinal secretions and movement increase, salivation increases, urination and defecation become easier, and blood pressure falls. The parasympathetic system does not actively dilate peripheral vessels; it removes sympathetic constriction, and the vessels relax. [V5] [V40]

| Organ or function | Sympathetic effect | Parasympathetic effect |
|---|---|---|
| Heart | Rate and contractility increase | Rate and contractility decrease |
| Airways | Bronchi dilate | Bronchi constrict |
| Pupils | Radial muscle contracts, pupils dilate | Pupils constrict |
| Salivary glands | Secretion decreases, mouth dries | Secretion increases |
| Gastrointestinal tract | Secretions and motility decrease | Secretions and motility increase |
| Peripheral blood vessels | Constrict | No active dilation; lack of constriction allows relaxation |
| Urinary bladder | Sphincter constricts, urination harder | Urination easier |
| Defecation | Inhibited | Promoted |

The two divisions maintain homeostasis. If the heart beat too slowly, cells would receive too little oxygen and lactic acid would accumulate; the sympathetic system corrects that by releasing adrenaline. The parasympathetic system corrects the opposite problem, allowing rest, digestion and energy storage. [S4] [V5]

Two exceptions stop the rule from becoming a trap. The alpha 2 receptor sits in the sympathetic system but provides negative feedback, so its effect is broadly opposite to the other adrenergic receptors; without it, an intense sympathetic surge could drive the heart into ischaemia. The parasympathetic pathway also has nicotinic receptors on its first neuron, so not every cholinergic receptor is muscarinic. Setting these two exceptions aside still explains most ANS drugs. [V7] [V9]

## 3. Transmitters and receptor families

Adrenergic transmission uses four major receptors. Alpha 1 sits on peripheral vessels, gut smooth muscle, the radial muscle of the eye, the bladder sphincter and the prostate. Beta 1 sits on the heart, kidneys and GI tract. Beta 2 sits on the lungs, mast cells and skeletal muscle. Alpha 2 provides negative feedback. [S6] [V9] [V21]

At alpha 1, an agonist constricts vessels and raises blood pressure, slows the gut and can cause constipation, dilates the pupil and tightens the bladder sphincter. An alpha 1 antagonist does the reverse: vasodilation, lower blood pressure, increased gut activity, pupil constriction and bladder relaxation. At beta 1, an agonist increases heart rate (positive chronotropy) and contractile force (positive inotropy) and stimulates renin release. A beta 1 antagonist slows the heart, reduces contractility and lowers blood pressure partly by reducing renin release. At beta 2, an agonist dilates the airways and stabilises mast cells; in skeletal muscle it produces tremor. A beta 2 antagonist constricts the airways and has no clinical role. [V9] [V21] [V32]

| Receptor | Main locations | Agonist effect | Antagonist effect | Representative drugs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha 1 | Peripheral vessels, GI smooth muscle, radial muscle of eye, bladder sphincter, prostate | Vasoconstriction, raised BP, reduced GI motility, pupil dilation, urinary retention | Vasodilation, lower BP, increased GI activity, pupil constriction, bladder relaxation | Agonists: phenylephrine, oxymetazoline; antagonists: prazosin, doxazosin |
| Alpha 2 | Sympathetic nerve endings | Negative feedback that restrains sympathetic outflow | Not a major target here | Discussed only as the exception to the rule |
| Beta 1 | Heart, kidneys, some GI smooth muscle | Increased rate and contractility, renin release | Slower heart, reduced contractility, lower BP | Agonist: dobutamine; antagonists: metoprolol, atenolol |
| Beta 2 | Lungs, mast cells, skeletal muscle | Bronchodilation, mast cell stabilisation, tremor | Bronchoconstriction; no clinical use | Agonist: salbutamol |

Cholinergic transmission uses muscarinic and nicotinic receptors. The neuromuscular junction uses nicotinic receptors, which is why most cholinergic and anticholinergic drugs do not paralyse skeletal muscle. Parasympathetic effector junctions are mostly muscarinic. The muscarinic family has subtypes: M1 is mainly in the brain, M2 mainly in the heart, and M3 is associated with most other parasympathetic effects, including glandular secretion, pupil constriction, gut activity, urination and bronchoconstriction. [V38] [V40]

## 4. The four-part rule and the drug vocabulary

Every ANS drug fits one of four functional categories: it increases sympathetic activity, increases parasympathetic activity, decreases sympathetic activity, or decreases parasympathetic activity. Once you know the category, you can predict the effect on each organ from the normal division map. A drug that increases sympathetic activity raises heart rate, contractility and vasoconstriction, and therefore raises blood pressure. A drug that increases parasympathetic activity increases gut secretion and movement and can cause diarrhoea. A drug that decreases sympathetic activity constricts the airways, a dangerous effect in asthma. A drug that decreases parasympathetic activity lets the sympathetic system dominate, dilating the pupils and sometimes causing eye pain in bright light. [S5] [V7]

The vocabulary is larger than the physiology, and the unit expects every synonym. Sympathomimetic, adrenergic agonist, alpha agonist, beta agonist and adrenergic receptor stimulator all describe a drug that acts like adrenaline. Sympatholytic, adrenergic antagonist, adrenergic blocker, alpha blocker and beta blocker all describe a drug that blocks adrenaline's action. Parasympathomimetic, cholinergic agent, muscarinic agent and acetylcholine agonist describe a drug that mimics acetylcholine. Parasympatholytic, anticholinergic, antimuscarinic, muscarinic antagonist, parasympathetic antagonist and acetylcholine antagonist describe a drug that blocks acetylcholine. [S5] [V15]

A frequent error is to treat a sympatholytic and a parasympathomimetic as the same because both can slow the heart, increase gut activity and constrict the pupil. A sympatholytic removes sympathetic tone and allows parasympathetic tone to dominate; a parasympathomimetic adds cholinergic stimulation directly. The same distinction applies to a sympathomimetic and a parasympatholytic, which can both dilate the pupil and speed the heart by opposite receptor actions. [S5] [S15]

## 5. Non-selective adrenergic agonists: adrenaline and anaphylaxis

A non-selective adrenergic agonist binds all four adrenergic receptors and therefore acts like adrenaline throughout the body. This breadth is valuable in an emergency and troublesome in routine therapy, which is why later drugs were designed to be selective. The non-selective agonists to know are adrenaline, dopamine and pseudoephedrine. [S9] [S10] [V15]

Anaphylaxis is the clearest indication for adrenaline because it is a systemic type I hypersensitivity reaction. There are four types of hypersensitivity reaction; only type I is a true allergy. A local type I reaction, such as allergic rhinitis, affects one region. Anaphylaxis occurs when the same reaction becomes systemic and involves several organs at once. [V15]

The mechanism runs in sequence. On first exposure, the immune system identifies the allergen, usually a protein such as a food protein or a drug such as penicillin, and prepares memory and plasma cells. On re-exposure, plasma cells release immunoglobulin E (IgE), which attaches to mast cells in tissues and basophils in blood. Binding triggers degranulation and release of preformed mediators, of which histamine is the most important. Histamine constricts the bronchi, dilates blood vessels and increases their permeability. [V15]

The consequences follow from those three actions. Widespread vasodilation lowers peripheral resistance, and plasma leaking into interstitial spaces lowers blood volume, so blood pressure falls sharply. Fluid accumulation in the face and lips produces angioedema. Bronchoconstriction adds breathing difficulty. Adrenaline is the physiological antagonist of histamine because it reverses each limb: it constricts vessels, dilates the airways and speeds the heart so that blood pressure recovers and tissues are perfused. [V15]

Adrenaline is therefore the primary treatment for anaphylactic shock, and antihistamines are a useful adjunct but cannot replace it. A person at risk carries an auto-injector, commonly called an EpiPen, which delivers adrenaline into the thigh. Adrenaline and epinephrine both refer to the gland above the kidney. Even after self-injection, the person still needs hospital assessment [S9] [V15].

Adrenaline is also an extremely strong vasoconstrictor. An injection into the thigh can leave a small area of necrosis, and an accidental injection into a finger can threaten the fingertip. Phentolamine, a non-selective alpha antagonist, is the antidote because it reverses local vasoconstriction and restores blood flow. [V15] [V28]

Dopamine is a non-selective adrenergic agonist used in hospital for acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock, where the heart is not pumping enough blood and needs stimulation. It is given by injection. Because it acts on all adrenergic receptors, it affects every organ that adrenaline affects, but those effects matter less in the short-term hospital setting than the need to support circulation. Dopamine is not a treatment for chronic heart failure. [S10]

Pseudoephedrine is an oral non-selective sympathomimetic sold as a decongestant. It constricts nasal vessels effectively, but systemic absorption also raises blood pressure, can make urination difficult, dries the mouth and stimulates the CNS, producing insomnia, nervousness, excitability and anxiety. Its sale is recorded and restricted because it can be used recreationally and converted into illegal substances. [S10] [V18] [V56]

## 6. Selective adrenergic agonists

Selective agonists were developed because non-selective drugs caused effects in organs that did not need treatment. Four drugs illustrate the principle, each best remembered by its receptor. [S11] [S12]

| Drug | Receptor | Main use | Key effects and cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phenylephrine | Alpha 1 | Oral decongestant; pupil dilation; local vasoconstriction before minor surgery | Constricts nasal vessels; raises BP; constipation; urinary retention; eye pain in bright light; trials show little advantage over placebo as an oral decongestant |
| Oxymetazoline | Alpha 1 | Nasal spray decongestant | Applied locally, so systemic effects are uncommon; rebound congestion if used more than about three days; pregnancy category C |
| Dobutamine | Beta 1 | Acute heart failure, circulatory shock, hypotension, cardiac arrest | Positive chronotropy and inotropy; hospital use by injection |
| Salbutamol | Beta 2 | Asthma and COPD [V23]; bronchodilation and mast cell stabilisation | Muscle tremor from skeletal muscle beta 2; racing heart from cross-over onto other adrenergic receptors |

The side effects of an alpha 1 agonist follow the receptor's locations. Constricting peripheral vessels raises blood pressure. Slowing gut smooth muscle causes constipation. Tightening the bladder sphincter causes urinary retention. Contracting the radial muscle dilates the pupil, and in bright light that can be painful. These effects become therapeutic when the goal is to raise blood pressure in hypotension, dilate the pupil for an eye examination, or constrict local vessels before minor surgery so that bleeding and systemic absorption of local anaesthetic fall. [S11] [V19]

The main alpha 1 use is decongestion. A nasal spray places the drug where it is needed and limits systemic exposure, which is why sprays are preferred. Oxymetazoline should not be used for more than about three days in a row because longer use produces strong rebound congestion. Phenylephrine is available orally, but the best trials find it only slightly better than placebo, so a spray with or without an antihistamine is usually a better choice. [S11] [V19]

Beta 1 selective agonism is a hospital strategy. Dobutamine increases heart rate and contractile force, useful when the heart is failing acutely, but the same effect increases myocardial oxygen demand. The terms positive chronotropic and positive inotropic must carry the word positive or negative; chronotropic alone merely means an effect on rate, and inotropic alone merely means an effect on contractility. [S12] [V21]

Beta 2 selective agonism is the basis of inhaled asthma therapy. Salbutamol, also known as Ventolin, binds beta 2 receptors on airway smooth muscle to produce bronchodilation and on mast cells to stabilise them. It is available in several forms, but the usual route is a metered-dose inhaler, often called a puffer. Its characteristic adverse effect is muscle tremor, because beta 2 receptors are present on skeletal muscle; a racing heart can occur through cross-over onto other adrenergic receptors. [S12] [V21]

## 7. Adrenergic antagonists (sympatholytics)

A sympatholytic blocks sympathetic receptors and allows parasympathetic tone to dominate. It is not a parasympathomimetic, even though the visible result can be similar. Blocking alpha 1 dilates vessels and lowers blood pressure. Blocking beta 1 slows the heart and reduces contractility. Blocking beta 2 constricts the airways, making non-selective beta blockers contraindicated in asthma and other lung disorders. Blocking sympathetic control of the pupil leaves it constricted and can blur vision. Blocking sympathetic inhibition of the gut allows motility and secretion to rise. [S15]

| Drug | Selectivity | Main uses | Important adverse effects and cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labetalol | Non-selective; strong alpha 1, moderate beta, little alpha 2 | Hypertension, especially pre-eclampsia | Postural hypotension, blurred vision, bronchospasm, dyspnoea, swollen ankles, nasal congestion |
| Phentolamine | Non-selective alpha antagonist | Hypertensive emergencies, cocaine overdose, extravasated adrenaline | Profound vasodilation; restores flow at an adrenaline injection site |
| Prazosin, doxazosin | Alpha 1 selective; names end in -osin | Hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia | Alpha 1A-selective agents are preferred for prostate enlargement |
| Propranolol | Non-selective beta blocker | Hypertension, congestive heart failure; historical prototype | Bronchoconstriction, increased risk of type 2 diabetes, CNS effects |
| Timolol | Non-selective beta blocker | Hypertension with angina | Contraindicated in asthma; check for newly developed lung disease |
| Metoprolol, atenolol | Beta 1 selective | Hypertension, angina, some heart rhythm abnormalities | Bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension; do not stop abruptly |
| Beta 2 antagonists | Beta 2 selective | No clinical use | Would constrict bronchi and be contraindicated in nearly everyone |

Labetalol has strong alpha 1 affinity, moderate beta affinity and little alpha 2 binding. Blocking alpha 1 relaxes vessels while blocking beta 1 slows the heart, so the combination is a potent antihypertensive effect. It is often used in pre-eclampsia, diagnosed by high blood pressure together with protein in the urine. Most theories attribute pre-eclampsia to a placental abnormality, and it can progress to eclampsia, which is life-threatening; delivery is the definitive resolution. Labetalol's side effects follow its receptors: postural hypotension from vasodilation, blurred vision from blocked pupil dilation, bronchospasm and dyspnoea from beta 2 blockade, and swollen ankles and nasal congestion from vasodilation and fluid escape. [S16] [V28]

Phentolamine is a non-selective alpha antagonist reserved for situations needing profound vasodilation, such as hypertensive emergencies and cocaine overdose. It also treats an adrenaline injection site, because it reverses local constriction and lets blood return. [S16] [V28]

Alpha 1 blockers ending in -osin, such as prazosin and doxazosin, block the receptor on peripheral vessels, the urinary sphincter and the prostate. They are used for hypertension and for enlarged prostate in men. A subset selective for the alpha 1A receptor on the prostate is now preferred for prostate enlargement because it is almost exclusively prostate-selective. [S17] [V30]

Beta blockers end in -olol, but the suffix does not say whether the drug is selective. Propranolol was the prototype and remains widely prescribed, yet it performs less well than newer agents, increases the risk of type 2 diabetes and enters the brain, where it produces central effects. Timolol is a better non-selective choice and is useful when hypertension coexists with angina. Both are contraindicated in asthma, and a patient who develops asthma after starting a beta blocker must have the prescription reviewed. [S18] [V32]

Angina is a symptom rather than a disease. It is chest pain radiating to the left arm and jaw, caused by insufficient oxygen at the heart muscle. The heart has many pain receptors, so even a small fall in blood flow allows lactic acid to accumulate and produces pain quickly; atherosclerosis is the usual cause. Beta blockers reduce heart rate and contractility, lowering oxygen demand. [V32]

Beta 1 selective blockers such as metoprolol and atenolol treat hypertension, angina and some heart rhythm abnormalities. Metoprolol is among the most prescribed medicines in this class. Blocking beta 1 in the kidney reduces renin release and damps the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which lowers blood pressure through several mechanisms. Bradycardia, a resting rate below 60 beats per minute, is a serious adverse effect, and orthostatic hypotension can follow. Treatment must not be stopped abruptly, because angina can worsen; the dose is reduced gradually. [S18]

Beta 1 agonists and beta 1 antagonists are both used in heart failure because they treat opposite forms. In acute failure the heart needs stimulation, so dobutamine is used. In chronic failure the heart is overloaded and needs to be slowed and protected, so a beta blocker is used. [V34]

## 8. Cholinergic agonists: direct and indirect

Acetylcholine itself cannot be used as a drug because its half-life in the body is less than one second. Direct-acting agonists therefore bind muscarinic receptors without being broken down by acetylcholinesterase. Bethanechol and pilocarpine are the two to know. Pilocarpine is used mainly to lower intraocular pressure in glaucoma until surgery can be performed, and because it is usually given as topical eye drops it has few systemic effects, although some can reach the general circulation. Bethanechol is used after surgery, when anaesthesia has made urination difficult; it relaxes the bladder sphincter. As non-selective acetylcholine agonists, both can cause bradycardia, hypotension, diarrhoea and bronchoconstriction. [S20] [V42]

Indirect agonists work differently. They inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that normally clears acetylcholine from the synaptic cleft within milliseconds. Inhibition leaves more acetylcholine in the cleft for longer, so more messages reach the effector. This is not the same as adding a drug that binds the receptor: indirect drugs amplify whatever cholinergic transmission is already happening, including transmission at the neuromuscular junction. [S23] [V42]

The indirect drugs divide by their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier. Those that enter the brain are used in Alzheimer's disease; those that remain peripheral are used in myasthenia gravis. Neostigmine and pyridostigmine, the longer-acting member of the pair, stay outside the brain and treat myasthenia gravis. Donepezil and galantamine enter the brain and treat Alzheimer's disease. [S24] [V44]

Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction. The number of receptors falls, beginning with the muscles of the face and eyes and those used for swallowing, and later involving the respiratory muscles. The drugs cannot cure the disease, but by keeping acetylcholine in the cleft longer they compensate for the missing receptors. Cells can replace receptors, so an exacerbation is followed by upregulation. Atropine is often taken with neostigmine or pyridostigmine to counteract cardiovascular effects, especially bradycardia. [V44]

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive degenerative disorder with fewer neurons in the brain, and acetylcholine receptors are important in the affected regions. Galantamine is used in moderate dementia and donepezil in more advanced disease. Donepezil crosses the blood-brain barrier more easily and has an oral bioavailability of 100%, unusual for an oral medicine. Benefit is limited: the best result is a delay of roughly six months to a year. Treatment is assessed before it begins and again about two months later, and discontinued if there is no benefit after two to three months. Gastrointestinal complaints are the most frequent adverse effect, and donepezil is contraindicated in heart problems because cholinergic action slows the heart. [V44]

Indirect cholinergic poisoning produces a recognizable syndrome. Nerve gases inhibit acetylcholinesterase irreversibly, so acetylcholine accumulates. In muscles this causes constant contraction, and if the diaphragm cannot relax the person cannot breathe. In the heart it causes profound bradycardia. In the gut it causes salivation, cramping and diarrhoea. A reversible inhibitor produces the same effects in milder form, which is why the therapeutic margin is narrow. [S24] [V42]

## 9. Anticholinergic drugs and atropine

Anticholinergic drugs are also called parasympatholytics, muscarinic antagonists, antimuscarinic agents, parasympathetic antagonists and acetylcholine antagonists. They block muscarinic receptors and allow sympathetic tone to dominate. At the pupil this produces dilation and sometimes eye pain in bright light. At the heart it speeds rate and increases contractility. At vessels it removes cholinergic relaxation and can raise blood pressure. At the mouth it produces dry mouth. In the gut it reduces activity and causes constipation. At the bladder it tightens the sphincter and causes urinary retention. [S25] [V46]

Many drugs not prescribed as anticholinergics still bind acetylcholine receptors weakly and produce these effects; first-generation antihistamines are the standard example. Intensity is patient-specific: one person reports nothing, another reports only a dry mouth, and a third has effects severe enough to require stopping the medicine. Recognising the words "anticholinergic effects" should prompt you to look for dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision and confusion. [V46]

Central effects depend on the blood-brain barrier. Acetylcholine is used throughout the brain, so an anticholinergic that enters the brain can cause confusion and drowsiness and has been associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer's disease. This is the mirror image of the cholinergic drugs used to treat Alzheimer's disease. [V46]

Atropine is the anticholinergic to remember. It comes from the deadly nightshade plant, which contains atropine, scopolamine and hyoscyamine; medical atropine is a purified form used at a safe dose. It was first isolated in the 1830s. It is applied topically to dilate the pupil, given intravenously to reverse nerve gas and muscarinic mushroom poisoning, and used in some forms of heart failure to raise heart rate and contractility. [S25] [V46]

## 10. The respiratory system and its immune response

The respiratory system has upper and lower portions. The upper portion includes the nasal and oral cavities, the pharynx and the trachea. The trachea divides into the right and left primary bronchi, and after about twenty further bifurcations the air reaches the terminal bronchioles and alveoli, where gas exchange occurs. The upper respiratory disorders treatable with these drugs are allergic rhinitis and the common cold, managed with antihistamines and decongestants. [S28] [V52]

The lower respiratory disorders treatable with these drugs are the ventilation disorders, meaning those that obstruct airflow. The main drugs are bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids and oral corticosteroids. When mucus is thick or copious, expectorants or mucolytic agents may help. Antitussives act at the medulla to reduce the urge to cough and are appropriate only for non-productive coughs. Pleural effusion and pulmonary embolism involve the respiratory system but are managed according to their underlying cause, so they are covered with the cardiovascular content. [S28]

Healthy lungs are elastic. The diaphragm, the largest respiratory muscle, expands the chest, and elastic recoil then pushes air out; there are no major muscles for exhalation because recoil normally does the work. In emphysema, elasticity is lost, so the person must use accessory muscles and purse the lips to force air out. No medicine restores elasticity; bronchodilators are used to make breathing as easy as possible. [V52]

The lungs themselves lack pain receptors. This is why bacterial pneumonia can present with malaise, fever and anorexia rather than pain: the immune system fills the affected lobe with fluid exudate to fight the bacteria. Pain arises when infection reaches the pleura, the two-layer membrane around the lung, producing pleurisy, or when pneumonia becomes complicated and spreads into the pleural cavity. The pleural space can also fill with transudate in congestive heart failure, producing a pleural effusion. [V52]

Pulmonary embolism usually begins with a clot from a deep vein in the leg. The clot travels to the right side of the heart and is pushed into the pulmonary circulation. The lungs have a dual blood supply: the pulmonary circulation brings deoxygenated blood for oxygenation, while the bronchial circulation brings oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart. A small embolus therefore blocks part of the pulmonary circulation without immediately destroying tissue that the bronchial circulation still supplies. Treatment is with anticoagulants and fibrinolytic drugs. [V52]

The airway immune response links the ANS to respiratory pharmacology. In a type I reaction, mast cells release preformed granules containing histamine, which dilates vessels and increases their permeability. The same process occurs in allergic rhinitis, where the response is localised to the sinuses, and in the common cold, where a viral or bacterial protein triggers a similar local response. Antihistamines block histamine from binding its receptor; decongestants constrict the same vessels that histamine dilated. [S30] [V56]

## 11. Cough, mucus and nasal congestion

Cough receptors are not confined to the respiratory system; they also sit in the diaphragm and the ear, and all send signals to the medulla. The most effective cough medicines therefore act at the medulla. Soothing lozenges may reduce irritation, but they are not true antitussives. The true antitussives are the opioids, such as codeine, and the morphine derivative dextromethorphan. [S29] [V54]

| Class | Mechanism | Examples | Main use and caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Antitussive | Acts at the medulla to reduce the cough reflex | Codeine, dextromethorphan | Non-productive cough only; suppressing a productive cough traps mucus and risks obstruction or infection |
| Expectorant | Adds hydration and dilutes secretions, often by irritating the mucosa with a volatile oil | Guaifenesin | Loosens thick secretions so they are easier to clear |
| Mucolytic | Splits the molecular bonds within mucus | Acetylcysteine | Thick tenacious mucus in chronic bronchitis and cystic fibrosis; also the antidote for paracetamol overdose |

Codeine is a naturally occurring methylated opioid and the second most prevalent alkaloid in the opium poppy. It is a controlled medicine requiring a prescription and is prescribed less often as a cough medicine now. It also produces analgesia, constipation and euphoria, and it is frequently misused. Dextromethorphan is sold over the counter, acts at the medulla, lacks analgesic effect, and can cause hallucinations and withdrawal symptoms at high or regular doses. [V54]

Neither drug should be used for a productive cough. Mucus must be cleared; if it dries it can form a plug, and stagnant mucus is a good medium for microorganisms. In children, trials show no cough remedy outperforming placebo, and codeine is contraindicated, particularly under two years of age, because it can depress respiration. Opioids are contraindicated in pregnancy, while dextromethorphan is pregnancy category A and is therefore preferred when a cough medicine is needed in pregnancy. [V54]

Expectorants and mucolytics both aim to clear mucus but act differently. Expectorants add hydration so secretions become thinner and easier to expel. Many are volatile oils, such as eucalyptus oil, which are inhaled, irritate the mucosa and provoke a thin watery fluid that dilutes thick sputum. Guaifenesin is one of the most common expectorants in Australia. Mucolytics address thick, tenacious mucus, especially in chronic bronchitis and cystic fibrosis, where mucus plugs can obstruct airways and lead to permanent dilatation called bronchiectasis. Acetylcysteine splits the molecular bonds in mucus; it also prevents liver injury after paracetamol overdose when given within about two hours. [V54]

Decongestants are alpha 1 agonists, not antihistamines. Histamine dilates nasal vessels and increases permeability; a decongestant constricts those same vessels. Three are commonly used in Australia. Oxymetazoline is a nasal spray, fairly selective for alpha 1, placed directly at the target, and rarely causes systemic effects such as hypertension; its main cautions are rebound congestion after about three days and pregnancy category C status. Phenylephrine is taken orally, has fewer side effects, but in trials performs no better than placebo. Pseudoephedrine is an effective oral decongestant with systemic stimulant effects: insomnia, nervousness, excitability, anxiety and raised blood pressure, making it contraindicated in existing hypertension and some heart conditions. Night-time formulations add a first-generation antihistamine, whose drowsiness offsets the stimulation. [S30] [V56]

## 12. Lower respiratory disorders and the treatment of asthma

Lower respiratory disorders affect either ventilation, the flow of air to and from the alveoli, or gas exchange. Medicines can help ventilation disorders but cannot currently reverse the fibrosis and scarring that impair gas exchange. The main ventilation disorders are COPD, cystic fibrosis and asthma. COPD is the combination of emphysema and chronic bronchitis; smoking is the most common cause in Australia, and prevalence is falling. Cystic fibrosis is an autosomal recessive disorder that produces thick secretions in hollow tubes throughout the body, including the lungs and gut; it is not a respiratory disorder in origin, but respiratory complications usually dominate, with death often around age 38. [S31] [V58]

Asthma is the most common ventilation disorder in Australia, affecting about 10% of the population, and a further 30% are estimated to experience it at some point. It involves an immune response in the airways. Histamine is released immediately from mast cells and macrophages, producing bronchoconstriction and mucus formation. Later, the mast cell makes more potent mediators with longer action, including leukotrienes, which drive prolonged constriction and mucus release. Narrowing and thick mucus reduce the lumen, and halving the diameter reduces flow to roughly one-sixteenth. [S31] [V58] [V60]

Asthma is often called reversible, but that label is misleading. It involves chronic inflammation, which leads to scarring, tissue remodelling and permanent change if untreated or severe for long periods. The 30% of Australians who need asthma medicines at some point therefore need treatment that controls inflammation, not only treatment that relieves the acute attack. [S32] [V58]

Triggers differ between people. Some react to allergens, some to smoke, some to cold air, anxiety or exercise. A useful model is the "cup": every trigger adds to the cup, and an attack occurs when it overflows. Care includes identifying and removing the triggers that fill a particular person's cup, which is why one patient's plan cannot simply be transferred to the next. [V58]

| Step | Treatment | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Occasional symptoms | Short-acting bronchodilator, usually a beta 2 agonist such as salbutamol | Reliever: opens the airway quickly during symptoms or an attack |
| Persistent symptoms | Add an inhaled corticosteroid such as budesonide or fluticasone | Preventer: reduces inflammation and the frequency of attacks |
| Poor control | Add a leukotriene receptor antagonist such as montelukast to the inhaled corticosteroid; use an anticholinergic bronchodilator such as ipratropium as an alternative when beta 2 effects are not tolerated | Additional control, usually alongside an inhaled corticosteroid |
| Severe persistent symptoms | Oral corticosteroids | Systemic anti-inflammatory treatment when inhaled therapy is insufficient |

The reliever/preventer distinction is central. A short-acting beta 2 agonist is the main medicine once an attack begins. It binds beta 2 receptors on airway smooth muscle to dilate the airway and on mast cells to stabilise them, so it has a dual action: rapid bronchodilation plus an attempt to prevent further release of longer-acting mediators. Salbutamol is short-acting; salmeterol is long-acting and usually combined with an inhaled corticosteroid as a preventer. The names are easy to confuse, and the difference in duration matters. [S32] [V60]

Inhaled beta 2 agonists are selective but not perfectly so. Tremor is common because beta 2 receptors sit on skeletal muscle, and a racing heart can occur through cross-over onto other adrenergic receptors. A person who cannot tolerate these effects may instead use an anticholinergic bronchodilator; ipratropium bromide is short-acting and useful during exacerbations. [V60]

Antihistamines are not a treatment for asthma. Clinical trials show no overall benefit, although about 10% of people may gain some benefit as a prophylactic add-on. They should never be relied on once an attack has started, because the dominant mediators at that stage are longer-acting leukotrienes rather than histamine. Leukotriene receptor antagonists such as montelukast and zafirlukast are oral medicines that block leukotriene receptors and are taken in addition to inhaled corticosteroids. [S32] [V60]

Inhaled corticosteroids are the most effective anti-inflammatory medicines for asthma because they suppress all arms of the immune and inflammatory response, not one mediator. They are indicated when a person uses a reliever more than about three to four times per week. Because the dose is delivered to the lung, systemic effects are fewer than with oral corticosteroids; the main adverse effect is fungal infection in the lungs and mouth. Oral corticosteroids are reserved for persistent symptoms uncontrolled despite other treatment. [S32] [V60]

COPD follows the same logic with a different emphasis. Loss of elasticity in emphysema cannot be reversed, so bronchodilators reduce obstruction and make breathing easier. Chronic bronchitis contributes thick mucus, which is where mucolytics and expectorants help. Because COPD and asthma share bronchodilator therapy, learning asthma treatment gives most of the drug knowledge needed for other ventilation disorders. [S28] [V58]

## Key takeaways

- The ANS is the involuntary efferent division. It uses a two-neuron chain, with the sympathetic ganglion near the CNS and the parasympathetic ganglion near the target organ. [V3] [S3]
- Sympathetic activity is fight-or-flight: dilated airways and pupils, faster and stronger heart, raised blood pressure, reduced gut activity and urinary retention. Parasympathetic activity is close to the opposite. [V5] [V40]
- Alpha 1 controls vessels, gut smooth muscle, the radial muscle of the eye and the bladder sphincter. Beta 1 controls the heart and renin release. Beta 2 controls airway smooth muscle, mast cells and skeletal muscle. [S6] [V9]
- Every ANS drug fits one of four categories: sympathetic increase, sympathetic decrease, parasympathetic increase or parasympathetic decrease. [S5]
- Adrenaline is the physiological antagonist of histamine and the primary treatment for anaphylaxis; phentolamine reverses accidental local vasoconstriction. [V15] [V28]
- Selective agonists trade breadth for safety: phenylephrine and oxymetazoline for alpha 1, dobutamine for beta 1, salbutamol for beta 2. [S11] [S12]
- Non-selective beta blockers are contraindicated in asthma because beta 2 blockade constricts the airways, and beta 1 agonists and antagonists both treat heart failure because acute and chronic failure require opposite support. [S18] [V34]
- Direct cholinergic agonists bind muscarinic receptors; indirect agonists inhibit acetylcholinesterase and amplify existing cholinergic transmission. Anticholinergic effects are dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, pupil dilation, tachycardia and, centrally, confusion. [S23] [V42] [V46]
- Antitussives act at the medulla and are only for non-productive cough; expectorants dilute mucus while mucolytics split its bonds. [S29] [V54]
- Asthma treatment separates the reliever (short-acting beta 2 agonist) from the preventer (inhaled corticosteroid), with leukotriene antagonists and anticholinergics as add-ons. [S32] [V60]

## Common confusions

**Sympatholytic versus parasympathomimetic.** Both can slow the heart, increase gut activity and constrict the pupil. A sympatholytic blocks adrenergic receptors; a parasympathomimetic stimulates muscarinic receptors. [S5] [S15]

**Beta 1 agonist versus beta 1 antagonist in heart failure.** Dobutamine is for acute failure, where the heart needs stimulation. Metoprolol is for chronic failure, where the heart needs to be slowed and protected. The words "heart failure" alone do not identify the drug. [V34]

**Salbutamol versus salmeterol.** Salbutamol is short-acting and used as a reliever. Salmeterol is long-acting and used as a preventer, usually with an inhaled corticosteroid. [V60]

**Expectorant versus mucolytic.** Both help clear mucus. An expectorant dilutes secretions by adding fluid; a mucolytic splits the chemical bonds inside the mucus. [V54]

**Antitussive for every cough.** Antitussives suppress the cough reflex at the medulla and are appropriate only for a non-productive cough. Suppressing a productive cough can trap mucus, form a plug and promote infection. [S29] [V54]

**Decongestant as an antihistamine.** A decongestant is an alpha 1 agonist that constricts vessels; an antihistamine blocks histamine receptors. [S30] [V56]

**Muscarinic versus nicotinic receptors.** Most parasympathetic effectors are muscarinic, which is why cholinergic drugs usually spare skeletal muscle. The neuromuscular junction is nicotinic, and indirect acetylcholinesterase inhibitors can affect it. [V38] [V42]
