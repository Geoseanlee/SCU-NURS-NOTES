===== FILE: 04 - Divisions of the ANS.epub =====
Divisions of the ANS
-
-
The two divisions of the ANS
The two divisions of the autonomic nervous system are the Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS) and the Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS). The SNS and PNS have, for the most part, opposite effects because the autonomic nervous system helps maintain homeostasis. So, if our heart was beating too slow for the conditions at the time, our cells would start getting too little oxygen, lactic acid would start to accumulate and homeostasis would be disrupted. But that does not happen because the SNS comes to the rescue, sending out a bit of adrenaline and homeostasis is maintained.

===== FILE: 05 - Overviewing drugs of the ANS.epub =====
Overviewing drugs of the ANS
-
-
Overview
In this lesson, we outline one simple rule (along with two exceptions to the rule) that allows you to easily understand the drugs of the ANS.
By watching this video and doing the associated activities, you will streamline your work involved in understanding of the drugs of the ANS.
Just a few terms that are extremely important at this point:
-  Sympathomimetics are drugs that mimic the sympathetic nervous system, like adrenaline and dopamine. They can also be called sympathetic nervous system agonists. While they often have a similar clinical outcome to the ParasympathoLYTIC drugs, THEY ARE NOT.
-  Sympatholytic drugs are those that BLOCK the sympathetic nervous system, like the beta blockers. They can also be called sympathetic nervous system antagonists. While they often have a similar clinical outcome to the ParasympathoMIMETIC drugs, THEY ARE NOT.
-  Parasympathomimetics are drugs that mimic the parasympathetic nervous system (the rest and digest system), like acetylcholine, although acetylcholine itself cannot be used as a drug because of its incredibly short half life. They can also be called parasympathetic nervous system agonists.
-  A parasympathoLYTIC drug is a drug that is against the parasympathetic nervous system. It blocks acetylcholine and is often also called an anticholinergic drug or an antimuscarinic drug.

===== FILE: 28 - Preview of respiratory system.epub =====
Preview of respiratory system
-
-
Review of the respiratory system
This lesson reviews the important points about the respiratory system that will help you understand the relevant medications.
The respiratory system is broadly divided into the upper and lower respiratory system. Upper respiratory system disorders that are treatable with medications include allergic rhinitis and the common cold, and our main drugs used to treat these include the antihistamines and the decongestants.
The lower respiratory system disorders that are treatable with our medications include those disorders that result in obstruction of the airways, which are sometimes referred to as the ventilation disorders. Common medications used to treat those disorders include bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids and oral corticosteroids. If the disorder results in thick or copious amounts of mucus, the expectorants or mucolytic agents may also be of value. We also have antitussives or cough medicines that work at the level of the medulla to decrease the urge to cough, but those should only be used for non-productive coughs.
There are several other disorders that involve the respiratory system, including pleural effusions, and pulmonary embolism, however those disorders are covered elsewhere, according to the origin of the complaint.

Overview of antitussives, expectorants and mucolytic agents
Although they often do not require a prescription, antitussives, expectorants and mucolytic agents are all widely used, and as such, very important to understand.
Antitussives (or cough suppressants) that are correctly named will work at the level of the medulla, and include the opioids and dextromethorphan. Other medications that soothe the lungs, or get mucus off the cough receptors are not termed antitussives.
Expectorants and mucolytic agents generally aim to do the same thing, to get mucus out of the lungs, but they work by different mechanisms. In this lesson, we explore these three important classes of medications in much more depth.

===== FILE: 30 - Decongestants and antihistamines.epub =====
Decongestants and antihistamines
-
-
The role of decongestants and antihistamines
When an immune response happens in the sinuses, histamine is released from the local mast cells, and the histamine dilates the blood vessels and increases the permeability of those blood vessels. The escaping fluid and cells work on the mucus secreting cells, resulting in both plasma and mucus being excreted. An antihistamine blocks the action of histamine on the receptors and a decongestant does the opposite thing that histamine does, contracting the same blood vessels that histamine dilated.
In this lesson, we concentrate on understanding that process and on the decongestants, as our tutorial for this week fully covers the antihistamines.

===== FILE: 32 - Treatment of Asthma (and other Ventilation Disorders).epub =====
Treatment of Asthma (and other Ventilation Disorders)
-
-
Pharmacological management of asthma
As you have learned in the previous lessons, when an person experiences an asthmatic attack, the mast cells in their lungs degranulate and release histamine. So you would think that antihistamines are going to be helpful for asthmatics, but only about 10% of asthmatics get any additional benefits from them, and they should never be relied on for an acute asthmatic reaction. The main drugs for asthma are the bronchodilators, and those can be sympathomimetics, parasympatholytics, or other substances that dilate the lungs. Another very important target of our medications will be drugs that target the actual mediators of inflammation. The leukotrienes are mediators of inflammation that are often targeted, but the most useful medication that targets the immune system will be the corticosteroids (either inhaled or systemic). Corticosteroids decrease all arms of the immune system, so they are very important in the treatment of asthma.
Video: Treatment of Asthma
Remember that although asthma is regarded by many authors as a reversible disorder, it does involve chronic inflammation, and chronic inflammation almost always results in fibrosis and scarring, remodelling of the tissues, and permanent changes, so asthma must be treated and you must know how it is treated because 30% of Australians will need these asthma meds during at least one period of their lives.
