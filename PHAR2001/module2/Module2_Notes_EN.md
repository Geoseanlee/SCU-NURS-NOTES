## Build an ANS map

Distinguish sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways, neurotransmitters and receptor sites. Predict effects in the heart, vessels, airway, eye, gut and bladder before studying individual agents. Sympathetic blockade is not identical to direct parasympathetic stimulation.

## Adrenergic agonists

The chapter uses adrenaline, pseudoephedrine, dopamine and selective alpha/beta agents to connect receptor action to benefit and adverse effects. Explain why non-selective action affects several organs. The anaphylaxis lesson is foundational learning, not a substitute for an emergency protocol.

## Adrenergic antagonists

Compare non-selective blockers with alpha-1 and beta-1 selective agents. Work from receptor location to changes in vascular tone, heart function and other effects. Relate class selection to the person’s condition and comorbidities rather than applying a single agent to everyone.

## Cholinergic medicines

Direct cholinergic agonists act at receptors; indirect agents inhibit acetylcholinesterase and increase acetylcholine action. Distinguish muscarinic and nicotinic sites. Explain dry mouth and other antimuscarinic effects mechanistically.

## Respiratory pharmacology

Separate upper-airway allergy and congestion from lower-airway obstruction. Compare bronchodilation with anti-inflammatory control. Distinguish antitussives, expectorants and mucolytics by purpose and mechanism, and relate antihistamine/decongestant effects to the underlying physiology.

## Revision checks

Predict the effect of beta-1 versus beta-2 stimulation; explain alpha-1 blockade; compare a receptor agonist with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor; explain why a bronchodilator and a corticosteroid do different jobs. Do not infer clinical doses from these summaries.

## Receptor–organ map

| Target | Activation: useful direction to predict | Blockade: useful direction to predict |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha-1 | Vascular smooth-muscle contraction; increased vascular resistance | Reduced vascular tone; postural dizziness becomes an important safety question |
| Beta-1 | Increased cardiac rate/contractility | Reduced cardiac drive; consider bradycardia and other medicines affecting the heart |
| Beta-2 | Bronchial smooth-muscle relaxation | Loss of beta-2-mediated bronchodilation is relevant when comparing non-selective and selective beta blockers |
| Muscarinic receptors | Organ-specific parasympathetic effects, including secretions, gut/bladder activity and reduced heart rate | Dry mouth and reduced secretions; effects on bladder, bowel, eyes and heart follow receptor location |

Use this as a physiological map, not a dose or treatment algorithm. Selectivity is relative and may change with exposure.

## Five chapter checkpoints

1. Draw the sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways and name the transmitters/receptors rather than using 'opposites' as the whole explanation.
2. For adrenaline and selective agonists, predict effects in more than one organ; intended and adverse effects come from the same receptor map.
3. Compare alpha blockade and beta blockade. Explain why blocking one division is not the same mechanism as directly activating the other.
4. Compare direct cholinergic stimulation with acetylcholinesterase inhibition, which prolongs endogenous acetylcholine action.
5. For respiratory drugs, separate cough suppression, mucus clearance, bronchodilation and inflammation control. Antihistamines and decongestants act differently; antihistamines are not a substitute for acute-asthma treatment.
