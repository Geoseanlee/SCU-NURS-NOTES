    • Divided into two parts by function:
      • Conducting airways, through which air moves
        as it passes between the atmosphere and the
        lungs
      • Respiratory tissues of the lungs, where gas
        exchange takes place

                         External Respiration
                  (Also referred to as gas exchange)
     • Exchange of gases from an
       area of high concentration
       to an area of lower
       concentration
     • Takes place in the terminal
       respiratory bronchioles and
       the alveolar ducts and sacs
     • Can be reduced with
       inflammation, fibrosis, or
       decreased area

        How can pharmaceutical
             agents help?
     • Fighting infection
         • Antibiotics, antifungal agents
     • Preventing excess mucus production
     • Decreasing cough
     • Expelling mucus
     • Promoting ventilation or ‘bulk flow of air’
        • Promote airway adequacy by dilating airways or reducing
          excess mucus
     • Reducing inflammation
        • Inflammation can affect both ventilation (bulk flow) and
          respiration (exchange of gasses).

          Antitussives (suppress cough reflex)

     Cough suppressants
       • For Non-productive cough only
          • Depress the cough reflex located in the medullary cough centre (centrally
            acting)
          • Codeine (this is an opioid narcotic),
          • Dextromethorphan (this is a morphine derivative but not considered an opioid)




16




          Antitussives (suppress cough reflex)
     Cough suppressants

       • Codeine: Adverse effects:
          • Drowsiness, sedation (CNS depression), respiratory depression
       • Dextromethorphan: Adverse effects:
          • Allergic reactions, GIT complaints

       • Contraindications for codeine:
          • Pregnancy, young children, especially under 2

       • Dextromethorphan is a pregnancy category A drug

                               Mucolytic agents
             • For productive coughs
                • Chronic bronchitis, pneumonia, cystic fibrosis
             • Decreases mucus viscosity/ liquefies secretions

                • Pharyngitis, GI disturbances

           Antihistamines (H1 antagonists)
               Discussed in the tutorial
     • H1 are first generation (cross BBB; Dimenhydrinate)
       • Side effects include drowsiness and decreased motion sickness
          • These are often used for their ‘side effects’
       • These are associated with higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease
     • H1 can be second generation
       • do not cross BBB to the same degree, so less drowsiness
       • Loratadine and fexofenadine
     • Decrease the vasodilation effects of histamine
     • Antihistamines have varying amounts of secondary
       antimuscarinic actions
       • This is usually more profound with the first generation
         medications: dry mouth, difficulty urinating, eye pain

                                     Asthma
     • A condition characterised by:
        • Bronchospasm
        • Inflammation
        • Hyperactive airways
           • Mucus hyper secretion
           • Oedema
     • Extrinsic
        • Allergens
     • Intrinsic
        • Non-allergic: anxiety, exercise

         It may be best to consider an asthmatic to have a cup. Everything that
          irritates THEIR asthma fills up the cup. When the cup overflows, they
                                have an asthmatic attack.
         Try to get them to eliminate anything that contributes to their asthma.

                   Antihistamines in asthma
     • Antihistamines
       • Block the release of histamine
          • Reduces one mediator in the inflammatory and allergic response
          • Limited use in asthma because the mast cell that releases histamine also
            releases other stronger mediators of inflammation and the asthmatic is
            normally more sensitive to those. But some asthmatics respond.
       • Adverse effects mainly seen with first generation antihistimines
         (the ones that cause drowsiness)
          • Anticholinergic effects if using first generation: don’t!
          • Dry mouth and drowsiness if using first generation: don’t!
          • CNS depressants if using first generation: don’t!
       • Use second generation antihistamines: Loratadine and
         fexofenadine and discontinue if no effect.

                    Bronchodilators: inhaled
     • Beta 2 agonist (mimics the adrenaline on the Beta 2
       receptor on lungs)
       • Beta 2 receptors are located on the smooth muscle of
         blood vessels, bronchi and MAST cells

       • Adverse effects
          • Headache, Tachycardia after large systemic doses

       • Beta 2 selective agonists are long acting or short:
          • Salbutamol (Ventolin) (short acting rescue remedy)
          • Salmeterol (long acting, take with inhaled corticosteroid)

                               Bronchodilators
     • Anticholinergics/ antimuscarinic (against the parasympathetic
       nervous system’s main neurotransmitter, acetylcholine)
       • Block acetylcholine (muscarinic) receptors responsible for causing
         bronchoconstriction: allows the SNS to dominate
          • Bronchodilation
       • Adverse effects
          • Anticholinergic effects
              • Dry mouth, dizziness
       • Ipratropium bromide (Atrovent) (inhaler, nebuliser)
       • Interactions
          • Other anticholinergic drugs




40




             Leukotriene receptor antagonists
     • Leukotrienes are inflammatory mediators
     • Oral dose (PO; (means PER OS; Oral)) normally in addition to
       inhaled corticosteroids
     • Antagonists
       • Block leukotriene action
          • Reduced inflammatory response
          • Reduction in oedema, mucus secretion, smooth muscle contraction
       • Contraindication
          • Liver impairment (metabolised by the liver)
          • Pregnancy
       • Adverse effects:
          • Diarrhoea, nausea (Absorbed rapidly in the GI tract)
       • Zafirlukast (Accolate) and Montelukast


41




                                Corticosteroids
     Inhaled corticosteroids
     • Should be taken when the asthmatic starts reaching for their
       rescue inhaler more than 3 times per week
       • Decrease the inflammatory response
          • Prevent the rupture of mast cells > decreased chemical mediator response >
            reduced inflammatory and immune response = reduction in oedema,
            bronchoconstriction, mucus production = increased airflow
       • Adverse effects
          • Pharyngeal, laryngeal fungal infections (thrush)
       • Budesonide (Pulmicort)
       • Fluticasone (Seretide)

                               Corticosteroids
     • Oral corticosteroids
       • Suppress immune and inflammatory response
          • Reducing the formation of prostaglandins and leukotrienes (required for
            inflammatory response)
          • Inhibit and impair the function of WBCs
       • Adverse effects
          • Infection, hyperglycaemia
       • Prednisone (PO)
