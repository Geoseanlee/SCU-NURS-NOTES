In this final chapter of Module 2, we explore the respiratory system pharmacology. Our objectives for this chapter include the following:

  • Briefly describe the structure and function of the lungs including the immune system response in the lungs
  • Briefly describe the pathophysiology of allergic rhinitis and asthma identifying and describing the actions of common pharmacological agents used in their treatment
  • Compare the difference between mucolytic agents and expectorants, citing examples of each.

When you add up all the surface area for diffusion it is between
 the size of a tennis court and a football field. 
One of the important things to consider when we are trying to understand about pulmonary embolisms versus blockage of blood to other portions of the body is that the lungs have two sources of blood. One source is the pulmonary circulation, and that is the deoxygenated blood coming from the right side of the heart. But we also have the circulation that takes fresh blood from the left side of the heart and supplies the oxygen to the cells. A person with a small pulmonary embolism for instance, will have the flow of the pulmonary artery blocked, but will still be getting fresh blood to the cells for their oxygen and energy needs, so there will not be as much damage to the cellular structures, as long as the problem is resolved within a few months.

In this picture and associated diagram, the apices of the lungs are pictured
 just about right – possibly a bit lower than they should be. Compare the drawing to the X-ray and you would find that there is a bit of the lung missing from the drawing. 
The drawing over this young man depicts that pleural space which is well endowed with pain receptors and can fill with fluid from a number of problems. This would be called pleural effusion if not infected or 
pleuritis
 if there is an associated infection. Fluid from the pleural space does not drain into the lungs. It is taken out of the lungs by the lymphatic system. An infection in the pleural space just like an infection in the meninges, the pericardium, or the peritoneum is likely to be or turn into a clinical emergency for a number of reasons. The bacteria proliferate rapidly in these areas and the toxins can are transported all through the system very quickly. 
I would like to reiterate the fact that 
pleuritis
 is the same as pleurisy, which is the same thing as an infection in the pleural cavity, which is the same thing as an infected pleural effusion. The space there is separate from the alveoli. If the alveoli fill up with fluid, it is called pulmonary oedema or if infected, pneumonia.

Ventilation is 
bulk flow of air. You ventilate a room by opening a window. If the breeze remains constant the ventilation increases the larger the window gets. 
Obstructive disorders decrease ventilation, like closing the window. Obstructive disorders include asthma which decreases the diameter of the lumen of the airways because of the constriction of the airways and the excess mucus. 
Obstructive disorders include chronic bronchitis which decreases airway diameter because of the remodeling that occurs due to chronic insult. 
Obstructive disorders include emphysema which decreases our ability to exhale
So obstructive disorders decrease our ability to get air in and or out of the lungs.

## ppt/notesSlides/notesSlide9.xml
13
External Respiration is the other function of the respiratory system.
 Respiration originally meant the 
process by which organisms exchange gases, especially oxygen and carbon dioxide, with the environment. The term respiration is now becoming used to explain both functions of the lungs (ventilation
 and external respiration) or even confused with ventilation. So perhaps we should start using the term gas exchange. 
The main factors in gas exchange include the distance for diffusion of gases and a decreased surface area for gas exchange
For instance, the reason that a large pulmonary embolism or multiple small pulmonary emboli are problematic is because there is less surface area available for gas exchange. 
Some of the occupational lung disorders adversely affect gas exchange because they make the surface area thicker, which decreases gas exchange
