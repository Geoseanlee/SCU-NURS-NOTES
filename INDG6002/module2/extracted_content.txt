===== Module overview [_7400118_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400042_1?view=content&state=view
The majority of non-Indigenous Australians know little of the history of Australia from Indigenous peoples' perspectives. Indigenous Australian Peoples continue to be negatively impacted. Today, this is reflected in the significant health and wellbeing challenges Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples still face.
In Module 2 we consider the invasion of Australia, the consequences of which underlie and fundamentally relate to the much discussed health ‘Gap’. ‘Epidemic’ proportions of chronic disease, intergenerational trauma, poverty, over incarceration, family violence, poor physical and mental health, and loss of cultural and family connection can be related to the British invasion.
Colonisation profoundly impacted over 300 Aboriginal Nations familial, cultural and community connections across Australia. Dispossession, protectionism, assimilation and attempted cultural genocide are among the flawed and racist policies that still impact Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples health and wellbeing. The Stolen Generations, racism and the ongoing removal of Indigenous children from families and culture remain significant threats to the SEWB and health of all Indigenous communities.
The trauma of invasion underlies many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples reluctance to engage with Western healthcare services today.
Module purpose
By the end of this module you will be able to:
recognise how colonisation continues to substantially impact the lives of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples
apply a critical lens to the ongoing damage of ‘Stolen Generations’ on the social, cultural and health status of all Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples
describe/summarise how racism impacts Indigenous Australian peoples' health

===== Colonisation: Historical and contemporary impacts [_7400120_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400044_1?view=content&state=view
Since the initial shock of the arrival of the British, Australian First Nations Peoples have been subjected to flawed and racist goverment policies, initially aimed at physical and cultural genocide. The legacy and ongoing impacts of this history continue to impact all Australians. The dominant negative and deficit based narrative on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples health continues, despite government policies and rhetoric being focused on reconciliation and Closing the Gap (Bond & Singh, 2020). Australia’s approach to United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) is another example of government rhetoric not matching government action. Although Australia has formally endorsed the Declaration, it still hasn’t incorporated it into domestic law. Government emphasise the importance of respecting Indigenous knowledges and claim to support Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples rights to manage their own health, yet they stop short of embedding those commitments (from UNDRIP) in legislation (Pitt, 2025).
During this module, we will explore the key stages of the colonisation process and its impact on the health and wellbeing of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.
These stages include:
Initial colonisation
Successive flawed/racist government policies and practices
Dispossession
Ongoing removal of children by the state
Impacts of institutional racism.

===== Colonisation: historical and contemporary impacts [_7400121_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400045_1?view=content&state=view
‘Hidden’ history
The British took Australia under the lie of ‘terra nullius’ (land belonging to no one) and immediately commenced the systematic removal of Traditional Owners from Country. Dispossession, massacres and warfare, along with diseases introduced by colonisers, took an immense toll on First Nations populations within only several decades of colonisation (Best & Fredericks, 2019).
‘Terra nullius’ remained as an active government policy until the Mabo Decision in the High Court in 1992. The Mabo Case was a landmark legal case in Australia that recognised the land rights of the Meriam people, traditional owners of the Murray Islands (which include the islands of Mer, Dauer and Waier) in the Torres Strait. The Mabo Case was successful in overturning the myth that at the time of colonisation Australia was ‘terra nullius’ or 'land belonging to no one'. The High Court finally recognised that Indigenous peoples had lived in Australia for thousands of years and enjoyed rights to their land according to their own laws and customs.

===== The Stolen Generations [_7400122_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400046_1?view=content&state=view
It is estimated that as many as one in three Indigenous children were forcibly removed from their families between 1910 and the 1970s, profoundly impacting almost all Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities across Australia. Children were forcibly removed from families and communities as a result of race-based policies set up by both State and Federal governments. Aboriginal children who were placed in homes, or adopted or fostered out to non-Indigenous families suffered and still suffer today from grief and trauma as a consequence of losing connections to family, identity, land, language and culture. These children are known as the Stolen Generations (Healing Foundation, 2021).
"National Sorry Day 2015, Historic Stolen Generations Commemorative Badge 1998, Australia" by butupa is licensed under CC BY 2.0
Genocide: The colonial agenda
The Aborigines Act 1905 placed into law the absolute right of the State over Aboriginal Peoples, demonstrating how racist beliefs merged into legislation. Aboriginal Peoples were considered to be less than human and government legislation was used to control and confine them away from white settlers and society. Very few Aboriginal people evaded the ruthless legislation that controlled and governed their lives in all aspects. ‘Half Caste’ (considered a highly disrespectful and insulting term) children were to be taken from their families so they could be ‘civilised’ and have ‘better opportunities in life’ away from their ‘savage’ origins (Dudgeon, Milroy & Walker, 2014).
Indigenous children were forcibly separated from their families and communities since the early days of European occupation of Australia (Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission, 1997). The exact numbers of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children removed by authorities between 1788 and 1997 remains unknown. However, it has been extimated that up to 50,000 Indigenous children were taken from their families and placed in government institutions and white foster homes. It is estimated that between 1912 and 1962 two out of three mixed descent Aboriginal children spent some of their childhood separated from their families and communitites as part of the Removal policy, driven by government policies such as Assimilation (Bennett, 2019, pp. 3-30).

===== Racism: Continuing the violence of colonisation [_7400124_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400048_1?view=content&state=view
The ongoing violence of colonisation is perpetuated through racism, both on an individual and institutional level, which is experienced by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples every day. There are many different types of racism, both overt and covert. Examples of institutional racism occur in most mainstream health and social services where the western system of appointment times and service delivery demonstrates a lack of respect for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples alternative ways of thinking and understanding (Ranzij, McConnochie & Nolan, 2009).
We identify and discuss institutional racism as a major barrier to improving health outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.
Institutionalised racism: can be defined as
“societal systems and patterns that have the net effect of imposing oppressive or otherwise negative conditions on identifiable groups based on race or ethnicity “ (Durey et al., 2011, p. 19).
Within health care, this is evident in systems founded on Western biomedical models of care and managed predominantly by white, middle‑class Australians, which often fail to understand the language, social contexts, and lived realities of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. As a result, “cross‑cultural misunderstandings undermine the quality of care to Aboriginal patients” (Durey et al., 2011, p. 19).
This understanding aligns with MacPherson’s definition of institutional racism as “the collective failure of an organisation to provide an appropriate and professional service to people because of their colour, culture, or ethnic origin.” Such failure may be detected in organisational processes, attitudes, and behaviours that result in discrimination through “unwitting prejudice, ignorance, thoughtlessness and racist stereotyping,” ultimately disadvantaging minority ethnic peoples (MacPherson, cited in Bourke, Marie & Marrie, 2019, p. 612).

Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400051_1?view=content&state=view
This module has provided a brief overview of historical and ongoing events and issues that remain as barriers to accessible and acceptable health and social services for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. The deliberate omission of the truth about the brutality of colonisation, as exemplared by the taking of this continent by the British using terra nullius is described. The immediate and transgenerational trauma inflicted on Indigenous families and communities from the Stolen Generations has been touched upon. Institutional and systemic racism is defined and detailed.
