## ppt/slides/slide15.xml
The future of social work in Australia relies on dismantling colonial structures and rethinking professional practice. 
Decolonising means recognising that inequity stems from colonialism, not Indigenous peoples, and requires health and social care workers to challenge the dominance of Western culture.
 By embracing cultural safety and valuing Indigenous ways of knowing, professionals can help redress systemic imbalances and foster more equitable relationships. 
Walter, M., & 
Baltra
-Ulloa, A. (2019). Decolonising social work: Indigenous perspectives and practices

## ppt/slides/slide16.xml
There is significant evidence confirming that Aboriginal Torres Strait Islander Peoples health continues to be a major challenge to governments, its agencies, health professionals and their communities
It is a statistical fact that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples are the poorest, unhealthiest, less employed, worse housed and most imprisoned Australians, but only half the community believes Aboriginal people are worse off than other Australians (only around 30 per cent believe they are ‘a lot’ worse off), then there is a significant gap between the facts and what many believe about the position of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples 
(Newspoll survey 2000 cited in Behrendt 2003: 65)
We believe the critical issues that underlie the lack of improvement in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples health are the continuation of colonisation, through the dominance of western worldviews in research, policy, planning, and praxis; the lack of Indigenous health models and the dominance of the bio-medical model; and everyday personal and institutional racism. 
(Sherwood & Edwards 2006, pp. 182-3)
Why decolonisation is critical

## ppt/slides/slide21.xml
The Yarrabah Health and Wellbeing Model
What Happened:
Yarrabah community created its own health service.
Indigenous leaders took control of governance and service design.
Gurriny 
Yealamucka
 Health Service became the main provider of primary care.
Staff are mostly local Aboriginal workers.
Programs include cultural camps, youth leadership, healing circles, and chronic disease support.
Health promotion is delivered through relationships, culture, and community events.
Why It Matters:
Decolonisation
: Shifts power from colonial systems to Indigenous governance.
Strengths‑based practice
: Focuses on culture, resilience, identity, and community strengths.
Cultural safety
: Care is respectful, relational, and grounded in Indigenous worldviews.
Self‑determination
: Community decides how health services operate.
Structural change
: New funding models, new workforce structures, new ways of delivering care.
Positive wellbeing outcomes
: Increased trust, better access, stronger identity, healthier community.

## ppt/slides/slide22.xml
Decolonisation
 – Steps to take 
Be specific about Nations
 Name the exact Country, Nation, or language group. Avoid broad terms unless discussing pan‑Indigenous issues.
Use community‑preferred names
 Follow the spelling, terms, and language used by local Elders and knowledge holders.
Focus on strengths, not stereotypes
 Highlight resilience, innovation, and deep knowledge traditions — not deficit narratives.
Recognise Indigenous knowledges as knowledge systems
 They are complex, relational, place‑based, intellectual — not myths or “add‑ons”.
Acknowledge cultures as living
 Indigenous cultures continue to grow, adapt, and thrive today.
Use respectful language
 Shift from “they believe…” to “In Noongar knowledge systems…” or “According to Bundjalung Elders…”.
Learn the full history
 Understand colonisation, dispossession, resistance, and ongoing impacts without minimising harm.
Know your own position
 Be aware of how your identity and background shape the way you learn and speak about Indigenous knowledges.
Prioritise Indigenous voices
 Use materials, teachings, and stories from Indigenous people. Do not speak for communities.
Avoid generalising
 There are hundreds of Nations — always identify whose knowledge you’re referring to.
Include Indigenous perspectives regularly
 Indigenous content should appear across subjects, not as a one‑off topic.
Think in terms of relationships
 Indigenous knowledges are connected to Country, kinship, community, and responsibility.
Balance truth with strength
 Learn about colonial impacts alongside survival, creativity, and ongoing cultural strength.
Stay humble
 It’s okay not to know — be open to learning, listening, and being corrected.
