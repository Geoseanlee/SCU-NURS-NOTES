## ppt/slides/slide26.xml
Privilege and Oppression
Are about unjust distributions of power
Privilege is unearned advantage 
This is not about how good or bad someone’s life is, or whether or not they are happy
Individuals do not choose to get those advantages, and do not have them by merit
Privilege is concerned with whether a given society, economic system, or institution unjustly benefits certain groups
Oppression is unearned disadvantage
The same points hold true for oppression.
So, an individual is oppressed in the event that they get a range of disadvantages due to their socio-economic position
Their disadvantages are not a consequence of their choices. Merit  is irrelevant

## ppt/slides/slide28.xml
Racism –Systemic racism, and how it presents as Institutional or Structural racism
Systemic racism is:
“When the policies and practices of institutions result in unfair treatment of some groups compared to others. Like everyday racism, systemic racism does not necessarily target a particular person.”
Institutional racism [based on policies and entrenched societal ‘norms’]
 
This is when racism is established as a normal behaviour within an organisation or society. It often results in discrimination by people who are doing jobs that others have given to them. For example, police are sometimes criticised for racial profiling, and police agencies now make efforts to eliminate institutional racism;
Structural/Systemic racism [based on the organisations and structures that uphold these systems]
 This refers to inequalities found in societies that tend to exclude some groups of people. For example, when certain groups are under-represented in fields like the media, the legal profession, or politics, it can lead to inequalities in other areas too.

Healing Through Culture: The Woorabinda Men’s and Women’s Healing Camps (Central Queensland)
What Happened:
Woorabinda created cultural healing camps on Country.
Elders, cultural mentors, and Aboriginal health workers lead the program.
Activities include ceremony, storytelling, Yarning circles, bush foods, and time on Country.
Healing focuses on identity, belonging, and cultural pride.
Trauma is acknowledged but not centred — strength is the focus.
Why It Matters:
Strengths‑based healing:
 Culture, identity, and community are central.
Intergenerational trauma:
 Healing through connection, not deficit framing.
SEWB:
 Supports spirit, mind, body, family, community, and Country.
Yarning & Dadirri:
 Build trust, safety, and deep understanding.
Cultural continuity:
 Elders’ knowledge strengthens wellbeing.
Community leadership:
 Healing is Indigenous‑led and culturally grounded.

10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing
Start with a clear main point: 
Emphasise that short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Example sentence starters such as: 
“This concept refers to…”
 or 
“In the scenario, this is evident when…”
.
2. Explain the key concept briefly:  D
efine or clarify the concept in their own words before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms 
3. Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
 
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian 
Indigenous Peoples understandings 
of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background information.
4. Use scholarly or unit evidence: 
Short responses still require academic grounding. Evidence can be a unit concept, framework, or scholarly idea. Keep it brief. 
5. Write concisely and coherently: 
Use short, direct sentences - improves clarity and reduces grammatical errors - avoid long introductions, unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing continued …
6. Maintain respectful terminology: 
Remember: Correct and consistent use of your chosen term
7. Conclude by linking back to the question: 	
A final sentence should reinforce the main point to close the paragraph logically and signals relevance to the marker.
8. Avoid common pitfalls: 
Highlight typical issues: over‑description, missing the main point, unclear links to Article 3, lack of evidence, incorrect terminology, and overly long sentences. 
9. Use postgraduate verbs: 
Encourage verbs that show analysis and interpretation: 
analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise
. Discourage vague verbs like

Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without undergoing peer review.

INDG6002 - GenAI Use
In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally restricted to protect the integrity and authenticity of the learning process. Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to culturally responsive learning—something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-generated content.
