## ppt/slides/slide11.xml
Akeyulerre Healing Centre 
What happens:
Aboriginal Elders created a healing centre in Alice Springs.
Activities include bush medicine making, trips to Country, language teaching, and storytelling.
Families and kin are involved in healing.
Decisions are made by the community (community‑controlled).
Culture is treated as medicine.
Why it matters:
Cultural determinants:
 Culture, Country, language, kinship, identity.
Strengths‑based practice:
 Focus on resilience, pride, and cultural continuity.
SEWB:
 Supports spirit, mind, body, family, community, and Country.
Community governance:
 Elders lead and decide.
Holistic wellbeing:
 Culture improves emotional, spiritual, and social health.
Contrast with deficit models:
 Moves away from “problems” to cultural strengths.
https://www.akeyulerre.org.au/

Recap (Module 2): Racism & Bias
Racism is a stressor that has been reported to affect both mental and physical health. 
There is a growing body of literature that validates the public health impact of racial bias, implicit and explicit, on the lives and health of First Nations Peoples.
Stereotyping and negative discourse enable the racism to continue – t
he construction of understanding Aboriginal peoples “reinforces unequal power relationships; in other words, an image of sick, 
disorganised
 communities can be used to justify paternalism and dependency”
 (Bond & Singh, 2020)

## ppt/slides/slide24.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing
Start with a clear main point: 
Emphasise that short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Example sentence starters such as: 
“This concept refers to…”
 or 
“In the scenario, this is evident when…”
.
2. Explain the key concept briefly:  D
efine or clarify the concept in their own words before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms 
3. Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
 
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian Indigenous Peoples understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background information.
4. Use scholarly or unit evidence: 
Short responses still require academic grounding. Evidence can be a unit concept, framework, or scholarly idea. Keep it brief. 
5. Write concisely and coherently: 
Use short, direct sentences - improves clarity and reduces grammatical errors - avoid long introductions, unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

## ppt/slides/slide25.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing continued …
6. Maintain respectful terminology: 
Remember: Correct and consistent use of your chosen term
7. Conclude by linking back to the question: 	
A final sentence should reinforce the main point to close the paragraph logically and signals relevance to the marker.
8. Avoid common pitfalls: 
Highlight typical issues: over‑description, missing the main point, unclear links to Article 3, lack of evidence, incorrect terminology, and overly long sentences. 
9. Use postgraduate verbs: 
Encourage verbs that show analysis and interpretation: 
analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise
. Discourage vague verbs like 
talk about
 or 
discuss
.
10. Follow a simple structure (PEEL): Point → Explain → Evidence/Scenario → Link
.

Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without undergoing peer review.
Step 4: Write Your Response

## ppt/slides/slide36.xml
INDG6002 - GenAI Use
In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally restricted to protect the integrity and authenticity of the learning process. Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to culturally responsive learning—something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-generated content.
