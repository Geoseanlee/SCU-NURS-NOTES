Overview
What does cultural safety mean to you? 
In this module, we will look at the origins and meaning of Cultural Safety and provide a rationale for why this is an effective framework for working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. 
Five core principles of Cultural Safety are described and discussed in detail. 
There are a range of terms used when working with culturally diverse groups and it is easy to be distracted by the terminology (e.g. cultural competency, cultural awareness). 
This module will focus on Cultural Safety and Culturally safe practice 
and how these apply to your professional practice when working with 
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples
.

Transcultural Nursing: Early Approach to Culture in Health Care
Developed in the 1960s, influenced by anthropology and Madeleine Leininger’s work
First nursing model to integrate cultural considerations into health care practice
Focused on teaching health professionals about cultural differences
Culture was narrowly defined as ethnicity or race
Nurses learned cultural protocols for clients from different ethnic or religious backgrounds
Limitation: risk of relying on stereotypes about behaviour based on ethnicity

## ppt/slides/slide13.xml
Cultural Safety – An Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Perspective 
Discussing and addressing cultural safety in Australia is essential because racism and the enduring effects of colonisation continue to drive significant health disparities for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples
.
 
(Mohamed et al.,2024)
As previously stated in this unit, racism and lack of cultural safety drives Indigenous Peoples fear and avoidance of health and social services. Lack of trust between Indigenous Peoples and western provided health and social services  has a strong colonial history (Gatwiri, Rix, & Rotumah, 2021).

## ppt/slides/slide14.xml
AHPRA and Cultural Safety in Australia
Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) 
regulates over 800,000 health practitioners. 
Cultural safety 
is now a guiding principle in the National Law and embedded in professional codes of conduct. 
AHPRA’s Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health and Cultural Safety Strategy 2020–2025 aims to: 
Eliminate racism in healthcare. 
Make clinical and cultural safety inseparable.
Ensure cultural safety is defined by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.
 
Cultural safety is reinforced through:
Mandatory education and CPD frameworks.
Accreditation standards for health programs.
Monitoring and reporting systems. 
Indigenous Leadership at the core
AHPRA’s cultural safety initiatives are Indigenous-led, ensuring Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander voices shape policy and practice.

## ppt/slides/slide24.xml
SBS Report on Naomi Williams(Oct,2018)
Naomi Williams was a 27-year-old Wiradjuri (First Nations) woman, six months pregnant, who died in 2016 from sepsis. 
She had visited the local rural hospital many times (reportedly 18 times in 8 months) for symptoms like severe pain, vomiting, and nausea. 
Despite her repeated visits, she was given minimal treatment (in one instance, just two Panadol) and sent home without a proper assessment.
Experts at the inquest argued that there was 
implicit racial bias
 — hospital staff made stereotypical assumptions about her, including that she was a “drug seeker” because she had said she used marijuana
This stereotyping is linked to broader systemic racism in healthcare: for example, evidence was brought forward that Indigenous patients often receive fewer procedures than non-Indigenous patients

## ppt/slides/slide26.xml
Activity: 
Case Scenario
David, an Aboriginal man, presents to the emergency department with chest pain. He is accompanied by a family member. The nurse begins asking clinical questions without introducing themselves or acknowledging the family. The nurse assumes David speaks English fluently and does not ask about language preferences or cultural needs. When David hesitates to answer, the nurse becomes impatient and says, “We need to hurry; this is serious.” The family member tries to speak, but the nurse dismisses them, saying, “We only need the patient’s answers.”
1.	What examples of culturally unsafe care can you identify in this scenario?
2.	How could the nurse have provided culturally safe care?
3.	What strategies would you use to minimise power imbalances and respect cultural protocols?
4.	Reflect on your own practice: How would you ensure cultural safety in similar situations?
