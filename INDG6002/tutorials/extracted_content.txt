Acknowledgment of Country 
An Acknowledgement of Country is a demonstration of respect for the Traditional Caretakers of the land on which a meeting or event is being held. It is a recognition of the continuing relationship between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and their Country, acknowledging their continuing connections to land, sea, and community.

## ppt/slides/slide5.xml
Gnibi Elders Principles
Indigenous Knowledge is a relevant, ethical, intelligent, effective and a useful way of knowing.
Indigenous peoples are active, able and worthy peoples.
The truth about our histories and value of Indigenous ways are determined by us, we carry this knowledge.
Elders hold our Aboriginal Spirituality and our culture close. We choose what is open and shared and what will be closed. Aboriginal spirituality is the basis for life Indigenous Education and Cultural Competency, it infuses everything.
Our knowledge is relational. Our principles are the basis for SCU to establish and sustain an active informed and respectful relationship with Indigenous Elders who will give considered collective input. The continuance of this relationship through the Elders group is essential.
Culture is sacred to us. We are and own this living culture. We do not need a ‘book’. We share with you so that you can learn how to listen and hear what we say.
Identity is based upon things [inside] beneath the surface. Empowered, individual, affirmed identity is the basis of positive, productive, healthy and non-destructive lives - opportunities to engage and build knowledge builds [strong mob] through [inside learning].
Learning everyday with each other has to be informed and sustained by a politic of open, positive and ethical interactions.
We as Elders have presented these things all our lives without being heard –we are often listened to but our message is not heard nor heeded. We take this opportunity to speak in the understanding that you will listen in the right way, hear us and learn.

GNIBI GenAI use 
 
In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally 
restricted
 to protect the 
integrity and authenticity of the learning process.
 Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to culturally responsive learning, something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-generated content.
 Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT, 
may not be used 
for this Assessment Task.

independently

 use of GenAI

## ppt/slides/slide6.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing
Start with a clear main point: 
Emphasise that short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Example sentence starters such as: 
“This concept refers to…”
 or 
“In the scenario, this is evident when…”
.
2. Explain the key concept briefly:  D
efine or clarify the concept in their own words before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms 
3. Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
 
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian 
Indigenous Peoples 
understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background information.
4. Use scholarly or unit evidence: 
Short responses still require academic grounding. Evidence can be a unit concept, framework, or scholarly idea. Keep it brief. 
5. Write concisely and coherently: 
Use short, direct sentences - improves clarity and reduces grammatical errors - avoid long introductions, unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

## ppt/slides/slide7.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing 
continued …
7
6. Maintain respectful terminology: 
Remember: Correct and consistent use of your chosen term
7. Conclude by linking back to the question: 	
A final sentence should reinforce the main point to close the paragraph logically and signals relevance to the marker.
8. Avoid common pitfalls: 
Highlight typical issues: over‑description, missing the main point, unclear links to Article 3, lack of evidence, incorrect terminology, and overly long sentences. 
9. Use postgraduate verbs: 
Encourage verbs that show analysis and interpretation: 
analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise
. Discourage vague verbs like 
talk about
 or 
discuss
.
10. Follow a simple structure (PEEL): Point → Explain → Evidence/Scenario → Link
.

Assessment 1 Overview
 
Purpose of the Assessment
Builds understanding of 
Australian Indigenous perspectives of health and wellbeing
.
Introduces 
self‑determination
 as a core principle in 
UNDRIP Article 3
.

Australian Indigenous wholistic health
 and

.

.
Develops foundational knowledge for 
culturally safe practice
 in health, social services, and community work.
Strengthens 
critical thinking
 and culturally grounded analysis for professional contexts.
Task
 
Description
Read 
Article 3 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP)
:
"Indigenous peoples have the right to self-determination. By virtue of that right, they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their economic, social and cultural development."
Write a 
1,000-word short response
 explaining how this article relates to Australian Indigenous Peoples understandings of health and wellbeing.
 

## ppt/slides/slide10.xml
Assessment 1 - Instructions
 
Step 1: Read UNDRIP Article 3
Consider what the term 
self-determination
 means and why it is important for Australian Indigenous Peoples.
Step 2: Review Unit Content
Use learning materials from:
Module 1: Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing.
Module 2: Colonisation and its impacts on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Step 3: Research
Locate additional scholarly literature that supports your discussion.
Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without undergoing peer review.
Step 4: Write Your Response
Your response should explain how Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, self-determination, and community-accepted communication styles are interconnected, and how these relate to the ongoing impacts of colonisation on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

## ppt/slides/slide6.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing
Start with a clear main point: 
Emphasise that short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Example sentence starters such as: 
“This concept refers to…”
 or 
“In the scenario, this is evident when…”
.
2. Explain the key concept briefly:  D
efine or clarify the concept in their own words before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms 
3. Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
 
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian 
Indigenous Peoples 
understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background information.
4. Use scholarly or unit evidence: 
Short responses still require academic grounding. Evidence can be a unit concept, framework, or scholarly idea. Keep it brief. 
5. Write concisely and coherently: 
Use short, direct sentences - improves clarity and reduces grammatical errors - avoid long introductions, unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

## ppt/slides/slide7.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing 
continued …
7
6. Maintain respectful terminology: 
Remember: Correct and consistent use of your chosen term
7. Conclude by linking back to the question: 	
A final sentence should reinforce the main point to close the paragraph logically and signals relevance to the marker.
8. Avoid common pitfalls: 
Highlight typical issues: over‑description, missing the main point, unclear links to Article 3, lack of evidence, incorrect terminology, and overly long sentences. 
9. Use postgraduate verbs: 
Encourage verbs that show analysis and interpretation: 
analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise
. Discourage vague verbs like 
talk about
 or 
discuss
.
10. Follow a simple structure (PEEL): Point → Explain → Evidence/Scenario → Link
.

Assessment 1 - Instructions
 
Step 1: Read UNDRIP Article 3

self-determination
 means and why it is important for Australian Indigenous Peoples.
Step 2: Review Unit Content
Use learning materials from:
Module 1: Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing.
Module 2: Colonisation and its impacts on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Step 3: Research
Locate additional scholarly literature that supports your discussion.
Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without undergoing peer review.
Step 4: Write Your Response
Your response should explain how Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, self-determination, and community-accepted communication styles are interconnected, and how these relate to the ongoing impacts of colonisation on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

## ppt/slides/slide26.xml
Privilege and Oppression
Are about unjust distributions of power
Privilege is unearned advantage 
This is not about how good or bad someone’s life is, or whether or not they are happy
Individuals do not choose to get those advantages, and do not have them by merit
Privilege is concerned with whether a given society, economic system, or institution unjustly benefits certain groups
Oppression is unearned disadvantage
The same points hold true for oppression.
So, an individual is oppressed in the event that they get a range of disadvantages due to their socio-economic position
Their disadvantages are not a consequence of their choices. Merit  is irrelevant

## ppt/slides/slide28.xml
Racism –Systemic racism, and how it presents as Institutional or Structural racism
Systemic racism is:
“When the policies and practices of institutions result in unfair treatment of some groups compared to others. Like everyday racism, systemic racism does not necessarily target a particular person.”
Institutional racism [based on policies and entrenched societal ‘norms’]
 
This is when racism is established as a normal behaviour within an organisation or society. It often results in discrimination by people who are doing jobs that others have given to them. For example, police are sometimes criticised for racial profiling, and police agencies now make efforts to eliminate institutional racism;
Structural/Systemic racism [based on the organisations and structures that uphold these systems]
 This refers to inequalities found in societies that tend to exclude some groups of people. For example, when certain groups are under-represented in fields like the media, the legal profession, or politics, it can lead to inequalities in other areas too.

## ppt/slides/slide24.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing
Start with a clear main point: 
Emphasise that short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Example sentence starters such as: 
“This concept refers to…”
 or 
“In the scenario, this is evident when…”
.
2. Explain the key concept briefly:  D
efine or clarify the concept in their own words before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms 
3. Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
 
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian Indigenous Peoples understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background information.
4. Use scholarly or unit evidence: 
Short responses still require academic grounding. Evidence can be a unit concept, framework, or scholarly idea. Keep it brief. 
5. Write concisely and coherently: 
Use short, direct sentences - improves clarity and reduces grammatical errors - avoid long introductions, unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

## ppt/slides/slide25.xml
10 Key Steps for Strong Short Response Writing continued …
6. Maintain respectful terminology: 
Remember: Correct and consistent use of your chosen term
7. Conclude by linking back to the question: 	
A final sentence should reinforce the main point to close the paragraph logically and signals relevance to the marker.
8. Avoid common pitfalls: 
Highlight typical issues: over‑description, missing the main point, unclear links to Article 3, lack of evidence, incorrect terminology, and overly long sentences. 
9. Use postgraduate verbs: 
Encourage verbs that show analysis and interpretation: 
analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise
. Discourage vague verbs like 
talk about
 or 
discuss
.
10. Follow a simple structure (PEEL): Point → Explain → Evidence/Scenario → Link
.

Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. Overview
What does cultural safety mean to you? 
In this module, we will look at the origins and meaning of Cultural Safety and provide a rationale for why this is an effective framework for working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. 
Five core principles of Cultural Safety are described and discussed in detail. 
There are a range of terms used when working with culturally diverse groups and it is easy to be distracted by the terminology (e.g. cultural competency, cultural awareness).

## ppt/slides/slide13.xml
Cultural Safety – An Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Perspective 
Discussing and addressing cultural safety in Australia is essential because racism and the enduring effects of colonisation continue to drive significant health disparities for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples
.
 
(Mohamed et al.,2024)
As previously stated in this unit, racism and lack of cultural safety drives Indigenous Peoples fear and avoidance of health and social services. Lack of trust between Indigenous Peoples and western provided health and social services  has a strong colonial history (Gatwiri, Rix, & Rotumah, 2021).

## ppt/slides/slide14.xml
AHPRA and Cultural Safety in Australia
Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) 
regulates over 800,000 health practitioners. 
Cultural safety 
is now a guiding principle in the National Law and embedded in professional codes of conduct. 
AHPRA’s Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health and Cultural Safety Strategy 2020–2025 aims to: 
Eliminate racism in healthcare. 
Make clinical and cultural safety inseparable.
Ensure cultural safety is defined by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.
 
Cultural safety is reinforced through:
Mandatory education and CPD frameworks.
Accreditation standards for health programs.
Monitoring and reporting systems. 
Indigenous Leadership at the core
AHPRA’s cultural safety initiatives are Indigenous-led, ensuring Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander voices shape policy and practice.

## ppt/slides/slide24.xml
SBS Report on Naomi Williams(Oct,2018)
Naomi Williams was a 27-year-old Wiradjuri (First Nations) woman, six months pregnant, who died in 2016 from sepsis. 
She had visited the local rural hospital many times (reportedly 18 times in 8 months) for symptoms like severe pain, vomiting, and nausea. 
Despite her repeated visits, she was given minimal treatment (in one instance, just two Panadol) and sent home without a proper assessment.
Experts at the inquest argued that there was 
implicit racial bias
 — hospital staff made stereotypical assumptions about her, including that she was a “drug seeker” because she had said she used marijuana
This stereotyping is linked to broader systemic racism in healthcare: for example, evidence was brought forward that Indigenous patients often receive fewer procedures than non-Indigenous patients

Case Scenario
David, an Aboriginal man, presents to the emergency department with chest pain. He is accompanied by a family member. The nurse begins asking clinical questions without introducing themselves or acknowledging the family. The nurse assumes David speaks English fluently and does not ask about language preferences or cultural needs. When David hesitates to answer, the nurse becomes impatient and says, “We need to hurry; this is serious.” The family member tries to speak, but the nurse dismisses them, saying, “We only need the patient’s answers.”
1.	What examples of culturally unsafe care can you identify in this scenario?
2.	How could the nurse have provided culturally safe care?
3.	What strategies would you use to minimise power imbalances and respect cultural protocols?
4.	Reflect on your own practice: How would you ensure cultural safety in similar situations?

## ppt/slides/slide27.xml
Yarning
Yarning is a word used by many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, meaning ‘Let’s have a chat’ (Bessarab, 2010). 
Use of minimal questions and prompts, with acute listening skills are fundamental to allowing comfortable and informal stories and discussions to unfold. 
Do not interrupt, this shows lack of respect for what is being shared
Take on the role of listener, learner, and student

 – Steps to take 
Be specific about Nations
 Name the exact Country, Nation, or language group. Avoid broad terms unless discussing pan‑Indigenous issues.

Acknowledge cultures as living
 Indigenous cultures continue to grow, adapt, and thrive today.
Use respectful language
 Shift from “they believe…” to “In Noongar knowledge systems…” or “According to Bundjalung Elders…”.
Learn the full history
 Understand colonisation, dispossession, resistance, and ongoing impacts without minimising harm.
Know your own position
 Be aware of how your identity and background shape the way you learn and speak about Indigenous knowledges.
Prioritise Indigenous voices
 Use materials, teachings, and stories from Indigenous people. Do not speak for communities.
Avoid generalising
 There are hundreds of Nations — always identify whose knowledge you’re referring to.
Include Indigenous perspectives regularly
 Indigenous content should appear across subjects, not as a one‑off topic.
Think in terms of relationships
 Indigenous knowledges are connected to Country, kinship, community, and responsibility.
Balance truth with strength
 Learn about colonial impacts alongside survival, creativity, and ongoing cultural strength.
Stay humble

## ppt/slides/slide9.xml
Primary Health Care (PHC)
Primary Health Care is a whole-of-society approach to health that aims at ensuring the highest possible level of health and wellbeing and their equitable distribution by focusing on people’s needs as early as possible along the continuum from health promotion and disease prevention to treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care, and as close as feasible to people’s everyday environment.
WHO, 2023 

## ppt/slides/slide10.xml
Primary Health Care (PHC) continued …
A primary health care approach includes three components:
Meeting people’s health needs throughout their lives,
Addressing the broader determinants of health through multisectoral policy and action, and
Empowering individuals, families, and communities to take charge of their own health.

## ppt/slides/slide12.xml
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples Definition of:
Primary Health Care (PHC)
“Primary health care (PHC)
 is … generally the first ‘layer’ of health care services that individuals, families and the community encounter that usually provide comprehensive and continuous care. Primary health care includes general practitioners (GPs), Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Workers and Practitioners, nurses, allied health professionals, midwives, pharmacists and dentists. The services provided as part of primary health care are broad, and include health promotion and education, prevention and screening, early intervention, treatment and management.”
https://healthinfonet.ecu.edu.au/learn/health-system/primary-health-care/

## ppt/slides/slide13.xml
NACCHO
Peak national body representing 146 ACCHO’s in urban, rural and remote areas
“A process which allows the local Aboriginal community to be involved in its affairs in accordance with whatever protocols or procedures are determined by the Community”
Objectives
To ameliorate poverty within the Aboriginal community through self determination
Promote, develop and expand health services through ACCHS
Represent and advocate for health service delivery, health information, research, public health, health financing and programs
To deliver holistic and culturally appropriate health and health-related services to the Aboriginal community.
https://www.naccho.org.au/acchos/

## ppt/slides/slide14.xml
Recap: ACCHO
An ACCHO is a primary health care service initiated and operated by the local Aboriginal community to deliver holistic, comprehensive, and culturally appropriate health care 
to the community which controls it
, through a locally elected Board of Management.

## ppt/slides/slide15.xml
Coomealla Health Aboriginal Corporation’s Community‑Controlled Primary Care Model (Wentworth, NSW)
What Happened:
Wentworth lost both GP clinics in 2018 → major access crisis.
CHAC (an ACCHO) stepped in to establish a new primary care clinic.
Aboriginal board led all decisions: service design, staffing, priorities.
Clinic built around holistic, culturally grounded care.
Salaried GP model + Medicare 19(2) exemption ensured sustainability.
Outreach and allied health integrated across multiple towns.
Why It Matters:
Self‑determination:
 Indigenous governance drives system redesign.
Holistic health:
 Care reflects SEWB, family, culture, and community.
Cultural safety:
 Longer appointments, relational practice, trusted environment.
Strengths‑based health promotion:
 Delivered through relationships, not pamphlets.
Community‑controlled solutions:
 Structural innovation replaces failed mainstream models.
Improved outcomes:
 Access, continuity, trust, and community engagement strengthened.
https://www.chac.org.au/post/welcome-to-coomealla-health-aboriginal-corporation-chac-community-controlled-healthcare-in-dareto

## ppt/slides/slide29.xml
A human rights-based approach to health
Human rights provide a framework for addressing the consequences of health inequality experienced by First Nations Peoples. 
This includes recognising its underlying causes as well as the interconnections with other issues. 
Human rights require more than a rhetorical acknowledgement of the existence of inequality and general commitments to overcome this situation at some unspecified time in the future.
Key elements include: 
Availability, Accessibility, Non-discrimination
The 
National Strategic Framework
 identifies as a key result area achieving a health system that is more effective and responsive to First Nations Peoples needs.

## ppt/slides/slide42.xml
INDG6002 GenAI Use
In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally restricted to protect the integrity and authenticity of the learning process. Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to culturally responsive learning—something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-generated content.
