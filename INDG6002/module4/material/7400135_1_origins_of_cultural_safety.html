Origins of Cultural Safety

Original Blackboard item · _7400135_1

The epistemological origins of cultural safety stem from the ongoing negative impacts of colonisation on Māori people in Aotearoa/NZ in 1989. Cultural safety emerged from the work of Māori nurse Irihapeti Ramsden, in response to her concerns at witnessing the discriminatory and inequitable treatment of her people within the Aotearoa/NZ health system.

Cultural safety is now recognised globally as an effective practice framework for working with all Indigenous Peoples. It is increasingly being cited as most effective for working with all minority cultural groups (Lokugamage , Rix, Fleming, Khetan, Meredith & Hastie, 2021).

Cultural safety is promoted by antiracism. It is an agile practice-based process of change for eliminating individual, institutional and systemic racism (Tascon & Gatwiri, 2020). As mentioned previously, racism and the lack of cultural safety drives Indigenous Peoples fear and avoidance of health and social services. The lack of trust between Indigenous Peoples and Western health and social services has a strong colonial history (Gatwiri, Rix & Rotumah, 2021).

"Many of our people, don’t feel safe in hospital. I’ve heard people say they would rather die than go to hospital where they are made sicker by encounters of racism. I hear Aboriginal people say “I don’t trust them (medical professionals) … they might want to hurt me deliberately”. This is because we have a history of genocide. This is our history. Our ancestors are genocide survivors. We need more First Nations workers and racially safe hospitals so that our people can feel safe to seek treatment"

— (Gatwiri, Rix and Rotumah, 2021 p. 6).

Cultural safety definition and principles

All health and social work professionals must play a role in reducing health inequities for Indigenous Australian Peoples. When professionals lack in understanding and acceptance of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples knowledges and ways of knowing, being and doing, their attitudes and actions perpetuate institutional racism, hence continuing poor health (Biles & Biles, 2020). 

Modules 1, 2, & 3 provided background knowledge for understanding the above statement.

In order to benefit Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples health and remove social inequities the social and cultural determinants of health, which includes institutional racism, must be addressed (Mohamed et al., 2024). The cultural safety framework requires organisations and institutions to provide all staff with training in working with peoples rooted in diverse cultures in a culturally competent and safe way.

Whilst not a linear process, cultural safety is achieved when the recipients of care and services consider care to be meeting their cultural needs. This requires all healthcare workers to reflect on the impact of their own interactions and cultural identity. Although cultural safety is an Indigenous framework, it does require the workers from the dominant (colonising) culture to engage in critical self-reflection and decolonising practices, both at an individual and organisational level.

The Nursing Council of New Zealand (1992) define cultural safety as the effective nursing practice when working with a person or a family from cultures that differ to 'the mainstream' or dominant culture. Effective nursing practices are determined by the person or family working with a nurse practitioner, who should undertake a process of reflection on his/her own cultural identity to recognise how their own culture impacts their professional practice.

The five principles of cultural safety summarise what is required of you as a professional to enable you to ‘decolonise’ your practice (see Module 5 topic) and provide acceptable, accessible and respectful care and support for Indigenous client's with whom you are working with. These principles were documented by two non-Indigenous nurse academics with decades of experience in working with and learning from Indigenous Peoples in a health and well-being context.
