## Origins and focus

The module traces cultural safety to Māori nurse Irihapeti Ramsden and the response to inequitable care in Aotearoa New Zealand. The focus is not mastering a checklist about another culture but examining power, racism and the effects of professional practice.

## Safety versus competence

Cultural awareness or knowledge may support care, but self-declared competence does not prove that care is safe. Examine whose judgement counts, whether people can exercise meaningful choice, and how institutions recognise or dismiss their experience.

## Critical self-reflection

Identify your assumptions, stereotypes, social position and professional authority. Recognise that organisational routines and apparently neutral standards can reproduce dominant cultural values and disadvantage.

## Apply the framework

For a service encounter, examine communication, consent, family participation as desired by the person, power in decisions, and the response to racism. Ask what must change in the organisation rather than asking the client alone to adapt.

## Revision prompts

Compare cultural safety and cultural competence using the course table. Explain how well-intentioned care can still be unsafe. Ground analysis in the prescribed reading and original scenario; do not treat this revision guide as an assessment answer.

## From terminology to practice

| Term/question | Revision focus |
|---|---|
| Awareness | Recognising differences and one’s own assumptions is a beginning, not proof of safe care |
| Competence | Knowledge and skills may help, but a practitioner’s self-rating does not settle the person’s experience |
| Cultural safety | Examine power, racism and whose judgement determines safety |
| Critical reflexivity | Connect personal positioning with organisational rules and professional authority |
| Observable practice | Ask about communication, choice, consent, desired family involvement and the service’s response when racism is reported |
