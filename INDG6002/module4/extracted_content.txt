===== Module Overview [_7400133_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400058_1?view=content&state=view
What does the word cultural safety mean to you? In this module we will define the origins and meaning of cultural safety and provide a rationale for why this is the most effective and appropriate term and theoretical framework for working with Indigenous Australian Peoples.
The five core principles of cultural safety are described and discussed in detail in relation to healthcare, and how these principles translate to practicing social work. The rationale for cultural safety as the most effective and appropriate framework for working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples is discussed.
You are urged not to be distracted by the multitude of (potentially) confusing terms used to refer to working with culturally diverse peoples. To exemplify this, cultural safety and cultural competence are compared and contrasted.
Your main reading for this week provides a detailed history and overview of cultural safety versus cultural competence. A real-life scenario is presented to expand your understanding on cultural safety in a pragmatic way, and as a guide to your final Assessment task 2.
Module purpose
By the end of this module you will be able to:
define and discuss the origins of cultural safety and what sets it apart from the plethora of alternative terms used to describe working with diverse cultures in a health and social support context
reflect on your own culture/worldview and identify how this influences your ability to work with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples
understand the core principles of cultural safety and how these relate to your professional practices while working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.

===== Introduction [_7400134_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400059_1?view=content&state=view
Cultural encounters with clients in healthcare settings can be challenging for healthcare professionals who are often effected by fear and apprehension.
For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples the consequence of past experiences of institutionalised, internalised, and interpersonal racism within healthcare settings and other government or formal organisations have led to avoidance of mainstream services.
There remains a clear lack of knowledge and understanding of antecedents and the ongoing impacts that are the bases for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples fear and avoidance of accessing services. Originating from colonisation and exacerbated by institutional racism (see Module 2), Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander clients continue to be reluctant to engage with all Australian government organisations (Durey, Thompson & Wood, 2012; Rix, Moran, Kapeen & Wilson, 2016).
This module will provide you with a cultural safety framework for working in equitable, culturally safe, and respectful ways in service settings when working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. Today, disconnect between the cultural values and worldviews of Indigenous Peoples and non-indigenous Australians is still apparent. Disconnection extends beyond personal relationships into 'mainstream' organisations providing services that reflect institutionalised culture.

===== Origins of Cultural Safety [_7400135_1] =====
Source: https://learn.scu.edu.au/ultra/courses/_156096_1/document/_7400060_1?view=content&state=view
The epistemological origins of cultural safety stem from the ongoing negative impacts of colonisation on Māori people in Aotearoa/NZ in 1989. Cultural safety emerged from the work of Māori nurse Irihapeti Ramsden, in response to her concerns at witnessing the discriminatory and inequitable treatment of her people within the Aotearoa/NZ health system.
Cultural safety is now recognised globally as an effective practice framework for working with all Indigenous Peoples. It is increasingly being cited as most effective for working with all minority cultural groups (Lokugamage , Rix, Fleming, Khetan, Meredith & Hastie, 2021).
Cultural safety is promoted by antiracism. It is an agile practice-based process of change for eliminating individual, institutional and systemic racism (Tascon & Gatwiri, 2020). As mentioned previously, racism and the lack of cultural safety drives Indigenous Peoples fear and avoidance of health and social services. The lack of trust between Indigenous Peoples and Western health and social services has a strong colonial history (Gatwiri, Rix & Rotumah, 2021).
"Many of our people, don’t feel safe in hospital. I’ve heard people say they would rather die than go to hospital where they are made sicker by encounters of racism. I hear Aboriginal people say “I don’t trust them (medical professionals) … they might want to hurt me deliberately”. This is because we have a history of genocide. This is our history. Our ancestors are genocide survivors. We need more First Nations workers and racially safe hospitals so that our people can feel safe to seek treatment"
— (Gatwiri, Rix and Rotumah, 2021 p. 6).
Cultural safety definition and principles
All health and social work professionals must play a role in reducing health inequities for Indigenous Australian Peoples. When professionals lack in understanding and acceptance of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples knowledges and ways of knowing, being and doing, their attitudes and actions perpetuate institutional racism, hence continuing poor health (Biles & Biles, 2020).
Modules 1, 2, & 3 provided background knowledge for understanding the above statement.
In order to benefit Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples health and remove social inequities the social and cultural determinants of health, which includes institutional racism, must be addressed (Mohamed et al., 2024). The cultural safety framework requires organisations and institutions to provide all staff with training in working with peoples rooted in diverse cultures in a culturally competent and safe way.
Whilst not a linear process, cultural safety is achieved when the recipients of care and services consider care to be meeting their cultural needs. This requires all healthcare workers to reflect on the impact of their own interactions and cultural identity. Although cultural safety is an Indigenous framework, it does require the workers from the dominant (colonising) culture to engage in critical self-reflection and decolonising practices, both at an individual and organisational level.
The Nursing Council of New Zealand (1992) define cultural safety as the effective nursing practice when working with a person or a family from cultures that differ to 'the mainstream' or dominant culture. Effective nursing practices are determined by the person or family working with a nurse practitioner, who should undertake a process of reflection on his/her own cultural identity to recognise how their own culture impacts their professional practice.
The five principles of cultural safety summarise what is required of you as a professional to enable you to ‘decolonise’ your practice (see Module 5 topic) and provide acceptable, accessible and respectful care and support for Indigenous client's with whom you are working with. These principles were documented by two non-Indigenous nurse academics with decades of experience in working with and learning from Indigenous Peoples in a health and well-being context.

The need for health practitioners to reflect on their practice is a critical aspect of culturally safety practice. Because most practitioners are members of the dominant cultural groups, reflection is critical on how this might impact on people who are members of minority ethnic groups.

Untitled Text

Reflect

Talk, ask, engage in dialogue with your client. Well this may seem to be stating the obvious, however, there are countless examples of encounters where Indigenous patients are spoken about, around and on behalf of, but often not talked to or with. A culturally safe approach will require true and respectful engagement with people to understand their unique needs, beliefs, understandings and preferred ways of doing things. When there is a perceived barrier to conversation, clients may remain unengaged and unempowered. Talking, asking and engaging with Indigenous clients is not always easy, given historical and contemporary negative experiences impacting Indigenous Peoples’ ability to engage with and trust non-Indigenous professionals.

Engage

Seek to minimise the power differentials between yourself and clients. Western health care has traditionally been hierarchical in nature, although this is slowly changing. Health professionals occupy positions of power over all their clients with their expert knowledge of what may have brought their patients under their care. Being members of the dominant culture further exacerbates the potential for power imbalances. Consider what may shift these power imbalances in your own practice setting. Think about the way clients are sometimes referred to as ‘non-compliant’, ‘absconders’ and ‘frequent flyers’. These kinds of labels position the professional as the one in power, with clients reduced to simple labels.

Power balancing

Undertake a process of decolonisation. This is a somewhat controversial aspect of the New Zealand model that came in for some criticism in the press. It is this element, however, that separates cultural safety from all other approaches, acknowledging the key role of a colonising history in contemporary health outcomes for Indigenous Peoples.

Decolonisation

Ensure you do not diminish, demean, or disempower others through your actions. Sometimes it is easier to identify culturally unsafe practice rather than it is to identify culturally safe approaches. Both, however, require a level of self-awareness and willingness to critique practice and systems. Actions can include subtleties of body language, and how you say things and what you say, as well as more overt behaviours.

“Stop deploying your white privilege on me!” Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander engagement with the Australian Association of Social Workers in Australian Social Work
By doing a search of literature on working with different cultures in healthcare, you will find there is a plethora of terms and definitions that can be both confusing and distracting. Many of you may have heard terms such as cultural competence, cultural awareness, cultural humility, cultural sensitivity, and on it goes. We won’t provide you with definitions and details for all these terms. Instead we will simply state that cultural safety is the only framework specifically developed by and for Indigenous Peoples.
The cultural safety framework requires non-Indigenous workers within the healthcare domain to reflect on their own privileges, worldviews, and power imbalances and also how they may decolonise their practices.
The table below compares and contrasts cultural safety with cultural competence, clearly demonstrating the differences. It shows how cultural safety provides a framework that can enable professionals to work with Indigenous clients, hence reducing institutional racism and improvment of service delivery:
Cultural safety
Cultural competence
Requires practitioners to use critical self-reflection on: their white privilege, biases, assumptions, stereotypes and power imbalances.
Recognises that sociocultural difference manifests, in part, as a power imbalance between culturally different peoples.
Recognises the dynamics of institutional racism: that cultural differences, while centrally important to many Indigenous peoples and different ethnic minorities, are not recognised as 'ordinary' by institutions, therefore, clients are often not properly provided for.
Cultural safety recognises that it is not only services that need to be culturally appropriate; if services are delivered inadquately, then the delivery method of those services can become a negative determinant of health outcomes.
Requires care to be determined by the recipient of care.
A broad concept with various definitions based on a number of frameworks.
Originally defined by Cross et al. in 1989 as: "A set of congruent behaviours attitudes and polices that come together in a system, agency, or among professionals to work effectively in cross cultural situations".
Limited through focusing on the mastery of knowledge, skills and attitudes by inferring it is a static outcome that can be checked off some list.
Relies on recognition of social and cultural influences and creating interventions that take these into account.
Cultural competence has potential to confuse professionals with complex jargon. Multiple terms are often used interchangeably: cultural awareness; cultural sensitivity; cultural security; cultural humility.

Activity: MyReading 4.3
Jennings et al., 2018. The power of talk and power in talk: a systematic review of Indigenous narratives of culturally safe healthcare communication
Talk has the power to build trust, safety, and engagement
Jennings et al. identify communication (“talk”) as central to Indigenous experiences of culturally safe healthcare. Indigenous narratives consistently describe good talk as fostering trust, strengthening relationships, and supporting engagement with services. Talk that is respectful, relational, and pitched at the client’s level enables Indigenous people to feel heard, valued, and safe within healthcare encounters.
Importantly, talk is not simply the transfer of information. It is a relational practice through which safety is either established or undermined. When healthcare workers listen, use accessible language, and engage respectfully, communication becomes a protective factor that improves access and outcomes.
Good talk is a critical ingredient in culturally safe care because it enables trust, strengthens engagement, and supports positive health experiences.

An Indigenous Elder from a rural community has been called to a hearing clinic to collect his new hearing aid.
