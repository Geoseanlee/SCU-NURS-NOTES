 INDG6002 HEALTH AND INDIGENOUS
 PEOPLES
 Summary
  Title                   Assessment 1
  Type                    Short response
  Due Date                Friday, 13th November, Week 3 11:59 pm AEST/AEDT (QLD 10:59 pm)
  Length                  1,000 words
  Weighting               40%
  Academic Integrity      Level 2. Purpose-Specific GenAI Use
                          GenAI may NOT be used EXCEPT Grammarly Premium AI.
  Submission              Word document submitted to Blackboard

  Unit Learning           This assessment task maps to the following ULOs:
  Outcomes                ULO1: Discuss key concepts of health and wellbeing as determined by
                          Aboriginal People.
                          ULO2: Identify key stages of the colonisation process and its relationship to
                          the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous People.

 Rationale
Understanding Australian Indigenous perspectives of health and wellbeing is essential for culturally safe
health practice. This assessment introduces students to the concept of self-determination, a key
principle recognised in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).

 Through this task, students will explore how Australian Indigenous understandings of health and
 wellbeing differ from Western biomedical approaches and consider how colonisation has affected
 Australian Indigenous Peoples ability to exercise self-determination. This assessment develops and
 extends foundational knowledge and critical thinking skills needed for health, social service, and
 community-based professionals working with Australian Indigenous Peoples.

 Task Description
    •   Read Article 3 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples
        (UNDRIP):
    •   "Indigenous peoples have the right to self-determination. By virtue of that
        right, they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their
        economic, social and cultural development."
    •   Write a 1,000-word short response explaining how this article relates to Australian Indigenous
        Peoples understandings of health and wellbeing.

 Task Instructions
Step 1: Read UNDRIP Article 3
Consider what the term self-determination means and why it is important for Australian Indigenous
Peoples.
Step 2: Review Unit Content
Use learning materials from:
    • Module 1: Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing.

    •   Module 2: Colonisation and its impacts on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

Step 3: Research
Locate additional scholarly literature that supports your discussion.
                           Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
      Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts
      before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are
      written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles,
      academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author
      credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly
      sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without
      undergoing peer review.

Step 4: Write Your Response
Your response should explain how Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, self-
determination, and community-accepted communication styles are interconnected, and how these
relate to the ongoing impacts of colonisation on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

Suggested Structure
Students may use the following structure:
Introduction (Approx. 100 words)
    • Introduce UNDRIP Article 3.

    •   Outline the main points of your response.

Health and Wellbeing (Approx. 300 words)
   • Examine the key concepts of health and wellbeing from Australian Indigenous perspectives.

    •   Reflect on why self-determination is important for Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

    •   Critically evaluate one cultural determinant which shapes Australian Indigenous health and
        wellbeing.

Community-accepted communication styles (Approx. 300 words)
Critically analyse the role of community-accepted communication styles in supporting Australian
Indigenous health and wellbeing.

Colonisation and Health (Approx. 200 words)
    • Critically reflect on the enduring effects of colonisation on Australian Indigenous health and
       wellbeing.

Conclusion (Approx. 100 words)
   • Summarise your key points. NO new information

 Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) Guidelines
 Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT, may not be used for this Assessment
 Task.

 In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally restricted to protect the
 integrity and authenticity of the learning process. Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived
 experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these
 knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to
 culturally responsive learning, something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-
 generated content.

 You must demonstrate that you have developed the required knowledge and skills
 independently, without the assistance of GenAI tools. Unauthorised use of GenAI may be
 considered a breach of academic integrity under the Student Academic and Non-Academic
 Misconduct Rules (Section 3).’

Approaching different assessment tasks - Short responses
Short responses have become a very common assessment type at SCU. They can be formatted
in different ways. Always check the assessment details and rubric to understand requirements.

It is important not to underestimate how much time these shorter assessments take to put
together. Add tasks to your weekly study schedule in the same way you would for any task.

Short response assessments do share common features and structure. Understanding these
common elements can make it easier to write your short response assessments, and help you
to improve grades.



Low-stakes
These lower-weighted assessments give you a chance to engage with unit content and get
feedback. They offer an opportunity to improve your overall grade for the unit.

Shorter word count
Use a concise style where every word counts.

PEEL paragraph structure
Strictly following the PEEL body paragraph structure to use a concise evidence-rich writing
style.

Narrow focus
Offer a narrow but deep discussion that covers just one or two examples or issues in a bit of
detail.

Direct informed response
Make sure that you use unit content to offer your direct answer.



Common short response assessment structure:

   1. First, state your informed response, or direct answer to the question. Use keywords
      from the question.

   2. The rest of the short response should only develop (evidence and explain) your
      informed answer.

   3. Conclude the response by summarising your direct answer and any implications you
      have discussed (no new information).

TIPS
Start early

You will still need to read, research, note-make, brainstorm, plan, draft and edit your
assessment. The actual writing and editing phases are likely to take around a third of the
overall time it takes to complete the task.

This means it is important to:

   •   start early (probably earlier than you think)

   •   allow enough time for reading/research, note-making, and planning phases

   •   add tasks to your weekly study schedule.

Short Response Guide                                                                         2
Use unit approaches
Always use unit approaches (concepts, theories, models, processes or guidelines) in your
short responses. Often, this means defining and applying unit concepts or theories. At higher
levels of study short response assessments often ask students to evaluate unit theory. Always
check assessment requirements.

Before you start working on the assessment, find definitions in unit materials. This step will
help you gain a more detailed grasp of unit ideas and theories so that you can show this
understanding to the marker.



Be strategic
It is easy to dismiss short response assessments as not that important because they have a low
weighting compared to other assessments. However, doing well on these tasks is an easy way
to improve your grade for the unit. Just as importantly, these shorter assessments can offer a
chance to get early feedback from your tutor, supporting your results in later tasks.



Use a coherent structure
Make it easy for your marker to understand your position, or response by structuring your
assessment coherently and using PEEL body paragraphs.

Structure short response assessments so that they:

   •   start by clearly stating your direct answer to the question (your position/main point)

   •   present reasons (supporting points) and reliable evidence that support and develop
       your response

   •   offer a logical reading path (think about the order of your discussion)

   •   conclude by summarising your response (or position), and implications (discussed in
       the assessment).

Use PEEL struture for all body paragraphs. The first sentence should state the key point of
the paragraph. Remaining sentences should elaborate and develop the same key point,
using reliable evidence.

A useful model for writing paragraphs is the PEEL method. P (point) represents the topic
sentence or key point. The E‘s signify explanation, elaboration, examples, and evidence. L (link)
denotes the concluding sentence that links to the thesis and may link to the next paragraph.

Coherent structure can help you to save time and words as well as improve grades.

Narrow, narrow, narrow your focus
Short responses are narrow but deep academic discussions.



Short Response Guide                                                                             3
Don't try to COVER EVERYTHING you know about a topic. While it can be tempting, this is not
your task in a short response, and you don't have enough words. Your job is to offer your
informed answer to the question within a limited word-count. It is very likely that you only offer
and evidence one or two reasons (supporting points). Longer response papers might offer
three or four supporting points.

As you brainstorm, plan and draft keep trying to narrow, narrow, narrow your focus. Keep
asking what can't be left out and still meet requirements. Doing this will help you to make
decisions about how to focus your short response.



Pretend your reader disagrees with you
Sometimes writing short responses can be difficult because we can't "see" our position, or
main point. This can happen during the reading and planning phases, when we are experiencing
information overload.

It can help to pretend that your reader disagrees with your answer/response. Make a short
list of reasons and evidence you could use to persuade them. This quick excercise can make
writing phase much quicker and easier. Always check your list against assessment
requirements to make sure that you stay on track.

Key steps for strong short response writing

1.      Start with a clear main point
Short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Use direct sentence starters such as: “Article 3
emphasises that…” “Self-determination refers to…”

2.      Explain the key concept briefly
Define or clarify the concept (e.g., self-determination, holistic health, cultural determinants) in your own words
before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms.

3.     Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian Indigenous Peoples’
understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background
information.

4.      Use scholarly or unit evidence
Short responses require academic grounding. Evidence may include:
   • unit concepts
   • cultural determinants
   • colonisation frameworks
   • peer-reviewed literature
   • Keep evidence brief and purposeful.

 5. Write concisely and coherently
Use short, direct sentences to improve clarity and reduce grammatical errors. Avoid long introductions,
unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

6.     Maintain respectful terminology
Use correct and consistent terms such as Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. Avoid deficit language
and ensure cultural safety.

7.     Conclude by linking back to the question
End with a sentence that reinforces your main point and signals relevance to the marker. Do not introduce new
information.

 8. Avoid common pitfalls - Watch for:
 • over-description
 • missing the main point
 • weak or unclear links to Article 3
 • lack of evidence
 • incorrect terminology
 • overly long or complex sentences
 •
9.     Use postgraduate verbs
Choose verbs that show analysis and interpretation: analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise.
Avoid vague verbs like talk about or discuss.

10.    Follow a simple structure (PEEL)
           Point → Explain → Evidence → Link
           This keeps the paragraph focused, analytical, and academically grounded.

Respectful Tone

Tone refers to the attitude, emotional quality, and level of respect communicated through your language
choices. It is not just what you say, but how you say it. When discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
peoples, cultures, knowledges, and communities, tone is critically important because it signals whether you
are approaching the topic with respect, cultural humility, and awareness of historical and ongoing
injustices. A culturally safe tone avoids assumptions, stereotypes, and deficit language; it centres strengths,
sovereignty, and lived experience; and it acknowledges that these knowledges are not simply “content” but
belong to real communities with deep histories and continuing cultural authority. Using an appropriate tone
helps build trust, demonstrates ethical engagement, and ensures that your writing honours the significance of
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives rather than speaking over, minimising, or misrepresenting
them.
Apostrophes

When using “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” in a possessive sense, do not add an
apostrophe. The term “Peoples” names multiple distinct cultural groups and is treated as a proper noun in
respectful academic and governmental usage. Adding an apostrophe can imply ownership or introduce
meanings that are culturally inappropriate.

Correct:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples mental health”



Not preferred:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People’s mental health”

The plural “Peoples” refers to several distinct groups, helping avoid homogenising diverse First Nations
communities. It is capitalised when referring to specific cultural groups, as in “Indigenous Peoples.”



When adding another phrase

“Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” is the correct term when you are talking about the many
distinct First Nations peoples collectively.

But when you add another word after it — like health, culture, knowledges, histories, or worldviews — you
remove “Peoples” and use the shorter form:

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories

Types of Academic Sources – Peer-Reviewed, Scholarly, & Authoritative

Peer-Reviewed Sources

   •   Academic works (e.g., journal articles) evaluated by experts before publication.

   •   The peer-review process ensures credibility, accuracy, and scholarly quality.

   •   Commonly found in academic journals indexed in databases such as Scopus, PubMed,
       IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library.

Scholarly Sources

   •   Written by experts or researchers for an academic audience.

   •   Includes peer-reviewed journal articles, academic books, conference papers, theses.

   •   Key features:

           o   Author credentials provided

           o   References and citations included

           o   Purpose is to inform, analyse, or report research—not entertain

Authoritative Sources

   •   Highly reliable sources produced by recognised, reputable organisations or experts.

   •   May not be peer-reviewed but are grounded in specialist knowledge, established
       methodology, or community-controlled governance.

   •   Examples include:

           o   National peak bodies

           o   Australian Indigenous community-controlled organisations

           o   Government statistical agencies

           o   Professional associations

   •   Used to support factual accuracy, policy relevance, and culturally grounded
       perspectives.



Key Difference (Overarching Statement)

All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, and many scholarly sources are authoritative.
However, not all scholarly or authoritative sources are peer-reviewed. Peer-reviewed
sources undergo formal academic evaluation; scholarly sources are written by experts for
academic purposes; authoritative sources are trusted because of the expertise, governance, or
institutional credibility behind them.

Reflective writing
What is reflective writing?
Reflective writing is a personal style of writing. It is expected when you are asked to reflect on and think critically
(analyse and judge in relation to the theory) about ideas and practices that relate to you personally (e.g. your teaching
practices). Its purpose is to help you to understand your course material better, to help you create links between your
past and present learning and clarify the relationship between theory and practice.
You can write your paragraphs to give your opinion (interpretation/analysis) first, then the supporting evidence, as in
Example 1. Or you can present the theory first to provide the basis for your subsequent opinion, as in Example 2. If you
do not link your opinion to the theory, then your opinion lacks credibility, as in Example 3.

Features of the language of reflective writing
    •    You can write in the first person when you are reflecting. That is, you can include yourself (I, me, we) in your
         writing when you are expressing personal experiences, observations or opinions.
    •    When you are referring to theory or other writers you should write in the third person. That is, refer to other
         writers by name and do not use I, or me.
    •    The actions (verbs) when you reflect are usually those of feeling and thinking e.g. feeling, felt, considered,
         experienced, wondered, remembered, discovered, learned.
    •    When you are reflecting, if your opinion is not all that strong, you can modify or soften it by using words like
         may, perhaps or might.
