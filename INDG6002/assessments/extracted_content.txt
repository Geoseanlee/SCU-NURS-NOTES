Unit Learning Outcomes (ULOS)
This assessment task maps to the following ULOs:
ULO1: Discuss key concepts of health and wellbeing as determined by Aboriginal People.
ULO2: Identify key stages of the colonisation process and its relationship to the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous People
Task overview
This assessment invites students to critically analyse the relationship between self-determination, as articulated in Article 3 of UNDRIP, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander understandings of health and wellbeing. Drawing on unit content and scholarly evidence, students will examine Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, evaluate the contribution of a cultural determinant, analyse the significance of community-accepted communication styles, and critically reflect on the enduring impacts of colonisation on Indigenous health outcomes
Rationale
Understanding Australian Indigenous perspectives of health and wellbeing is essential for culturally safe health practice. This assessment introduces students to the concept of self-determination, a key principle recognised in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).
Through this task, students will explore how Australian Indigenous understandings of health and wellbeing differ from Western biomedical approaches and consider how colonisation has affected Australian Indigenous Peoples ability to exercise self-determination. This assessment develops and extends foundational knowledge and critical thinking skills needed for health, social service, and community-based professionals working with Australian Indigenous Peoples.

Instructions
Step 1: Read UNDRIP Article 3
Consider what the term self-determination means and why it is important for Australian Indigenous Peoples.
Step 2: Review Unit Content
Use learning materials from:
Module 1: Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing.
Module 2: Colonisation and its impacts on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Step 3: Research
Locate additional scholarly literature that supports your discussion.
Definition of Scholarly and Peer-Reviewed sources
Peer-reviewed sources are academic publications that have been evaluated by experts before publication to ensure quality, accuracy, and credibility. Scholarly sources are written by researchers or experts for an academic audience and include journal articles, academic books, conference papers, and theses. They typically provide author credentials and references. All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, but not all scholarly sources are peer-reviewed. For example, an academic book may be scholarly without undergoing peer review.
Step 4: Write Your Response
Your response should explain how Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, self-determination, and community-accepted communication styles are interconnected, and how these relate to the ongoing impacts of colonisation on Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Referencing
You are to use the APA 7th style for citations and referencing. For additional guidance, refer to the SCU Library referencing guides.
GenAI use
In Gnibi College units, the use of GenAI in assessments is generally restricted to protect the integrity and authenticity of the learning process. Indigenous Knowledges are grounded in lived experience, cultural practices, and relational ways of knowing. Engaging meaningfully with these knowledges requires personal reflection, respectful interpretation, and a commitment to culturally responsive learning, something that cannot be authentically achieved through AI-generated content.
You must demonstrate that you have developed the required knowledge and skills independently, without the assistance of GenAI tools. Unauthorised use of GenAI may be considered a breach of academic integrity under the Student Academic and Non-Academic Misconduct Rules (Section 3).’
The Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tool, Grammarly Premium AI, is the only generative software allowed to be used, and only for the specific purpose outlined below. Any use beyond this scope is not permitted.
This assessment is classified as:
Level 2: AI use permitted for specific purposes. You can use AI for specific tasks as identified by the Unit Assessor.
✅ You may use GenAI to:
Formatting, spelling and grammar - Grammarly Premium AI is permitted for formatting, spelling and grammar. Grammarly is permitted for language improvements – no content or knowledge creation is permitted.
Paraphrasing permitted - Use of Grammarly Premium AI is permitted for paraphrasing. Pay careful attention to any language adjustments that may affect the clarity of your work. Paraphrasing using Grammarly Premium AI must be referenced.
❌ You may not use GenAI to:
Use of any other GenAI for content or knowledge creation is not within the permitted uses for this task.
Present GenAI-generated material as your own original ideas
Ask GenAI to write any part of the analysis, commentary, or conclusions about your chosen study.
Upload or provide text from the research article or for GenAI to rewrite, summarise, or interpret the article.

Formatting your assessment for APA 7th
General Formatting
The following guidelines have been set out by the American Psychological Association (n.d.a).

Accepted fonts and sizes
    •    Times New Roman 12
    •    Calibri 11
    •    Arial 11
    •    Lucinda Sans Unicode 10
    •    Georgia 11
    •    Computer Modern 10


References
Use a hanging indent – 0.5in. (or 1.27 cm; this is the automatic default in Microsoft Word). This means the first line will
align with the left margin and subsequent lines are indented. There should be no extra spacing between reference list
entries.

Paragraphs
Paragraphs should begin with a first line indent in which the text of the first line is indented 0.5in. (1.27cm) from the left.
The tab key can be used for this. Alternatively, in the ‘Home’ tab in Word, find ‘Paragraph' in the top ribbon menu, and
click on the arrow in the bottom right corner to expand the paragraph options. Then select ‘First-line’ under the
indentation drop-down menu (See images below). Note: First line indents are not used in an abstract.

Headers
The header should only consist of the page number, unless otherwise specified by your unit assessor. The page number
should be in the top right corner, starting on the title page. A running head is no longer required unless requested from
unit assessor.

Spacing
Your paper should be double spaced unless otherwise specified by your unit assessor. There should be no extra spacing
between paragraphs. This does not include the title page (see Figure 1), within tables and figures, footnotes or
equations.

Margin
Your paper should have a 1in. (2.54cm) margin on all sides.

Tables and Figures
Tables and figures are now formatted the same in APA 7th. The heading “Figure 1” or “Table 1” is now bold and placed
above the figure/table. Figures/tables require a title, written in italics, with title case, and on a second line. Finally,
figure/table captions are now “notes”. These are placed under the figure/table and begin with “Note.” in italics. For
example, see Table 1 and Figure 1. For more information see American Psychological Association (n.d.b)

Title Page
Title pages should be used unless your unit assessor has requested the SCU cover page. The title page should include
the title of the work, your name (as author), your affiliation (i.e. which Faculty you are in), the unit number, your unit
assessor or tutors name, the due date, and a page number. This should follow the formatting displayed in Figure 1 on
p.3.
Note: the paper title is 3-4 lines below the top. The title is in bold, has the major words capitalized, and has an extra
space before the author name. The text on the title page is centered, each element is on a separate line and double
spacing is used.

 Rationale
Understanding Australian Indigenous perspectives of health and wellbeing is essential for culturally safe
health practice. This assessment introduces students to the concept of self-determination, a key
principle recognised in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).

 Through this task, students will explore how Australian Indigenous understandings of health and
 wellbeing differ from Western biomedical approaches and consider how colonisation has affected
 Australian Indigenous Peoples ability to exercise self-determination. This assessment develops and
 extends foundational knowledge and critical thinking skills needed for health, social service, and
 community-based professionals working with Australian Indigenous Peoples.

 Task Description
    •   Read Article 3 of the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples
        (UNDRIP):
    •   "Indigenous peoples have the right to self-determination. By virtue of that
        right, they freely determine their political status and freely pursue their
        economic, social and cultural development."
    •   Write a 1,000-word short response explaining how this article relates to Australian Indigenous
        Peoples understandings of health and wellbeing.

Approaching different assessment tasks - Short responses
Short responses have become a very common assessment type at SCU. They can be formatted
in different ways. Always check the assessment details and rubric to understand requirements.

It is important not to underestimate how much time these shorter assessments take to put
together. Add tasks to your weekly study schedule in the same way you would for any task.

Short response assessments do share common features and structure. Understanding these
common elements can make it easier to write your short response assessments, and help you
to improve grades.



Low-stakes
These lower-weighted assessments give you a chance to engage with unit content and get
feedback. They offer an opportunity to improve your overall grade for the unit.

Shorter word count
Use a concise style where every word counts.

PEEL paragraph structure
Strictly following the PEEL body paragraph structure to use a concise evidence-rich writing
style.

Narrow focus
Offer a narrow but deep discussion that covers just one or two examples or issues in a bit of
detail.

Direct informed response
Make sure that you use unit content to offer your direct answer.



Common short response assessment structure:

   1. First, state your informed response, or direct answer to the question. Use keywords
      from the question.

   2. The rest of the short response should only develop (evidence and explain) your
      informed answer.

   3. Conclude the response by summarising your direct answer and any implications you
      have discussed (no new information).

TIPS
Start early

You will still need to read, research, note-make, brainstorm, plan, draft and edit your
assessment. The actual writing and editing phases are likely to take around a third of the
overall time it takes to complete the task.

This means it is important to:

   •   start early (probably earlier than you think)

   •   allow enough time for reading/research, note-making, and planning phases

   •   add tasks to your weekly study schedule.

Short Response Guide                                                                         2
Use unit approaches
Always use unit approaches (concepts, theories, models, processes or guidelines) in your
short responses. Often, this means defining and applying unit concepts or theories. At higher
levels of study short response assessments often ask students to evaluate unit theory. Always
check assessment requirements.

Before you start working on the assessment, find definitions in unit materials. This step will
help you gain a more detailed grasp of unit ideas and theories so that you can show this
understanding to the marker.



Be strategic
It is easy to dismiss short response assessments as not that important because they have a low
weighting compared to other assessments. However, doing well on these tasks is an easy way
to improve your grade for the unit. Just as importantly, these shorter assessments can offer a
chance to get early feedback from your tutor, supporting your results in later tasks.



Use a coherent structure
Make it easy for your marker to understand your position, or response by structuring your
assessment coherently and using PEEL body paragraphs.

Structure short response assessments so that they:

   •   start by clearly stating your direct answer to the question (your position/main point)

   •   present reasons (supporting points) and reliable evidence that support and develop
       your response

   •   offer a logical reading path (think about the order of your discussion)

   •   conclude by summarising your response (or position), and implications (discussed in
       the assessment).

Use PEEL struture for all body paragraphs. The first sentence should state the key point of
the paragraph. Remaining sentences should elaborate and develop the same key point,
using reliable evidence.

A useful model for writing paragraphs is the PEEL method. P (point) represents the topic
sentence or key point. The E‘s signify explanation, elaboration, examples, and evidence. L (link)
denotes the concluding sentence that links to the thesis and may link to the next paragraph.

Coherent structure can help you to save time and words as well as improve grades.

Narrow, narrow, narrow your focus
Short responses are narrow but deep academic discussions.



Short Response Guide                                                                             3
Don't try to COVER EVERYTHING you know about a topic. While it can be tempting, this is not
your task in a short response, and you don't have enough words. Your job is to offer your
informed answer to the question within a limited word-count. It is very likely that you only offer
and evidence one or two reasons (supporting points). Longer response papers might offer
three or four supporting points.

As you brainstorm, plan and draft keep trying to narrow, narrow, narrow your focus. Keep
asking what can't be left out and still meet requirements. Doing this will help you to make
decisions about how to focus your short response.



Pretend your reader disagrees with you
Sometimes writing short responses can be difficult because we can't "see" our position, or
main point. This can happen during the reading and planning phases, when we are experiencing
information overload.

It can help to pretend that your reader disagrees with your answer/response. Make a short
list of reasons and evidence you could use to persuade them. This quick excercise can make
writing phase much quicker and easier. Always check your list against assessment
requirements to make sure that you stay on track.

Key steps for strong short response writing

1.      Start with a clear main point
Short responses must begin with the answer, not background. Use direct sentence starters such as: “Article 3
emphasises that…” “Self-determination refers to…”

2.      Explain the key concept briefly
Define or clarify the concept (e.g., self-determination, holistic health, cultural determinants) in your own words
before applying it. This prevents vague or incorrect use of terms.

3.     Link the concept to UNDRIP Article 3
Show clearly how the concept relates to the rights described in Article 3 and to Australian Indigenous Peoples’
understandings of health and wellbeing. Interpret the connection—do not retell general background
information.

4.      Use scholarly or unit evidence
Short responses require academic grounding. Evidence may include:
   • unit concepts
   • cultural determinants
   • colonisation frameworks
   • peer-reviewed literature
   • Keep evidence brief and purposeful.

 5. Write concisely and coherently
Use short, direct sentences to improve clarity and reduce grammatical errors. Avoid long introductions,
unnecessary detail, or narrative writing.

6.     Maintain respectful terminology
Use correct and consistent terms such as Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. Avoid deficit language
and ensure cultural safety.

7.     Conclude by linking back to the question
End with a sentence that reinforces your main point and signals relevance to the marker. Do not introduce new
information.

 8. Avoid common pitfalls - Watch for:
 • over-description
 • missing the main point
 • weak or unclear links to Article 3
 • lack of evidence
 • incorrect terminology
 • overly long or complex sentences
 •
9.     Use postgraduate verbs
Choose verbs that show analysis and interpretation: analyse, interpret, examine, integrate, contextualise.
Avoid vague verbs like talk about or discuss.

10.    Follow a simple structure (PEEL)
           Point → Explain → Evidence → Link
           This keeps the paragraph focused, analytical, and academically grounded.

                                    Accepted Terminology Guide

When discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in INDG6002, there are several respectful terms
you may use. List of terms which are acceptable, you MUST choose one term and use it consistently throughout
your assessment. The only time it is considered appropriate to change – is in a direct quote.



Acceptable terms

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander P/peoples
   •   Indigenous P/peoples
   •   Indigenous Australian P/peoples
   •   Australian Indigenous P/peoples
   •   First Nations P/peoples



Capitalising

Each of these terms MUST be capitalised (as per ‘Gnibi Academic communication style’ / ‘Respectful
terminology and language’ in Welcome to Gnibi Module)



Peoples

The word ‘peoples’ is the only word students can choose to capitalise OR not. Also, it must be plural – ‘peoples’
with the ‘s’ AND it MUST be consistent.



Respectful Tone

Tone refers to the attitude, emotional quality, and level of respect communicated through your language
choices. It is not just what you say, but how you say it. When discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
peoples, cultures, knowledges, and communities, tone is critically important because it signals whether you
are approaching the topic with respect, cultural humility, and awareness of historical and ongoing
injustices. A culturally safe tone avoids assumptions, stereotypes, and deficit language; it centres strengths,
sovereignty, and lived experience; and it acknowledges that these knowledges are not simply “content” but
belong to real communities with deep histories and continuing cultural authority. Using an appropriate tone
helps build trust, demonstrates ethical engagement, and ensures that your writing honours the significance of
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives rather than speaking over, minimising, or misrepresenting
them.
Apostrophes

When using “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” in a possessive sense, do not add an
apostrophe. The term “Peoples” names multiple distinct cultural groups and is treated as a proper noun in
respectful academic and governmental usage. Adding an apostrophe can imply ownership or introduce
meanings that are culturally inappropriate.

Correct:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples mental health”



Not preferred:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People’s mental health”

The plural “Peoples” refers to several distinct groups, helping avoid homogenising diverse First Nations
communities. It is capitalised when referring to specific cultural groups, as in “Indigenous Peoples.”



When adding another phrase

“Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” is the correct term when you are talking about the many
distinct First Nations peoples collectively.

But when you add another word after it — like health, culture, knowledges, histories, or worldviews — you
remove “Peoples” and use the shorter form:

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories

Types of Academic Sources – Peer-Reviewed, Scholarly, & Authoritative

Peer-Reviewed Sources

   •   Academic works (e.g., journal articles) evaluated by experts before publication.

   •   The peer-review process ensures credibility, accuracy, and scholarly quality.

   •   Commonly found in academic journals indexed in databases such as Scopus, PubMed,
       IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library.

Scholarly Sources

   •   Written by experts or researchers for an academic audience.

   •   Includes peer-reviewed journal articles, academic books, conference papers, theses.

   •   Key features:

           o   Author credentials provided

           o   References and citations included

           o   Purpose is to inform, analyse, or report research—not entertain

Authoritative Sources

   •   Highly reliable sources produced by recognised, reputable organisations or experts.

   •   May not be peer-reviewed but are grounded in specialist knowledge, established
       methodology, or community-controlled governance.

   •   Examples include:

           o   National peak bodies

           o   Australian Indigenous community-controlled organisations

           o   Government statistical agencies

           o   Professional associations

   •   Used to support factual accuracy, policy relevance, and culturally grounded
       perspectives.



Key Difference (Overarching Statement)

All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, and many scholarly sources are authoritative.
However, not all scholarly or authoritative sources are peer-reviewed. Peer-reviewed
sources undergo formal academic evaluation; scholarly sources are written by experts for
academic purposes; authoritative sources are trusted because of the expertise, governance, or
institutional credibility behind them.

Reflective writing
What is reflective writing?
Reflective writing is a personal style of writing. It is expected when you are asked to reflect on and think critically
(analyse and judge in relation to the theory) about ideas and practices that relate to you personally (e.g. your teaching
practices). Its purpose is to help you to understand your course material better, to help you create links between your
past and present learning and clarify the relationship between theory and practice.
You can write your paragraphs to give your opinion (interpretation/analysis) first, then the supporting evidence, as in
Example 1. Or you can present the theory first to provide the basis for your subsequent opinion, as in Example 2. If you
do not link your opinion to the theory, then your opinion lacks credibility, as in Example 3.

Features of the language of reflective writing
    •    You can write in the first person when you are reflecting. That is, you can include yourself (I, me, we) in your
         writing when you are expressing personal experiences, observations or opinions.
    •    When you are referring to theory or other writers you should write in the third person. That is, refer to other
         writers by name and do not use I, or me.
    •    The actions (verbs) when you reflect are usually those of feeling and thinking e.g. feeling, felt, considered,
         experienced, wondered, remembered, discovered, learned.
    •    When you are reflecting, if your opinion is not all that strong, you can modify or soften it by using words like
         may, perhaps or might.

Unit Learning Outcomes (ULOS)
This assessment task maps to the following ULOs:
1. Discuss key concepts of health and wellbeing as determined by Aboriginal people
2. Identify key stages of the colonisation process and its relationship to the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous people
3. Analyse the relationship between socio-economic factors and the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous people
4. Explain how primary health care and/or health promotion enhances the wellbeing of Indigenous people and role of the health professional as an agent of social change
Task overview
In this assessment, you will write a 2,200-word critical review based on the scenario of Sarah, an Aboriginal woman accessing healthcare services. Drawing on concepts explored throughout the unit, you will critically analyse Australian Indigenous understandings of health and wellbeing, the impacts of colonisation, the influence of socioeconomic determinants of health, and the role of primary health care and health promotion in supporting positive health outcomes.
Using evidence from the scenario, scholarly literature, and unit materials, you will evaluate how culturally safe, strengths-based healthcare practices can enhance Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing. You will also provide evidence-based recommendations to support culturally safe care, self-determination, and improved health outcomes for Australian Indigenous peoples. The review should be presented in a clear, coherent, and respectful academic style and be supported by a minimum of 12 scholarly sources.
Rationale
This assessment provides an opportunity to apply concepts and theories explored throughout the unit to a realistic health care scenario. Students will critically examine how culturally safe, strengths-based, and socially responsive health care practices can support Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Using the scenario provided, students will analyse Australian Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, the ongoing impacts of colonisation, the influence of socioeconomic determinants of health, and the role of primary health care and health promotion in supporting positive health outcomes.
The assessment encourages students to move beyond deficit-focused approaches by recognising strengths, resilience, cultural connections, and self-determination as important foundations for Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Furthermore, students will also consider the role of health professionals as agents of social change who contribute to culturally safe and equitable health care.

Formatting your assessment for APA 7th
General Formatting
The following guidelines have been set out by the American Psychological Association (n.d.a).

Accepted fonts and sizes
    •    Times New Roman 12
    •    Calibri 11
    •    Arial 11
    •    Lucinda Sans Unicode 10
    •    Georgia 11
    •    Computer Modern 10


References
Use a hanging indent – 0.5in. (or 1.27 cm; this is the automatic default in Microsoft Word). This means the first line will
align with the left margin and subsequent lines are indented. There should be no extra spacing between reference list
entries.

Paragraphs
Paragraphs should begin with a first line indent in which the text of the first line is indented 0.5in. (1.27cm) from the left.
The tab key can be used for this. Alternatively, in the ‘Home’ tab in Word, find ‘Paragraph' in the top ribbon menu, and
click on the arrow in the bottom right corner to expand the paragraph options. Then select ‘First-line’ under the
indentation drop-down menu (See images below). Note: First line indents are not used in an abstract.

Headers
The header should only consist of the page number, unless otherwise specified by your unit assessor. The page number
should be in the top right corner, starting on the title page. A running head is no longer required unless requested from

Spacing
Your paper should be double spaced unless otherwise specified by your unit assessor. There should be no extra spacing
between paragraphs. This does not include the title page (see Figure 1), within tables and figures, footnotes or
equations.

Margin
Your paper should have a 1in. (2.54cm) margin on all sides.

Tables and Figures
Tables and figures are now formatted the same in APA 7th. The heading “Figure 1” or “Table 1” is now bold and placed
above the figure/table. Figures/tables require a title, written in italics, with title case, and on a second line. Finally,
figure/table captions are now “notes”. These are placed under the figure/table and begin with “Note.” in italics. For
example, see Table 1 and Figure 1. For more information see American Psychological Association (n.d.b)

Reflective writing
What is reflective writing?
Reflective writing is a personal style of writing. It is expected when you are asked to reflect on and think critically
(analyse and judge in relation to the theory) about ideas and practices that relate to you personally (e.g. your teaching
practices). Its purpose is to help you to understand your course material better, to help you create links between your
past and present learning and clarify the relationship between theory and practice.
You can write your paragraphs to give your opinion (interpretation/analysis) first, then the supporting evidence, as in
Example 1. Or you can present the theory first to provide the basis for your subsequent opinion, as in Example 2. If you
do not link your opinion to the theory, then your opinion lacks credibility, as in Example 3.

Features of the language of reflective writing
    •    You can write in the first person when you are reflecting. That is, you can include yourself (I, me, we) in your
         writing when you are expressing personal experiences, observations or opinions.
    •    When you are referring to theory or other writers you should write in the third person. That is, refer to other
         writers by name and do not use I, or me.
    •    The actions (verbs) when you reflect are usually those of feeling and thinking e.g. feeling, felt, considered,
         experienced, wondered, remembered, discovered, learned.
    •    When you are reflecting, if your opinion is not all that strong, you can modify or soften it by using words like
         may, perhaps or might.

 Rationale
This assessment provides an opportunity to apply concepts and theories explored throughout the unit
to a realistic health care scenario. Students will critically examine how culturally safe, strengths-based,
and socially responsive health care practices can support Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Using the scenario provided, students will analyse Australian Indigenous concepts of health and
wellbeing, the ongoing impacts of colonisation, the influence of socioeconomic determinants of health,
and the role of primary health care and health promotion in supporting positive health outcomes.
The assessment encourages students to move beyond deficit-focused approaches by recognising
strengths, resilience, cultural connections, and self-determination as important foundations for
Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.

Furthermore, students will also consider the role of health professionals as agents of social change who
contribute to culturally safe and equitable health care.

Assessment Task
Write a critical review of the scenario that examines how health professionals and health services can
support Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing through culturally safe, strengths-based
approaches. Drawing on evidence from the scenario, analyse how key concepts explored throughout
the unit can be applied to enhance Australian Indigenous health and wellbeing.
Support your analysis with relevant scholarly and authoritative evidence, and provide
recommendations for culturally safe practice based on the experiences and service improvements
described in the scenario.

 INDG6002 Assessment 2 Brief
Scenario: Sarah
Sarah is a 28-year-old Aboriginal woman, university student, mother, and community member. She is
studying while working part-time and caring for her children.

Sarah values her connections to family, community, culture, and Country. She describes these
connections as an important source of strength, identity, belonging, and wellbeing. When she is able to
spend time with family and participate in community and cultural activities, she feels more supported
and connected.

Recently, Sarah has experienced increased anxiety, stress, and difficulty sleeping. She decides to seek
support from a local community health clinic.

During her appointment, Sarah shares that she has previously experienced racism in healthcare
settings. She explains that some health professionals made assumptions about her abilities and failed
to listen to her experiences. These encounters reduced her trust in healthcare services and made it
more difficult to seek support when needed.

As Sarah describes these experiences, the clinician responds:

"I think people are often too quick to call things racism. Let's focus on what's happening today."

Sarah becomes uncomfortable and contributes little to the remainder of the consultation.

Following feedback from Australian Indigenous clients, local Australian Indigenous Health Workers, and
community members, the clinic decides to review its services. The clinic recognises that strengthening
culturally safe practice, meaningful relationships, and community-informed approaches may improve
the wellbeing of Australian Indigenous clients.

As part of this review, the clinic plans to expand its primary health care services and develop health
promotion initiatives that support Australian Indigenous Social and Emotional Wellbeing (SEWB).
Proposed activities include community yarning circles, family wellbeing programs, cultural connection
activities, and health education sessions co-designed with Australian Indigenous community members.

The clinic has asked health professionals to develop recommendations that support Australian
Indigenous SEWB, self-determination, culturally safe care, effective primary health care, and strengths-
based health promotion.

Writing a critical review
What is a critical review?
A critical review is a critical evaluation of a document (or book or chapter or article). It is not just a summary of the
contents. You are expected to read, make judgments about the document and justify these judgments by using the
criteria given to you by your lecturer or indicated in the theory.
If you have been given criteria to undertake the review in your assessment task (or in the marking criteria), use those
criteria to formulate questions.

How do I write a critical review?
Before you can critically review something, you need to understand what you are reviewing. Prepare yourself for reading
the document by:
    •    formulating some questions to answer (see suggestions below).
    •    researching some related literature so you can make comparisons between the assessment document and the
         relevant literature.
Then read the document carefully, questioning as you read. Make notes as you read but reserve judgment until you have
finished the article.

Questions to ask yourself
    •    What is the author’s purpose? Make a short statement in one or two sentences about the author’s purpose. If
         you cannot do this, it might mean that you have not read the article carefully enough or it might mean that the
         author’s purpose is not clear or well developed.
    •    How has the author developed his/her thesis and ideas? Are there paragraphs to support (or not support) the
         author’s purpose? Are there any unclear ideas?
    •    Are the ideas supported (or not supported) by other research? Is the evidence relevant, reliable and recent?
    •    Is there a particular focus/bias? If so, is this because of the author’s purpose, or because assumptions have
         been made, or because a particular perspective has been taken? Is this a limitation?
    •    What is your overall evaluation? That is, what is the document’s worth or value? Does the author achieve his/her
         purpose?
    •    What are the particular strengths and weaknesses of the document in the way the information is presented, in
         the way the argument has been structured and/or in the evidence used?
    •    How does the document contribute to knowledge in this field and to your understanding of this knowledge?

Writing the critique
    •    Construct an introduction that:
          o specifically identifies the document at the beginning of the review i.e. include the bibliographic reference
               details (author, date, title, publisher)
          o states your overall evaluation of the document (thesis) based on your critical analysis.
    •    Construct a body that:
          o justifies your evaluation
          o identifies and substantiates the strengths/weaknesses of various aspects of the document
          o identifies and explains the criteria used in your evaluation
          o states how the document has contributed to the related field of knowledge
          o states how the document has contributed to your understanding of the topic.
    •    Construct a conclusion that restates your overall judgement together with an overview of the reasons for that
         decision.
          o Construct your list of references. Ensure you list all references cited in the review.

Example paragraph from a critical review of an article:
 The findings by Renk et al. (2003) are consistent with those of other researchers but also differ on some points. For
 example, the finding by Renk et al. (2003) that mothers predominantly take responsibility for child-related tasks is
 consistent with a large body of related research (e.g., Baxter, 2002; Craig, 2003; McBride & Mills, 1993, as cited in
 Renk et al., 2003; Sanderson & Thompson, 2002; Yeung, 2001). In contrast, there is little support for the finding by
 Renk et al. (2003) that there is no significant difference in either direct interaction or accessibility by mothers and
 fathers. In fact, other research data indicates that mothers spend significantly more time than fathers interacting with
 and being accessible to their children (e.g., Baxter, 2002; Child Trends, 2002; Craig, 2003; Craig & Bittman, 2003; Silver,
 2000; Yeung, 2001). Despite these differences, the findings from these studies are consistent with Renk et al.’s (2003)
 findings that the age of the child impacts on the time parents spend with their children which decreases as the child
 gets older. Overall, there is also supportive evidence for the original proposition put forward by Renk et al. that non-
 traditional gender roles and attitudes positively affect the amount of time fathers spent with their children (Hoffman &
 Moon, 1999; Sanderson & Thompson, 2002) but not their findings that there is no differences between men and
 women in terms of their direct interaction with or access to children.


Take note of:
    •   How the writer has used other literature to make judgments about the content of Renk et al.’s article.
    •   How paragraph structure is used to:
         o make a judgment about the content of the article
         o give examples to support that judgment
         o draw a conclusion about the content.

                                    Accepted Terminology Guide

When discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in INDG6002, there are several respectful terms
you may use. List of terms which are acceptable, you MUST choose one term and use it consistently throughout
your assessment. The only time it is considered appropriate to change – is in a direct quote.



Acceptable terms

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander P/peoples
   •   Indigenous P/peoples
   •   Indigenous Australian P/peoples
   •   Australian Indigenous P/peoples
   •   First Nations P/peoples



Capitalising

Each of these terms MUST be capitalised (as per ‘Gnibi Academic communication style’ / ‘Respectful
terminology and language’ in Welcome to Gnibi Module)



Peoples

The word ‘peoples’ is the only word students can choose to capitalise OR not. Also, it must be plural – ‘peoples’
with the ‘s’ AND it MUST be consistent.



Respectful Tone

Tone refers to the attitude, emotional quality, and level of respect communicated through your language
choices. It is not just what you say, but how you say it. When discussing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
peoples, cultures, knowledges, and communities, tone is critically important because it signals whether you
are approaching the topic with respect, cultural humility, and awareness of historical and ongoing
injustices. A culturally safe tone avoids assumptions, stereotypes, and deficit language; it centres strengths,
sovereignty, and lived experience; and it acknowledges that these knowledges are not simply “content” but
belong to real communities with deep histories and continuing cultural authority. Using an appropriate tone
helps build trust, demonstrates ethical engagement, and ensures that your writing honours the significance of
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives rather than speaking over, minimising, or misrepresenting
them.
Apostrophes

When using “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” in a possessive sense, do not add an
apostrophe. The term “Peoples” names multiple distinct cultural groups and is treated as a proper noun in
respectful academic and governmental usage. Adding an apostrophe can imply ownership or introduce
meanings that are culturally inappropriate.

Correct:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples mental health”



Not preferred:

   •   “Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People’s mental health”

The plural “Peoples” refers to several distinct groups, helping avoid homogenising diverse First Nations
communities. It is capitalised when referring to specific cultural groups, as in “Indigenous Peoples.”



When adding another phrase

“Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples” is the correct term when you are talking about the many
distinct First Nations peoples collectively.

But when you add another word after it — like health, culture, knowledges, histories, or worldviews — you
remove “Peoples” and use the shorter form:

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges

   •   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander histories

Types of Academic Sources – Peer-Reviewed, Scholarly, & Authoritative

Peer-Reviewed Sources

   •   Academic works (e.g., journal articles) evaluated by experts before publication.

   •   The peer-review process ensures credibility, accuracy, and scholarly quality.

   •   Commonly found in academic journals indexed in databases such as Scopus, PubMed,
       IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library.

Scholarly Sources

   •   Written by experts or researchers for an academic audience.

   •   Includes peer-reviewed journal articles, academic books, conference papers, theses.

   •   Key features:

           o   Author credentials provided

           o   References and citations included

           o   Purpose is to inform, analyse, or report research—not entertain

Authoritative Sources

   •   Highly reliable sources produced by recognised, reputable organisations or experts.

   •   May not be peer-reviewed but are grounded in specialist knowledge, established
       methodology, or community-controlled governance.

   •   Examples include:

           o   National peak bodies

           o   Australian Indigenous community-controlled organisations

           o   Government statistical agencies

           o   Professional associations

   •   Used to support factual accuracy, policy relevance, and culturally grounded
       perspectives.



Key Difference (Overarching Statement)

All peer-reviewed sources are scholarly, and many scholarly sources are authoritative.
However, not all scholarly or authoritative sources are peer-reviewed. Peer-reviewed
sources undergo formal academic evaluation; scholarly sources are written by experts for
academic purposes; authoritative sources are trusted because of the expertise, governance, or
institutional credibility behind them.
