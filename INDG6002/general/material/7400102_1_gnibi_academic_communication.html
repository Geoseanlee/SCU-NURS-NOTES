Gnibi Academic communication style

It is a requirement of studying with Gnibi College of Indigenous Australian Peoples and of Southern Cross University that all our communication be safe, positive, supportive and collegial in its content and intent.

Collegiality is about contributing our personal and academic strengths toward creating a culturally safe teaching and learning environment. We feel that all of your questions about this Unit are good questions, please ask them freely because this is how we learn. As a learning community, our responses to each other’s enquiries and observations should always reflect our understanding that people are not problems so our focus is problems and not persons.

We are all different people and respecting differences is a core value of cultural competency and Indigenous pedagogy. An Indigenous approach to teaching aims to focus on individual views and strengths on learning together around a sometimes difficult subject area. We need to be patient and polite with each other to do this to best effect.

The following 3 criteria are a guide for communicating respectfully across differences. Does my communication:

  1. Express my collegial engagement? 
  2. Demonstrate respect for others right to difference?
  3. Apply culturally competent values?

If your communication does not fit these 3 criteria, then please think very carefully again about the wisdom of sending it. The discussion board provided is an academic learning and teaching tool. It is to be used for scholarly and collegial discussion that relates directly to the materials and subject matter of this unit.

First Nations Vocabulary – using culturally appropriate language and terminology

Published 27 July 2022

We have recently received advice that the Minister for Indigenous Australians, the Hon Linda Burney
MP, has a preference to use the term ‘First Nations peoples’ when referring to Aboriginal and Torres
Strait Islander peoples. In line with the 2022 Reconciliation Week theme of Be Brave and Make a
Change, here are some tips to start making the change.

Be consistent. The preference is to use ‘First Nations peoples’, ‘Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
people(s)’, or ‘Indigenous Australians’. Choose one and stay consistent, including in data tables and
graphs.

Use capital letters, it’s a noun – First Nations, Aboriginal Australian, Indigenous Australian, Torres
Strait Islander. When referencing to Elders, capitalise the ‘E’ as it is a title. Capital letters also
apply to Native Title, Traditional Owners, Custodians and Culture.

Avoid acronyms or shortening words. Always use the full words. Acronyms or shortened versions are
considered offensive. The only time you can use an acronym is if it’s a part of a recognised
organisation name such as AIATSIS.

Use traditional place names where possible. If you are not sure of the traditional place name (for
example ‘Palawa’ or 'Gadigal’), visit the AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia - external site.

Terminology can change over time and, where possible, it is best practice to find out what the
preferred term is from the respective Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander group or individual you
are referring to. For further guidance, please see the Australian Government Style Manual - external
site.

Elder

The traditional meaning of an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Elder is someone who has gained
recognition within their community as a custodian of knowledge and lore, and who has permission
to disclose cultural knowledge and beliefs. Recognised Elders are highly respected people within
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities.

In some instances, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people above a certain age will be referred
to as ‘Elders’. However, it is important to understand that in traditional Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander cultures, age alone does not necessarily mean that one is a recognised Elder.

The use of Elder (uppercase) is generally acceptable, but it is important to be aware of the
differences in meaning outlined above.

When negotiating with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, ensure that recognised
Elders are involved. This may occur directly, or through other Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander
people negotiating on the Elders’ behalf. Although negotiation with recognised Elders is important, it
should not replace negotiation with local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander organisations, such as
the local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Community Controlled Health Organisation.

It can be offensive when literature refers to Aboriginal peoples or communities with a lower case ‘a’. Always capitalise the ‘A’ in Aboriginal peoples, as too with the ‘I’ in Indigenous Australian peoples. Through capitalising we alter these adjectives to pronouns, signifying that we are members of a particular and known group/s. By adding the ‘s’ to ‘people’ you are acknowledging the diversity that continues to exist amongst Indigenous communities. It’s important to note that the use of ‘Aboriginals’ is not accepted and is also not grammatically correct. Adopting appropriate language in your academic writing is respectful and will help to create an inclusive and accepted academic standard.

At Gnibi our written standard is to accept these terms of reference for academic writing:

Terms of reference

Aboriginal peoples is a collective term which recognises the diversity that exists across the many and varied nations represented in the Australian landscape.

Aboriginal peoples

Indigenous peoples is a collective name used for the original peoples of mainland Australia, its islands and the Torres Strait.

Indigenous Australian peoples

Torres Strait Islander peoples is a collective term which recognises the diversity that exists across the five major islands, inlets and smaller islands of the land and sea of the Torres Strait.

Torres Strait Islander peoples

First Peoples’ and ‘First Australians’ are collective names for the original people of Australia and their descendants, and are used to emphasise that Indigenous peoples lived on this continent prior to European invasion and subsequent colonisation.
These are by no means the only terms of reference, however for academic writing the above terms are most suitable.
At Gnibi our standard accepts that many other terms of reference may also be acceptable in some or all of our communities, and in communication and expressions in the broader arena. Included below are some examples:
Mob: This is a term that is increasingly being used by Aboriginal communities as a generic term to help locate Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander individuals within the larger extended family and community network.
Clan: This is a local descent group, larger than a family but based on family links through a common ancestry. A ‘clan’ is a subset of a nation.
Nation: This refers to a culturally distinct group of people associated with a particular, culturally defined area of land or country. Each nation has boundaries that cannot be changed and which existed before European invasion. Language and cultural ways are tied to that nation and its country.
Tribe: This has negative connotations for many Indigenous peoples. Definitions in dictionaries are inappropriate. The more appropriate words to use are ‘nation’ or ‘clan’ or ‘family groups’ –   but don’t forget to ask Indigenous Australians from the particular group you are writing about what they prefer.

First Peoples/First Australians

This is knowing where each person is from, where each person belongs, thus linking Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to who they are.
