# INDG6002 Module 0 — Unit Orientation

## Overview

Module 0 is the threshold of INDG6002 rather than a content module. It settles the questions a student needs answered before teaching begins: what the unit is for, who teaches it, which university requirements run alongside it, where to get access and technology help, how communication is meant to work, and what to read before week 1. The material sits in two parts of the course shell — the unit orientation folder and the Welcome to Gnibi folder. [S1][S7]

The subject matter makes this orientation unusually load-bearing. The unit raises understanding of Indigenous health issues: Indigenous concepts and frameworks for health, the negative impact of colonisation on health and wellbeing, cultural safety, and reflection on how to decolonise professional practice. It does this through discussion, critical analysis and service planning exercises. Because the content is relational and historically charged, the rules about communication, naming and listening are part of the curriculum rather than paperwork around it. [S1][S8]

## What you should be able to do

By the end of this module you should be able to:

- Explain what INDG6002 covers and restate its four unit learning outcomes in your own words. [S1]
- Describe the place of Gnibi College of Indigenous Australian Peoples within your studies and explain why its Elders Principles shape the unit. [S7][S9]
- Summarise the nine Gnibi Elders Principles and apply them to how you read, speak and write about Indigenous knowledge. [S9]
- Identify each member of the teaching team, their role, and the correct contact route and availability. [S2]
- Complete the two mandatory SCU modules and state what happens if the Academic Integrity Module is not finished. [S3]
- Locate accessibility support and technology support, and distinguish which service handles which problem. [S4]
- Apply the three Gnibi communication criteria before posting to a discussion board or emailing staff. [S8]
- Choose respectful terminology for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, and explain why the choice matters. [V2][S8]
- Prepare for week 1 by working through the Gnibi welcome material in the order the unit sets out. [S6][S7]
- Use both the informal and the formal feedback channels available during the teaching period. [S5]

## 1. What INDG6002 asks of you

The unit is built around four linked strands. The first is Indigenous concepts and frameworks for health. The second is the effect of colonisation on health and wellbeing. The third is cultural safety. The fourth is reflection on decolonising professional practice. Best-practice Indigenous health care strategies run through all four, and they are approached through discussion, critical analysis and service planning rather than through lecture material alone. [S1]

The four unit learning outcomes (ULOs) make that structure explicit. [S1]

| ULO | Statement | Strand |
|---|---|---|
| ULO 1 | Synthesise key concepts of health and wellbeing as determined by Aboriginal People | Concepts and frameworks |
| ULO 2 | Critically analyse key stages of the colonisation process and its relationship to the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous people | History and health status |
| ULO 3 | Critically analyse the relationship between socio-economic factors and the contemporary health status of Australian Indigenous people | Structural determinants |
| ULO 4 | Reflect on and synthesise how primary health care and/or health promotion enhances the wellbeing of Indigenous people, and the role of the health professional as an agent of social change | Practice and professional role |

The sequence is deliberate. ULO 1 asks you to take Aboriginal-defined concepts of health seriously before ULO 2 introduces the historical process that disrupted them. ULO 3 then adds the socio-economic layer that connects history to present-day health status. ULO 4 turns outward: primary health care, health promotion, and the health professional as an agent of social change. The first three outcomes are analytical; the fourth is reflexive and professional, which is why the unit cannot be passed by memorising content alone. [S1]

The teaching methods follow from that. Discussion and critical analysis mean you are expected to say something and to have it examined. Service planning exercises mean you are expected to translate ideas into a proposed response. Neither works if you read passively, so the orientation material treats participation as the method rather than an optional extra. [S1]

## 2. Gnibi College, the welcome and the Elders Principles

INDG6002 sits within Gnibi College of Indigenous Australian Peoples at Southern Cross University. The Welcome to Gnibi section opens with a welcome video from the Gnibi Course Coordinator, Dr Kylie Day, and uses artwork by Sheldon Harrington. The section then points you to the College website and to three further bodies of material: Gnibi Academic Communication and respectful terminology and language, the Indigenous terminology guides, and the Gnibi Elders Principles. [S7]

The Elders Principles carry the most weight of those three. The Elders council that advises Gnibi College shared the guiding principles in 2014, and the University has used them to underpin its strategies since then. In 2021 the Elders met again, reaffirmed the principles, and reaffirmed their commitment to working with the University so that all students grow in understanding and knowledge, and so that Indigenous students learn and thrive as leaders walking in all worlds. The University states that it values the generosity of Elders in sharing their wisdom. [S9]

The nine principles are worth learning as a set, because together they describe what the unit assumes about knowledge, authority and learning. [S9]

1. Indigenous Knowledge is a relevant, ethical, intelligent, effective and useful way of knowing.
2. Indigenous peoples are active, able and worthy peoples.
3. The truth about histories, and the value of Indigenous ways, is determined by Indigenous peoples, who carry that knowledge.
4. Elders hold Aboriginal spirituality and culture close, and they choose what is open and shared and what remains closed; spirituality is the basis for life, Indigenous education and cultural competency, and it infuses everything.
5. Knowledge is relational, and the principles are the basis for SCU to sustain an informed and respectful relationship with Elders, whose collective input must continue through the Elders group.
6. Culture is sacred and living, and Indigenous peoples own it; it does not need a book, and sharing happens so that students learn how to listen and hear.
7. Identity rests on things inside and beneath the surface; an empowered, affirmed identity is the basis of positive, productive, healthy and non-destructive lives, and opportunities to engage and build knowledge strengthen community through learning.
8. Learning together every day must be informed and sustained by open, positive and ethical interaction.
9. Elders have presented these things all their lives without being heard — often listened to, but not heard or heeded — and they speak in the understanding that students will listen in the right way, hear them and learn.

Three consequences follow for a student. First, the unit does not present Indigenous knowledge as opinion to be weighed against a textbook; principle 1 treats it as a valid way of knowing and principle 3 places authority for interpreting histories with Indigenous peoples themselves. Second, not everything is available to you: principle 4 states that Elders decide what is shared and what stays closed, so a request for information can legitimately be declined. Third, the learning is relational — principle 5 and principle 8 make the quality of the relationship part of the content, which is why the communication rules in section 6 are not soft skills bolted on. [S9]

The Course Coordinator's welcome video makes the same point in teaching terms. It foregrounds cultural safety, inclusivity and respect in the classroom, describes working with Elders so that Indigenous knowledge principles are embedded in the curriculum, and asks students to participate actively, attend, and follow the guidance of lecturers and unit assessors. It also points to support services such as the library and counselling. [V1]

## 4. The two mandatory modules at SCU

Two SCU modules sit alongside the unit. They are not part of the INDG6002 content, but they are conditions of enrolment and they carry consequences. [S3]

| Module | What it covers | Consequence or support |
|---|---|---|
| Academic Integrity Module | Academic integrity, responsible use of sources, referencing, collaboration, and common mistakes | Must be completed in the first study period; takes 1–2 hours; non-completion can withhold grades, withdraw you from future enrolled units, or block further enrolment |
| Respect Matters | Respectful relationships, consent, and contributing to a safer, more inclusive SCU community | Replaces the previous Respect at SCU module; enquiries to Student Support Services on 1800 72 4357 (Press 3) or studentsafety@scu.edu.au |

Academic integrity is described as a core expectation for every student. Whenever you submit work you are required to follow the Student Academic and Non-Academic Misconduct Rules, which set out the standards for honest scholarship and the consequences of breaching them. You are responsible for understanding those expectations and applying them in every assessment across your studies. The stated penalties range from failing an assessment, to failing the unit, to more significant University sanctions. [S3]

The Academic Integrity Module is the training that makes those rules concrete. Students starting at SCU must complete it during their first study period. Its content covers what academic integrity means, the responsible use of sources, referencing, collaboration, and how to avoid common mistakes. Completing it usually takes one to two hours, and the advice is to finish it early in the session rather than at a deadline. [S3]

The penalties for not completing it are administrative rather than academic, and they escalate. Your grades may be withheld, you may be withdrawn from future enrolled units, and you may be unable to enrol in further study. Those penalties stay in effect until the module is successfully completed, so delay does not simply postpone the task; it can interrupt your enrolment. [S3]

Respect Matters is the newer of the two. SCU describes it as part of a commitment to a community where everyone feels safe, respected and supported to learn, live and connect. The module was introduced in line with the National Higher Education Code to Prevent and Respond to Gender-Based Violence and invites reflection on respectful relationships, consent, and what each person can contribute to a safer and more inclusive community. It replaces the previous Respect at SCU module rather than being added alongside it. [S3]

The support route for Respect Matters is Student Support Services on 1800 72 4357, pressing 3, or the studentsafety@scu.edu.au address. The Academic Integrity route is different: speak with your Unit Assessor or use the Academic Integrity website. Keeping the two routes apart saves time when a question is urgent. [S3]

## 5. Accessibility and technology support

Two separate services cover two separate problems. Students who need accessibility services or support are directed to Inclusion Support. Students who have trouble with SCU technology systems — student email or MySCU, which runs on Blackboard — are directed to SCU Technology Services. [S4]

The distinction is practical. An accessibility need is about being able to participate on equal terms, and it is best raised before a teaching period becomes busy. A technology problem is about access to the platform itself, and the earlier it is fixed the less work it costs you. The orientation page gives no contact names, phone numbers, response times or request procedures for either service, so the links in the unit shell are the route to use. [S4]

## 6. How communication is expected to work

Gnibi states that all communication within the College and the University must be safe, positive, supportive and collegial in both content and intent. Collegiality is defined as contributing your personal and academic strengths towards a culturally safe teaching and learning environment. The unit takes the position that every question about the unit is a good question, and that students should ask freely because asking is how the learning community learns. [S8]

The accompanying rule is about targets. Responses to each other's enquiries and observations should reflect the understanding that people are not problems, so the focus belongs on the problem rather than the person. The material also names difference as a core value of cultural competency and Indigenous pedagogy, and describes an Indigenous approach to teaching as one that focuses on individual views and strengths while learning together around a sometimes difficult subject area. Patience and politeness are presented as the conditions that make that possible. [S8]

Three questions act as a test before anything is sent. Does my communication express my collegial engagement? Does it demonstrate respect for others' right to difference? Does it apply culturally competent values? If the answer to all three is not yes, the instruction is to think again about whether to send it. [S8]

The discussion board has a defined purpose. It is an academic learning and teaching tool, to be used for scholarly and collegial discussion that relates directly to the materials and subject matter of the unit. That rules out using it as a general social channel, and it sets the standard for tone: a post can be critical of an idea while still meeting the three criteria. [S8]

The reason for this emphasis is visible in the content. Colonisation, cultural safety and decolonising practice are subjects on which students arrive with different histories, different families and different stakes. A discussion board flattens tone and removes the face that softens a hard sentence, so the three criteria function as a substitute for the cues that are missing in writing. [S8]

## 7. Naming people correctly

The terminology material carries a specific argument: how you name people is part of how you respect them, and there is no single label that every Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander person prefers. The presenter, who introduces herself as a Yatta Yatta woman, a PhD candidate and a public health practitioner, begins by saying that different groups around the country prefer different terms, so the video cannot prescribe one answer for everybody. Her first instruction is to ask people how they would like to be referred to. [V2]

She illustrates why with a name analogy. If someone's name is Samantha and you decide to call her Jane because Jane is shorter, the person would object; the same logic applies to group names, which is why people react strongly to being renamed by others. The point is not sensitivity for its own sake but the ordinary courtesy of using the name a person has chosen. [V2]

Four rules follow from the video. First, one well-known abbreviation for Aboriginal people is offensive and must not be used, and the excuse that it is merely an abbreviation does not make it acceptable. Second, the word Indigenous is acceptable to some people and not to others, but when it is used it must be capitalised, because it is a proper noun referring to peoples with distinct cultures. Third, where you know the specific Country or nation a person belongs to, use that name. Fourth, Aboriginal on its own refers to mainland Aboriginal people and does not include Torres Strait Islanders, so when referring to all First Nations peoples of Australia the full phrase is Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. [V2]

A fifth rule concerns acronyms. The presenter states that the common acronym formed from those words is not appropriate and that its shortness is not a justification, and she asks people writing within character limits to find another way. Torres Strait Islanders are described as a distinct culture with their own title and their own preference, which is the reason the full phrase is used rather than a contraction. [V2]

The unit supplies two reference documents for this: a terminology guide for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and a First Nations vocabulary document. The terminology video is flagged as presenting an Indigenous perspective grounded in lived experience, and students are warned that some viewers may find the message confronting. The accompanying instruction is to notice how your own background and worldview shape the way you hear it, and to listen to Indigenous voices on their own terms. [S8]

| Situation | Use | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| You know the person's Country or nation | That name | It is the most specific and the one the person carries [V2] |
| You are referring to all First Nations peoples of Australia | Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples | Aboriginal alone excludes Torres Strait Islanders, a distinct culture [V2] |
| You are writing the word Indigenous | Indigenous, capitalised | It is a proper noun [V2] |
| You are unsure | Ask the person | Preferences differ between groups and individuals [V2] |
| You are tempted by a short form | Write it out | The common abbreviation and acronym are described as offensive or inappropriate [V2] |

## 8. Feedback: two channels, two purposes

The unit offers two ways to give feedback, and they are meant for different things. Informal feedback covers quick questions, suggestions or immediate concerns, and is given directly to the Unit Assessor or a tutor by email or during workshops and tutorials. Official unit surveys open towards the end of the teaching period and collect confidential formal feedback that the University says directly shapes the unit's future. [S5]

The two channels differ in timing and in audience. Informal feedback is fast and local: it can change how a question is answered this week. The official survey is slow and institutional: it is confidential, it is aggregated, and its effect is on the next delivery of the unit rather than on the current one. A complaint that needs action now belongs in the first channel; a judgement about how the unit was designed belongs in the second. [S5]

The page also carries a "you spoke, we listened" section, which states that student feedback drives continuous improvement and sets up a two-column table of what students said and what changed as a result. In the version supplied to students, both rows of that table are still unfilled, so the principle is stated but the worked examples are missing. [S5]

## 10. How the unit expects you to engage

The orientation material describes an active student. Attendance and participation are requested explicitly in the Course Coordinator's welcome, along with following the guidance of lecturers and unit assessors. That is a low bar for a unit whose assessed outcomes include critical analysis and reflection, which cannot be demonstrated by attendance alone. [V1][S1]

Asking questions is framed as a normal part of learning rather than a sign of confusion. The academic communication page states that all questions about the unit are good questions and asks students to ask them freely. It also asks students to be patient and polite with each other, on the reasoning that an Indigenous approach to teaching works through individual views and strengths in a group that is dealing with difficult material. [S8]

Written contributions carry the same standard as spoken ones. The three criteria — collegial engagement, respect for others' right to difference, culturally competent values — apply to the discussion board, which is reserved for scholarly discussion connected to the unit's materials. The test is applied before sending, not after a reply has landed badly. [S8]

Reflection is the fourth expectation, and it is assessed. ULO 4 requires reflection on primary health care and health promotion and on the health professional as an agent of social change, and the terminology material asks students to notice how their own background shapes what they hear. Reflection here is not a diary entry; it is the capacity to see your own position in relation to the content. [S1][S8]

Feedback is the fifth. Using the informal channel when something is not working, and the official survey when the teaching period ends, is part of participating in the unit rather than a comment on it. The unit states that student input shapes future deliveries. [S5]

The final expectation is professional. KIRRA's profile describes the studies as designed to equip students to advocate for First Nations health equity within the health system and beyond. That framing places the student, not only the teacher, inside the unit's purpose. [S2]

## 11. Assessment, in brief

Assessment detail is covered by the separate Assessment Guide, and Module 0 does not restate it. Two points from the orientation material do apply. The four ULOs are the outcomes the assessment is built to test, and ULO 4 makes reflection an assessable capability rather than an optional habit. Academic integrity rules apply to all assessment across your studies, and a breach can cost an assessment, a unit, or more. [S1][S3]

**Source gaps:** Several details a student would want are absent from this material. The feedback page's "you spoke, we listened" rows are unfilled, so no example of a past change is given. No contact names, numbers or request procedures are provided for Inclusion Support or SCU Technology Services. KIRRA's email, campus and consultation hours are not listed. The Course Coordinator's video exists only as an automatically generated summary, and that summary garbles proper nouns, so its names cannot be relied on. Class times, the week-by-week schedule, assessment weighting and due dates are not in these documents and belong to the timetable and the Assessment Guide.

## Common confusions

**Orientation is not the same as administration.** The usual assumption is that a Module 0 folder holds links and deadlines. Here it holds the standpoint of the unit: who determines the truth about histories, what knowledge is shared and what stays closed, and how students are expected to speak to one another. Skipping it leaves the rest of the unit without its frame. [S9][S8]

**Gnibi College and SCU are not competing authorities.** Gnibi is a College of the University, and its communication requirement is stated as a requirement of studying with both. The Academic Integrity Rules and the College's communication standards apply at the same time, to the same piece of work. [S7][S8][S3]

**Indigenous, Aboriginal, and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are not interchangeable.** Indigenous is a broad term that must be capitalised; Aboriginal alone refers to mainland Aboriginal people; the full phrase is needed when Torres Strait Islanders are included, because they are a distinct culture with their own title. Where the specific Country or nation is known, that name is more precise than any of them. [V2]

**The Academic Integrity Module and academic integrity are different things.** The Rules apply to every assessment whether or not you have done the module. The module is a one-to-two-hour training with its own enrolment consequences, and those consequences persist until it is finished. [S3]

**Respect Matters is a replacement, not an addition.** Students who did Respect at SCU in an earlier period should not assume the old module still satisfies the requirement, because the new module replaces it. [S3]

**Inclusion Support and Technology Services solve different problems.** An accessibility need is not a login problem, and a login problem is not an accessibility need. Sending each to the right service is faster than trying both. [S4]

**Informal feedback and the official survey are not substitutes.** Informal feedback reaches a staff member during the teaching period and can change something now. The survey is confidential, arrives at the end, and affects future deliveries. [S5]

**Automatically generated summaries are not transcripts.** The Course Coordinator's video is supplied as an AI summary, and that summary renders the coordinator's name differently from the document that names her. Where a proper noun matters, check the document. [V1][S7]
