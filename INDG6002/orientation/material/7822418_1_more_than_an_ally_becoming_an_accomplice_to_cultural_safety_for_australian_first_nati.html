Introduction
Practicing cultural safety as health professionals requires an understanding of the social, cultural, histor-
ical and systemic influences upon lived experience and outcomes for First Nations peoples. These influ-
ences include cultural protocols, support structures, and multiple systemic barriers of disadvantage and
discrimination, both obvious and subtle. Accompliceship emerges through incorporating this understand-
ing into equitable, meaningful actions within everyday practice. Our focus is Australian First Nations
peoples, and we are aware there are some similarities for First Nations peoples in other parts of the
world. The term accomplice is used to denote intentional affirmative action (Bruno, 2023; Parter et al.,
2023). We use the term accompliceship instead of ally, as the latter can be claimed without practical
action being taken or change occurring (Finlay, 2020; Parter et al., 2023). Accomplices stand alongside
First Nations peoples in their struggle for social justice and a voice in policies that directly impact their
people (Finlay, 2020; Southam, 2021).
   The term equity needs qualifying here, as it can be used in a way that obscures lack of cultural safety
or indeed equitable outcomes. Given the dominance of western knowledge and cultures in healthcare,
have you ever asked yourself ‘equity from whose perspective’? Equity cannot exist without acknowledg-
ing and addressing power imbalances and discriminatory practices within health institutions and social
and cultural structures (Curtis et al., 2019). The definition of equity applied here includes the fact that
conditions of privilege or discrimination are not necessarily predictive or causative of specific health

outcomes but correlated through ongoing inequity within institutional structures and professional prac-
tice. Authentic equity includes voice, access, participation, and rights (Burton et al., 2024; United Nations,
2008). Accompliceship to First Nations people enacts authentic practical equity through everyday actions
and relationships (Southam, 2021).
    Processes and practices that enable racist and discriminatory actions remain embedded within health-
care, despite being unethical and outdated (Curtis et al., 2019). However, many institutions have policy
requirements and guidelines for respectful professional practice when working with vulnerable minority
groups. Cultural safety is a professional and legal requirement in all healthcare professions (Australian
Health Practitioner Regulation Agency & National Boards, 2020). Not a mere box ticking exercise, but as
enacted reality. Cultural safety is defined in Australia as ‘the ongoing critical reflection of health practi-
tioner knowledge, skills, attitudes, practising behaviours and power differentials in delivering safe, acces-
sible and responsive healthcare free of racism’ (Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency &
National Boards, 2020). Each of us working in healthcare bears responsibility to integrate equity and
fairness into our practice. Some take up this challenge through daily actions of respectful care and con-
sideration. Others may contribute to institutional policy, calling out discriminatory protocols and practices.
    We all make a difference. Accessing healthcare forces First Nations people to interact with western
institutions and everything we do has positive or negative effects. Health practitioners in every role are
either contributing to equity in service provision or are complicit in inequity and discrimination, via their
silence, inaction, or worse (Schmidt, 2019). We cannot sit on the fence imagining we are neutral and
somehow outside of the reality of treatment and outcomes for First Nations people.
    Social and practical justice are manifestations of fundamental human rights (United Nations, 2008).
Racism and discrimination towards any minority have no place in healthcare. Instead, ethics of dignity,
respect and fair treatment create conditions for improving health outcomes for those with the worst
statistics and outcomes in Australia. As health settings become increasingly diverse, culturally safe pro-
fessional skills are crucial. Not just in relationship with First Nations peoples, for all minorities. This
includes wellbeing in the workplace for both recipients of care and practitioners. Although practitioner
wellbeing is not the focus of this article, workplace discrimination in the form of undermining or bullying
through sexism, homophobia and racism are also manifestations of wider prejudice and cannot be
ignored. Discrimination experienced by staff who are not members of the dominant cultural group, as
well as patients, are common everyday experiences in Australia (Bargaille, 2020). This has increased from
50% to 59% since the ‘NO’ campaign’s misinformation and social media blitz contributed to Australians’
rejecting recognition of First Nations peoples in Australia’s constitution in 2023 (Diversity Council of
Australia, 2023). Being fully informed on rights to respect at work and communicating respectfully within
institutional cultures are crucial to the skillset required of an accomplice to First Nations people (Parter
et al., 2023).
    When we stand up as accomplices to equity, there can be pushback from people who are benefiting
from the whiteness myth of superiority. Excluding, supressing and discriminating against some people
and acting in a destructive way towards alternative worldviews and lifestyles is a recipe for social disas-
ter, as many of the challenges of the 21st century attest. Enabling all people to participate fully in their
communities is a necessary condition for societal health and coherence, aligning with the United Nations
Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (United Nations, 2008). We note that at this time in
history, there is a groundswell of conservativism seeking to maintain/re-enshrine discriminatory policy
and practices. Now more than ever, cultural safety for every person we encounter as a health profes-
sional is an act of humanitarian resolve; an act of deciding to hold respect and care. In these times we
all need to hold a space of unbiased professional practice.
    We offer four guiding principles that open a pathway to becoming an accomplice. These principles
suggest points for exploration, practical steps and crucially, self-reflection. We offer anecdotes and stories
from our own professional experiences. We provide a foundation for building practitioner confidence to
begin their journey towards accompliceship. Towards authentic care and consideration of the needs of
First Nations peoples and other minority groups. These principles are transferable to all people or groups
we engage (Came & Griffith, 2017).
    Being an effective accomplice is a lifetime journey of unlearning for white Australians (Rix et al., 2024),
as we challenge views that have been taken for granted for centuries. For example, the default position
                                                                                      Critical Public Health    3


within healthcare, that western and biomedical knowledges are the ‘gold standard,’ relegates non-western
cultures and knowledges to the status of lesser, irrelevant, or simply the ‘other.’ Providing ‘equity’ in
healthcare requires respectful inclusion of diverse ways of being and knowing. Not to replace western
knowledges and practices, rather to enhance and increase effective care. We are not claiming absolute
or fixed solutions. Rather, letting go of thinking any group has all the answers, opening ourselves to
lifelong learning, can contribute to improvements (Bruno, 2023). Our four principles are: (1) Reality is
complex, (2) Ready to learn, (3) Action and being brave, and (4) Reflection.
    Firstly, the foundations of whiteness and intersectionality, and ‘nothing about us without us,’ are laid.


Whiteness and intersectionality
The terms white and whiteness are not used to directly denote skin tone, but as a signifier for structures
of power and institutional systems of discrimination where they are enacted by individuals and groups
holding power. The terms came about because those who enact or benefit from whiteness have histor-
ically been of European/Caucasian descent. This situation is an ongoing structure of discrimination
according to Mackay et al.: ‘Whiteness and institutionalised racism began in Australia in 1788 and con-
tinues to this day in many aspects of our culture and society’ (Mackay et al., 2023, p. 6), operating within
a hierarchy of automatically assumed superiority, based on appearance and assumptions. People of many
cultural backgrounds may be living/working within, perpetuating, and benefiting from western/white
systems and institutions. The way we use the term whiteness draws on extensive research and literature
into the enormity and pervasiveness of this structural and cultural system of colonialism, dominance and
discrimination (Bonds & Inwood, 2016; Mackay et al., 2023; McIntosh, 1990; Mitchell et al., 2022). It also
relates to the notion of white fragility, that is the often-emotional, victim-oriented reactions of those
facing challenges to their participation in and benefiting from these structures of power and discrimina-
tion (Diangelo, 2018).


Nothing about us without us
The voices of First Nations people must be sought when policies and practice affect their lives. To not
do so is patronising and insulting. Healthcare institutions have not traditionally sought the opinions of
those accessing services, yet the time taken to engage in authentic communication/consultation have
positive effects on health outcomes. The phrase ‘Nothing about us without us’ originated from Charlton’s
(1998) book of the same name. While Charlton’s work relates to disability rights, the phrase’s meaning is
transferrable to all minority groups experiencing discrimination. Protocols can be created to streamline a
process of effective, authentic and respectful consultation. Like many aspects of accompliceship, this is
not about getting it perfect, feedback and adjustment will support refinement. Not doing anything is the
only way to get it wrong.
   The central importance of this ethical and respectful perspective is demonstrated by the development
of this article. Authors Dr Elizabeth Rix and Dr Shae L Brown developed the draft principles, with con-
sultation with First Nations scholar, health professional and co-author Dr Darlene Rotumah refining and
validating the authenticity of our principles, and the veracity of our approach to accompliceship. This
collaboration demonstrates the necessity of stepping up as non-Indigenous professionals, with openness,
willingness and critical reflexivity, to listen deeply, and learn from First Nations experts.


Guiding principles of First Nations accompliceship
Our four guiding principles offer pragmatic strategies for a lifelong journey of accompliceship to practical
equity. These principles demonstrate relationship, respect, responsibility and reciprocity (Tsosie et al., 2022).
These four ‘Rs’ are the fundamental attributes of accompliceship with First Nations people, with relevance to
any minority group experiencing discrimination (National Health & Medical Research Council, 2018). The
principles are interconnected and overlapping; however, it is useful to have guidance in the form of specif-
ically focused points. They form a lens, enabling us to bring the complexities of human life into focus, so
we act with broader vision (Brown, 2021). Thinking with a complexity perspective helps us to see wider

ranging factors that influence any situation. These can be factors that privilege or discriminate (Brown, 2021).
Thinking this way assists us to connect with the whole person placed within our care (Gatwiri et al., 2021).
    Reflecting on my role as an Aboriginal health professional and the experiences I’ve had over the years, I’m
    reminded that our people depend heavily on spaces that feel safe, welcoming, and culturally grounded. I’ve
    always felt the weight of that, knowing that Aboriginal health workers are often the ones breaking down barriers
    and creating that sense of safety, even though our workforce is small and stretched thin (Rotumah, 2023).

    I’ve seen moments where care is respectful and centred on relationships, and those moments remind me why
    this work matters. But I’ve also seen the opposite, times when the interaction isn’t safe, and our people don’t
    return. Those moments stay with me.

    What strikes me is that many of my colleagues genuinely want to understand our cultural ways, but the systems
    we work in don’t always allow for the relational approaches our communities rely on. These systems were never
    built with us in mind, and that’s where discrimination shows itself, not always through individuals, but through
    structures that make our people feel unwelcome or unsafe.

    That’s why I keep coming back to the importance of accomplices. We need non-Aboriginal health professionals
    who speak up, advocate, and challenge the parts of the system that cause harm. When they put people first and
    work in ways that centre trust, relationships, and cultural respect, they help create health services where our
    people can truly belong. (Darlene)
   We argue that these accompliceship principles should be part of professional training, informing cul-
tural safety education and used to inform policies and practice.
   Accompliceship is an ongoing enacted process (Smith et al., 2016). Health and fitness provide a useful
metaphor. We cannot get fitter and healthier by simply talking about exercise. Five minutes exercise once
a month will have minimal impact. Health and fitness require regular engagement, a resolve to being in
relationship with exercise as part of our daily lives. It means persisting even when it’s hard or uncom-
fortable. Health and fitness are affected by everything that has happened in our lives, in our parents’,
grandparents’ and great grandparents’ lives, through generationally inherited epigenetics (Grazioli et al.,
2017). Health is also impacted by the environment and circumstances we are in right now. We can use
this metaphor to think of being an accomplice as equity health and fitness.
   All that we do in healthcare is entangled with our attitudes, beliefs, and actions (Gatwiri et al., 2021).
We are building professional culture daily, with our actions, communications and relationships. What kind
of culture are we generating? We can never underestimate the impact any of us have. An attitude of
resolve and determination is needed. Humility in understanding and using our privilege as educated pro-
fessionals for action will assist us. It can be challenging. We all make mistakes, as we learn and grow new
knowledge. Only First Nations people can know if we are culturally safe accomplices (Cargo et al., 2019).


Principle 1: Reality is complex
Professional practice is shaped and influenced by many complex factors (Brown, 2021; 2023).
Acknowledging and considering this complexity supports us to refrain from projecting narrow assump-
tions. We must be open to exploring and learning, treating each person and situation as unique. Thus,
within this principle, we explore multiple contexts.
    One of the key attributes of life’s complexity is diversity. All healthy ecosystems, communities and
societies include the contributions of diversity. Working purposefully with cultural diversity can move us
beyond reactive unconscious biases and discriminatory practice (Mohamed et al., 2024). Contextual sen-
sitivity can assist us to soften the demands of conformity, opening our curiosity into authentic listening
and learning.


Our own context
Our own cultural context and educational experience can create a limited worldview. We are all different,
with unique lived experience. Australian education has presented a narrow knowledge focus, obscuring
histories and perspectives of non-dominant cultures and peoples (Shay & Wickes, 2017). Acknowledging
and exploring the many factors that shape and influence us is a first step to authentic accompliceship.
                                                                                       Critical Public Health      5


What has gone before is always impacting the present. We cannot ignore nor deny the personal or col-
lective legacy/s we live with, nor our unique lens on reality, but we can shift the way the past influences
the now and our worldview.
   Knowing even a little of our own cultural heritage, beyond the 236+ years of non-Indigenous pres-
ence in Australia, is an important first step. We are not suggesting complex genealogical research, but
having some idea of the story of our family gives a more complete view of generational connection to
Australia and Country. This step can begin our own truth telling journey. If our ancestors were involved
in actions directly damaging to First Nations peoples, we may need to develop the emotional strength
to acknowledge this. Such information can fuel our resolve towards the reciprocity of becoming an
accomplice, not out of a sense of being expected to make ‘reparations’ but out of resolve for a more
equitable life for all in the 21st century. With regard to any form of generational violence and trauma,
truth telling and peace making are served by affirmative action (Southam, 2021).
   This step along the path can provide us with a way to introduce ourselves to First Nations people.
Our job title, and or other status information are of little importance in building relationship. The follow-
ing anecdotes are our own experiences.

  I use self-disclosure and storytelling when building rapport with a new patient/person. Who my family are,
  where my Country is, exchanging humorous anecdotes. I try to convey with actions and words my accomplic-
  eship in the struggle for culturally respectful and safe treatment and care. I ask people if they are on their
  Country, where their mob are from. This exchange of more than clinical information builds relationship and
  trust. (Elizabeth)

  My introduction to First Nations people often begins with the story of how I inherited my respect for diverse
  peoples from my mother, and my grandmother. I explain that I am a mother of two daughters and a grand-
  mother to two boys. How long I have lived on Bundjalung Country, and a favourite place that sustains me is
  something I might share. I always add that I am open to learning. (Shae)


Context of peoples and place
Learning about the history of Peoples/Nations on the Country where we are working and/or living can
create respectful engagement with First Nations people. This may include learning the names of dif-
ferent language groups. This information is freely available through the internet and libraries. It is our
own responsibility to become informed, rather than the responsibility of First Nations people to bear
this cultural load for our benefit (Bruno, 2023). Local First Nations organisations may be readily
equipped to provide information, especially once they know that we are sincere in our resolve of
accompliceship. These organisations can be useful to connect with along the way.


Wider context
We can also become informed about the current state of affairs for Australian First Nations peoples gen-
erally (for example, Chamberlain et al., 2024). First Nations websites provide valuable information. This
research can educate about specific topics/issues within your area of practice. Be aware that
non-Indigenous sites generally provide information that expresses mainstream/western cultural and polit-
ical perspectives, omitting truths about the ongoing violence of colonisation and flawed government
policies such as the Stolen Generations (Aboriginal & Torres Strait Islander Healing Foundation, 2014;
McCallum et al., 2012).
   Exploring policies and practices for working with First Nations peoples is important if we are to be
informed. All of us are required to operate within policy frameworks; accessing these can assist us to
contribute to updating policy and best practice approaches. Such knowledge becomes vital if we expe-
rience pushback from colleagues or management. As we advocate for First Nations patients, we may also
need to advocate for ourselves, and being well informed will support us.
   Acknowledging complex reality relates to our own commitment to make a difference (Brown, 2021).
Being clear when articulating the rationale for our journey to becoming accomplices can assist us.
We are not suggesting long-prepared speeches/lectures, however a well-honed personal/professional

‘position’ statement provides a clear, succinct response to questions and challenges. It is also important to
regularly recall what motivated us to become accomplices. This is the why of the accompliceship journey.
    During my six years of doctoral research, I had my position statement up where I could always see it. It included
    the words ‘I am using my non-Indigenous privilege to contribute to the visibility of Indigenous knowledge in
    the academy.’ This position statement was enacted through always seeking to cite Indigenous scholars at every
    opportunity (Southam, 2021). I am only one person, but every contribution is valuable. (Shae).

   Accompliceship requires retaining the ‘big picture’ of culturally safe practice, staying informed of pol-
icy developments within our disciplines, while remaining responsive to the needs and preferences of
First Nations peoples.


Principle 2: Ready to listen and learn
This principle is founded on deep listening and being open to learning (Bruno, 2023). Unbalanced power
relationships usually mean the flow of information and dialogue is one way: from practitioner to patient.
Listening becomes authentic when we seek to understand the speaker, rather than merely formulating
our response/counter point/s. In western culture we are accustomed to listening to respond/debate
rather than to deeply understand. Engaging in deep listening and waiting, rather than jumping in as
soon as we can, supports relationship. Gaining understanding from First Nations peoples must always be
juxtaposed with institutional/policy requirements of our professional role. Integrating First Nations voices
within policy and practice requirements is central to being an accomplice (Kirkham, et al., 2019). Deep
listening is the basis of authentic, meaningful engagement and relationality with First Nations peoples
(Bruno, 2023).
    Talking with an Elder today, I was struck by his ability to truly listen to my words. Then I noticed how he did not
    speak until he had thought about what I had said, giving me a thoughtful response. (Elizabeth) (Rix et al., 2014)

   Without listening, how can we know what is required of us in any role. Resolving to listen and learn
is a first step. However, this humble position is not always easy. Being open-minded and allowing our
humanity to show develops our knowledge of discrimination within healthcare policy and practice.
Accompliceship is not knowledge of fixed outcomes but is non-linear and ongoing. There is ambiguity,
no absolute answers and contradiction, because these are qualities of the world we live in. Western
knowledge may encourage us to think we can accumulate fixed and absolute knowledge and that as
individuals we can own that knowledge. Reality however is more complex. Human knowledge is always
partial, always shifting and emerging, with new learning and understanding (Brown, 2021). This is true
within medical research, theory and practice.


Principle 3: Taking action
Accomplices may be cautious, fearing getting it wrong or making mistakes. We recommend being brave
enough to get it wrong; an inadvertent mistake can lead to learning and relationship. However, taking
no action contributes to the complicity of silence (Fredericks et al., 2020). A clear and concise action plan
is useful. Aims, goals, actions, and communications relating to our professional role and scope of practice
can form a strong professional foundation, providing confidence to act (Bruno, 2023).
   Accompliceship actions include standing up against silencing and bullying towards anyone who
exposes racism. Such pushback occurs when we expose ourselves within health institutions as someone
who will not be complicit or ‘white-silenced’ about racism and discrimination. White silencing occurs
frequently within Australian institutions. It materialises as subtle or blatant workplace lateral violence: i.e.
dismissing/denying concerns about racism, scapegoating, gaslighting, bullying, being overlooked for pro-
motion or even the threat of or actual dismissal (Gatwiri et al., 2021)
    I was shocked and appalled at the shift in how staff treated me within a hospital when I outed myself as an
    active accomplice, I myself became the target of discrimination, a huge learning curve in my journey from ally
    to accomplice. (Elizabeth)
                                                                                           Critical Public Health     7


  Rigid power hierarchies are daunting to discover, and workplace bullying is real. Documenting events
and experiences is crucial. Our actions are a role model for others. Younger practitioners, peers, and
potential accomplices can be positively influenced by our knowledge sharing.


Principle 4: Reflection
Critical reflection is central to being a culturally safe practitioner. Without critically reflecting on our cul-
ture and biases, there can be minimal culturally safe practice (Mills et al., 2022). Reflecting on personal/
professional experience and learning across our accompliceship journey is crucial (Rix et al., 2024). Being
aware of unconscious biases and how these impact our professional practice is key to moving beyond
performative allyship (Fredericks et al., 2020; Gatwiri et al., 2021). There may be times of movement and
growth, of frustration, perceptions of roadblocks and when things appear to regress. At these times our
ongoing critical reflection is of greatest assistance.
   A deeper level of learning and understanding results from reflection, enriching our growing knowl-
edge. Keeping a reflective journal supports your accompliceship journey. A newsletter contribution, a
presentation, or an article are opportunities to encourage others. Sometimes sharing the accompliceship
journey triggers deeper reflection for us and can begin a conscious journey for others. Keeping a reflec-
tive journal, also provides supporting evidence in the advent of pushback against our actions as
accomplices.


Conclusion
We end here with an invitation to join us on this lifelong journey of accompliceship. The ongoing strug-
gle for practical equity and social justice requires committed practicing accomplices. Our four principles
for accompliceship aim to invite and encourage. Knowing that ‘Reality is Complex,’ being ‘Ready to Listen
and Learn,’ ‘Taking Action,’ and critical ‘Reflection,’ can provide a foundational pathway. Practical equity
means moving beyond superficial inclusion and box ticking. It is based on authentic relationality with
First Nations peoples. Humanity is at a crucial time in history. Social justice and equity are fundamental
to a positive future for all. It is time to move beyond being threatened by people who are different from
us. Respect for each other and learning to live with cultural diversity are key to a stable and healthy
future for all.
