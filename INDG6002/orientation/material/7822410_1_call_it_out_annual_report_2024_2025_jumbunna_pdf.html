                    INTRODUCTION BY AMELIA WHYMAN
My name is Amelia Whyman, I’m 17 years old. I’m a proud Malyangapa and Wankumarra
woman. I grew up in a small regional town called Wilcanna, in far Western NSW, where I
dance with the Barkinji Baaka dance group and speak up as a young leader for my
community and surrounds as a voice to empower future generations. I moved to Sydney
to build connections and get my voice out there to be more known. I started doing dance
with a well known dance group called ‘Bujja Bujja butterfly Dancers’. I’m working as a
Youth Ambassador with Mounty Aboriginal Youth and Community Services, building an
Aboriginal Youth Peak for NSW, so young people can have more of a voice in decisions
that affect their lives and have a place to be themselves.

What racism have you seen or experienced?
I came to Sydney from Wilcannia in search of better education opportunities that aren’t
available in my hometown. I started at a boarding school in Sydney. I would say there’s a
big racism problem within the boarding community at the school but also throughout all
schools. When I started there, there were problems with feeling culturally involved. It’s
the same for other students. Issues came up for First Nations students in the boarding
house where it’s supposed to be home away from home. How can they feel comfortable
there? It gets hard for them.

I have had racist run-ins. While I was waiting at [the station], this man walked past me,
and he sat this piece of paper on the pole in front of me. This is my spot, this is my paper,
he was calling me the N word, and racially profiling me. That guy was still slinging racial
words to me, while I was walking off. I was only 15 years old. The bystanders all just
watched. It made me feel like shit. I gave him the rude finger while walking off.

What do you do about racism?
I’m one of those people who will call racism out as it happens - if I can. Sometimes I call
it out, and sometimes I don’t because I get really scared. Even online.

It’s not always worth taking someone on who is a racist, you can’t really change their
thoughts. It can bring back all that trauma from generations - because stories get passed
down, especially the racist incidents. You can experience pain that is passed down from
your ancestors, you can get scared for your cultural safety, for your physical safety - you
feel it.

Sometimes when I’m doing cultural dance I feel a bit judged, people whisper. We might
not say anything while we’re dancing as it’s about culture. Other times we have to call it
out.

You can see when you should call it out. Sometimes you know culturally or spiritually
that people need to be called out.

My ancestors connect to me spiritually. There have been so many things over the years
that we as First Nations people weren’t allowed to do, through the stolen generations
and other racist policies. We couldn’t stand up back then but you can now. Sometimes
my ancestors will say this isn’t a safe space for you. Other times they’re saying you
should stand your ground.

A fair few adults and young people want to take action against racism. But some people
would be scared or clueless about how to do it. Some people will walk away and some
people will stand up because they won’t let anyone talk about their culture, others will
go the legal route. A lot of people don’t know the ways to go about it legally - and how they
can get help and handle it.

I help other young people with racism. If they contact me for help, I do some thinking,
then I talk to them and plan with them. So they don’t get in more trouble in how they
respond to racism – whether that happens in their community, in public spaces or
wherever.

At the school I just graduated from, a lot of the girls look up to me as a big sister or leader,
and when they have problems they get in touch with me and ask for advice and guidance.
A fair bit of the advice I give is about racism and how to solve it without making a bigger
problem. They are only young and I talk to them like I am their big sister. I have five
younger siblings. I help them understand the things they can and can’t do and how they
can work around issues to make it a more comfortable space, especially while boarding
at school.

While I was still at school I saw a young First Nations girl in Year 8 standing there crying
and talking to a teacher who wasn’t First Nations. I asked if she was okay. She said she
felt culturally unsafe, uncomfortable and that the school was ‘whitewashed’. I come
from a very cultural community so I knew how she felt. I gave her some support. I didn’t
walk past. I stopped and helped her with her feelings. She liked that I was talking to her,
rather than the teacher. She didn’t feel so alone. It was that connection with me that was
important.

Also, calling racism out is sometimes effective and sometimes not. When we
complained before about racism at school the principal didn’t do much about it. I think
they were blindsided by their relationships with the staff we were complaining about. But
when we have good leadership, like now, I encourage the other students to write down
when something happens. When you feel culturally unsafe, then take it to the principal.
She will listen.

How does racism affect young people?
Racism can affect the mindset of young people and how they see the world. A kid can
grow up in a happy family and then they experience racism and they get confused about
their place in the world. You don’t feel a sense of community. You feel isolated.

                                               8
Everyone has been told about it. But it affects everyone differently when you experience
it.

It can make some people get depressed and separate themselves from society. It can
lead to people feeling like suicide. It can affect their mental state, their physical state -
stop them from eating, drinking - to get skinny, sick.

What needs to be done about racism?
The solutions can come through culture and from First Nations people themselves, but
everyone has a role in addressing racism, recognising and responding to it. Everyone
needs to have an understanding of racism and its effects.

Sometimes at school they do Koori lunch meetings, there’s cultural dancing too, but
there is really nothing else for First Nations students and after these activities, they go
back into a school community where they don’t feel culturally safe. In communities we
exchange cultural stories, learn how to use clapsticks, didgeridoos, boondis and make
spears. We need consistent workshops and whole days in schools around cultural
activities like making clapsticks, learning language, how to do yarning circles and
weaving. This would better connect us with community and help us to feel culturally
connected in a school setting.

There’s Aboriginal Studies at school, but there’s only Indigenous history, rather than
culture. Students could learn weaving, artworks, the stories of the 7 sisters, artefacts and
other things. The First Nations students could come up with murals and explain it to the
non-First Nations students to help them understand cultural connection, and why it’s
needed, why it’s important to keep First Nations students spiritually connected when
they are away from their community.

Young First Nations people definitely need a voice on these issues.

A lot of kids don’t have a voice. I’m a young leader back home, and here I’m also doing
this.

Kids need to stop being shut down. I don’t get why young people are ignored and
neglected all the time. Young people can give a lot of valuable insights, and can help
other people understand how young people work. Our voice is very valuable and very
much needed.

There’s also solutions in coming together. Since I came to Sydney to go to school, and
became independent, I’ve become more confident and had to stand up for myself more.
With some young people from Mt Druitt, I’m helping with creating an Aboriginal Youth
Peak for NSW. In that space, I can just be me, I can be comfortable and talk up. It’s useful
to come together with other young Aboriginal people.




                                             9
There should be other groups like this, where we all learn together as a community by
talking about what each of us have learned - where there’s no conflict and everyone can
be equally understood. We need to create spaces for us to come together in this way, for
culture and safety.

And we also need more safe spaces. A community group where we can talk or write about
racism would be good, and in the corner there’s a box where you can write down how it’s
affected you and put what you write in the box - keep it private. People have different
ways of experiencing racism and this is one way to respond to this.

There are lots of supports out there, but people don’t know about them. If I got a really,
really bad experience of racism, let’s say there was a youth program, I might not feel
comfortable talking about this experience. Maybe I might prefer to put it into a song, a
poem or rhymes, a journal. I’ve experienced racism. I don’t want my kids or their kids to
experience it growing up. Someone might set up a program that people will trust that can
make it safer for First Nations people to grow up.

We need accountability and more education. At school, one student was suspended
after a racist incident against a First Nations student who was affected emotionally, who
didn’t feel safe. Part of me thinks that’s good - they then understand that racism isn’t the
right thing. But sending them back to their family, they’re probably not going to be pulled
up at home.

While they’re suspended I think they should be required to learn about cultural and
spiritual connection, how racism impacts First Nations people - to write an essay about
these things, about why racism shouldn’t exist, why it’s bad. Then they can think about
how to apologise when the First Nations student feels safe enough to hear this. The
school needs to play a role in this, but it would be helpful to have a community member
come in and help navigate.

Police can be corrupt if they arrest someone who is Indigenous or has a cultural
background. Police can be racist too. Just because they’re part of the law doesn't mean
they’re not racist. Someone can be arrested but not know their rights. Police can hurt that
person physically, and treat them badly. Give them a bad experience. Because that
person doesn’t understand the law they wouldn’t know what actions to take to stop that
corrupt cop.

There’s a lot of racism in the justice system. Not everyone is up for taking action on it. We
need a support system for those treated badly by police - where they can learn what their
rights are and how to uphold them. Maybe a community support program where young
people can access legal information and advice before they are likely to get targeted by
police. Things that they can do to support themselves, how to speak, so they won’t be
clueless and all alone when they are in the justice system.

Where do you get your strength from?
I draw my strength from my Elders, my ancestors, especially my dad. He is very strong
minded. We used to do marches across the bridge to have our voice heard. When I was
11, I designed the logo for the Indigenous Party of Australia. We put together the party
because of the issues, because we were being ignored, so we decided to have a voice in
parliament. I learnt my strength from my dad, because he taught me the rights and
wrongs, same as his father did. Same as his nan. They taught me what’s respectful and
what’s not, and as you get older you keep learning.

I had a lot of messages around being ‘staunch’ growing up. I still get those messages.

I cried when I got accepted into school in Sydney as I didn’t want to leave home, leave my
community and make new friends. My dad and brother brought me here. ‘Keep your head
down and focus, don’t look back, you can make changes a lot of kids in Wilcannia don’t
have the chance to do.’

I struggled with the culture shock when I got here. I was really culturally connected back
home but was new to town and there was less culture all around me. Then I started doing
cultural dancing, which helped me a lot. That’s why I think that there should be more that
schools do to help young people with culture shock, to stay connected and not be
overwhelmed by not being around community and culture anymore. It hits them hard.

When I graduated from school, my Uncle said ‘We’re proud of you, you have to keep
going.’ When I graduated, they all congratulated me, but my father had a talk with me.
‘You’ve made us proud. You can’t come back home, you have to stay down there and
make a change.’

I do want to make a change. In a workspace or school area or in any social interactions, I
want for people to look at another person and think they are just another person, I don’t
need to say anything about their cultural background. And that people don’t offend each
other, where they see each other as equals.

1. Introduction

1.1     About the Call It Out Register

The Jumbunna Institute for Education and Research (Jumbunna Research), in
collaboration with the National Justice Project (NJP), developed Call It Out as an online
register (the Register) to record all forms and levels of racism and discrimination
experienced by First Nations peoples.1 Call It Out was officially launched on 21 March
2022 to coincide with the International Day for the Elimination of Racial Discrimination.

Call It Out provides a simple and secure online reporting mechanism without the usual
barriers which often inhibit reports of racism. It is intended to provide First Nations
peoples who have experienced racism in all forms and witnesses to such racism with an
accessible platform on which to share their experiences.

The Register is an independent, Indigenous-controlled reporting mechanism which is
distinct from traditional complaints and legal processes. Reports of racist issues and
incidents to complaints bodies, for instance, may be restricted or limited to specific
forms of racism, and responding to racism through antidiscrimination and other laws and
complaints mechanisms can be difficult to initiate and navigate, with often fairly
prescribed remedies available. In contrast, Call It Out is an ongoing, respondent
generated initiative with a national focus. The Register provides the opportunity to
identify patterns of racism at institutional and structural levels, including to inform
evidence-based advocacy and campaigning initiatives that proactively strive towards
eliminating racism and discrimination towards First Nations peoples in this country.

We encourage anyone who experiences or witnesses racism against Aboriginal and/or
Torres Strait Islander people to make a report using the Call It Out mobile or web app (for
more information visit callitout.com.au). You can also make a report using the print and
digital questions forms, including in Easy Read, available for download on the Call It Out
Resources page.

Racism and discrimination take many forms. They can be direct and indirect,

    •   Support appropriate investment in Indigenous-led anti-racism activities and

    •   Analyse the actions and demographics of perpetrators to better understand how

    •   Measure patterns in the circumstances, nature, frequency and geographic spread

    •   Advocate for law reform and social change.

2.3.3 Laws, policies, practice

Systemic or institutional racism has again been reported with relative frequency to Call
It Out. As discussed earlier, when describing how the incident was experienced or
witnessed, reports of systemic/institutional racism on its own constituted 21% of all
reports, while experiences of both interpersonal and systemic/institutional were
identified in 33% of all reports.14 Thus, taken together, 54% of people described
experiencing the incident as systemic/institutional racism.

system, organisation or institutional policies systematically treat First Nations people
unfairly'. Related to this, this year we included ‘law, policies and practices that lead to
unfair treatment and negative outcomes’ as one category of ‘type of racism’. Just over
one in ten (12%) of all reports were identified as falling into this category (Figure 5 above).

5. Conclusion
This is our third Annual Report on Call it Out. It presents detailed accounts of racism
shared to the Call It Out Register between March 2024 to March 2025. The Register’s
distinctive approach allows not only those who directly experience racism, but also
friends, relatives, and bystanders, to document these events. Call It Out stands as a
unique archive and resource in providing a comprehensive record that amplifies the
voices of those affected and those witnessing racism. As we note in the Introduction,
racism can take many forms. However, the Call it Out archive allows us to see both the
patterns and the particularities of racism against First Nations people. There are many
similarities between the racism identified in this Report with previous years (for example,
workplace racism, online and social media racism) and also particularities in this
reporting period (for example, an increase reports involving First Nations children and
young people).

This Report draws on 442 documented incidents of racism targeting Aboriginal and
Torres Strait Islander people. Over half of these reports (56%) originated from witnesses,
while a third (35%) were submitted by the First Nations people who were directly
impacted. The remaining 9% were reported by friends or relatives. The register’s secure
online reporting system enables individuals to share their experiences, detailing the
emotions, physical impacts, and broader consequences of racism. These personal
narratives are crucial for identifying the multi-layered nature and patterns of racism at all
levels: interpersonal, institutional and structural. Importantly, the narratives document
and help us to understand the extensive and profound effects on those subjected to
racism. They also inform strategies to combat racism.

A recurring theme throughout the incidents of racism analysed in this Report is the urgent
need for accountability and education. Respondents emphasised the importance of
collective efforts, suggesting that more support like Call It Out should be established and
that solutions are rooted in unity and collaboration. The emotional impacts recorded in
the Register are significant and varied: anger, sadness, fear, emptiness, constant shame,
the relentless need to prove oneself, and exhaustion from the persistent weight of
racism. Disrespect and disdain were cited, alongside frustration in professional
environments where individuals felt they were not recognised as ‘Blak enough’. Such
emotional tolls often culminate in moments of vulnerability, such as sitting alone in a car
crying at the end of the day, overwhelmed by the continual need for self-justification.

The cumulative effect of ongoing racism is further illustrated by a First Nations woman
who described feeling invisible while shopping because she failed to receive any
assistance, ‘some may think it's a minor thing but that small act made a big impact on
me and my mental well-being’. It resulted in depression and a sense of isolation. These
experiences are rarely isolated; for many, they represent a daily struggle with little hope
for tangible support. The absence of effective remedies prompts some to ignore racism

                                            108
altogether, as exemplified by a First Nations person who left a government job, citing a
lack of support for Aboriginal people. Nonetheless, the register also documents the
resilience and motivation of individuals who persistently call out racism and actively
support Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in leading solutions to systemic
issues, including over-incarceration.

The report’s guest author with lived experience of racism, 17-year-old Amelia Whyman,
offers a perspective shaped by courage and spiritual connection to her ancestors. Her
narrative shows the generational trauma perpetuated by racism and the importance of
creating safe spaces for collaboration and learning. She expresses a desire for equality
and a future where standing up to racism is no longer necessary. The importance of
education and accountability is reiterated as essential for meaningful change and
healing.

Our analysis reveals that anger is the most common emotional response to racism
across all reports to the Register (22%), followed by disappointment (14%), frustration
(13%), and motivation to bring about change (11%). Those who self-reported incidents of
racism and reports from friends or relatives are more likely to reflect feelings of
humiliation and isolation than those from witnesses. Notably, sadness is the
predominant emotional response to incidents targeting children and young people
(13%), while motivation to instigate change is more frequently identified in cases
involving individuals aged 50 and above (15%).

The nature of racism described in this Report reveals that just over a third of incidents
were experienced as interpersonal racism (34%). Approximately a third (33%) involved
both interpersonal and systemic/institutional racism, while more than one in five (21%)
were categorised as solely systemic/institutional. The Register acknowledges that
racism can manifest across multiple forms and categories, and individuals may relate
their experiences to more than one type. Our Report highlights cases where a single
incident encompasses different forms of racism, such as interpersonal racism
perpetrated by a university student and systemic racism reflected in the institution’s
inadequate response.

Stereotyping emerged as the most prevalent form of racism reported (30%), followed by
verbal abuse or hate speech (17%), bullying, threats or intimidation (12%), discriminatory
laws, policies, or practices (12%), and failure to recognise cultural rights or protocols
(11%). The persistent nature of stereotyping was underscored by its ongoing presence
across various environments. Media representation was key concern, with reports
calling out the negative portrayal and deliberate targeting of First Nations people in news
coverage. Multiple reports detailed the perpetuation of racist stereotypes by
mainstream media, on social media and online platforms, with individuals sharing
screenshots of harmful, violent and deeply racist content circulated online. As



                                           109
highlighted in our earlier Reports, racist attitudes and stereotyping underpin racism in
many contexts in which it arises.

Aggressive racism, comprising verbal abuse (17%), bullying, threats, intimidation (12%),
physical harm (3%), and property damage (1%), accounted for a significant proportion
(33%) of all reported incidents and constituted 35% of ongoing cases. Intimidation and
harassment were evident in personal and real-life contexts, such as a non-Aboriginal
neighbour racially abusing and driving an elderly aunt to leave her public housing.
Shunning and exclusion were identified in reports, particularly in public transport and
commercial spaces. The impacts of such exclusion were profound, leading individuals
to avoid certain places, leave employment, or withdraw from educational settings
following racist incidents.

This year, the Register included ‘law, policies and practices that lead to unfair treatment
and negative outcomes’ as a new category of racism, identified in 12% of all reports.
Witnesses expressed concern about bureaucratic barriers that impede efforts to support
Aboriginal clients. Education policies, particularly in the Northern Territory, were
criticised for failing to account for cultural reasons behind student absences, resulting
in inaccurate records and reduced funding. Criminal laws, such as Victoria’s bail laws,
were also highlighted for their foreseeable adverse effects on First Nations people. In
these examples, racist intent may not always be obvious, although the effects
demonstrate institutional or systemic racism.

Government institutions were criticised for tokenistic efforts, such as establishing
Reconciliation Action Plans and cultural safety frameworks that lack genuine community
engagement and consultation. Mining and health companies were specifically
mentioned for policies that do not align with actual practices. The deceptive practice of
‘Black cladding’, where organisations falsely present themselves as Indigenous owned,
was called out for undermining authentic Aboriginal enterprises and depriving genuine
businesses of opportunities.

The child protection system was also scrutinised for systemic failures in working
inappropriately with First Nations families and communities, as well as non-compliance
with policies designed to protect First Nations rights and cultural recognition. Lack of
accountability remains a persistent issue. Reports again highlighted the non-recognition
of Indigenous sovereignty, cultural, and spiritual connections to land and country, with
an ongoing resistance to acknowledgements and Welcome to Country ceremonies.

Racism was documented across diverse settings, including workplaces, beauty salons,
universities, licensed clubs, saunas, health facilities, public transport and institutions.
In general, social media and online platforms were the most common reported locations
for incidents of racism (22%), followed by workplaces (11%) and educational settings
(10%). However, First Nations people who self reported directly experiencing racism


                                           110
most frequently reported experiences of racism in workplace environments (15%),
followed by social media (13%) and education settings (12%).

Racism was also identified in public settings, in commercial settings and on public
transport. First Nations people travelling on public transport were subject to over-
surveillance and discriminatory behaviour. Aggressive racism accounted for 42% of
public setting incidents, 32% of commercial place incidents, and 45% of public transport
incidents. Shunning and exclusion were most frequently reported in commercial
locations (14%) and public transport (15%). Being subjected to intense scrutiny or over-
visibility in commercial spaces was a recurring theme, leading to avoidance of certain
places and employment withdrawal.

Multiple reports this year addressed racism within the criminal legal system, with
incidents in ‘justice/legal settings’ comprising 6% of all reports. There is a particular
focus in our Report on police responses to First Nations victims of crime, including failure
to investigate assaults, as well as dehumanising practices such as invasive searches,
excessive force, unnecessary use of arrest, racist abuse and targeting, and institutional
neglect of a duty of care. Institutional actions, such as police extinguishing fires at Camp
Sovereignty, exemplified systemic racism and bias in law enforcement. Historic patterns
of exclusion, such as barring young people from public places like swimming pools, were
also referenced.

Many reports to Call It Out included multiple incidents experienced or witnessed by the
same individual, often spanning years and generations. This suggests that the register’s
figures significantly undercount the true prevalence of racism. Of the 369 respondents
to an optional question, just under half (47%) reported multiple incidents, with First
Nations people more likely to do so (54%) than friends/relatives (44%) or witnesses
(43%). More than half of all incidents occurred recently (within the last three months),
while nearly a quarter were ongoing (22%). Friend/relative reports and self-reports were
more likely to identify ongoing racism (33% and 27%, respectively) compared to witness
reports (18%). Aggressive racism constituted 35% of ongoing cases.

Racism targeting First Nations people is entrenched, endemic, and difficult to capture
statistically. The Register’s questions about incident frequency and duration offer some
insight. However, the complexity of lived experiences – of single incidents with multiple
layers, of recurring events across locations, and of intergenerational trauma – requires
ongoing attention. Historical references, such as a taxi driver’s use of the term ‘natives’
and accounts from First Nations women in government workplaces, shops and
supermarkets, and of Aboriginal children being removed from a public swimming pool,
illustrate the persistence of harmful stereotypes and policies of racial segregation from
past to present.




                                            111
A significant finding of our Report was that more than a quarter of all reports to the
Register involved children and young people (aged 0-19) as victims, signalling the
profound impact of racism on this group. There was an increase in reports involving
racism in schools and educational institutions compared to previous years. Media,
social media and online locations (31%) and schools and educational institutions (30%)
were the most commonly identified location involving children and young people.

Children and young people were more likely to experience bullying, threats, intimidation,
physical harm than other age groups. Where gender and age were noted, children and
young people victims were more likely to be male. Children and young people were
proportionally more likely to be targeted by perpetrators in their own age bracket.
Reports to the Register highlighted the failure of schools to respond effectively to racist
behaviour, with repeated complaints going unaddressed. Other incidents included
police targeting children, racist social media posts, and offensive depictions shared
within schools. The emotional impacts for children and young people included anger
(20%), disappointment (16%), frustration (15%), and sadness (13%), with proportionally
higher rates of these type of responses compared to other age groups.

In general, the most commonly identified perpetrators of racism were strangers (26%),
employees or workers (25%), and institutions or organisations as a whole (20%). As noted
above, children and young people were disproportionately affected by racism in schools
(30%) and media/online platforms (31%). For those aged 50 and above, media/online
was also the most common location of racist incidents (31%). Individuals in the 30-39
and 40-49 age brackets were more likely to experience racism in the workplace (20% and
17%, respectively).

The impacts of racism were severe, including homelessness, mental health issues such
as PTSD, and suicidal ideation. The lack of effective responses to challenges posed by
racism contributes to long-term harm, with some individuals reporting suicide attempts,
financial loss, and substance abuse as consequences. The multi-layered impacts of
racism are illustrated by the account of a young First Nations man who was sexually
assaulted in his workplace, resulting in lasting consequences such as loss of trust in
authority, depression, anxiety, suicide attempts, inability to maintain employment,
financial hardship, and substance abuse. These narratives demonstrate the pervasive
and enduring harm caused by racism.

The most frequent response to racism was seeking support from family or friends (26%
females, 24% males), consistent with previous annual reports. Males were more likely to
disengage (14% compared to 9% females), while females were more likely to report
directly to organisations or internal HR (13% compared to 8% males).

The barriers to reporting racism were significant, with the most common being a lack of
trust in the system (16%). Other frequently identified barriers included having reported
before without any action taken (12%), uncertainty about what to do or say (11%), and a

                                           112
belief that reporting is futile (10%). For incidents targeting children and young people,
distrust of the system was the most common barrier (19%), while fear of job loss was
more prevalent among adults aged 30-39 (11%). These interconnected barriers –
distrust, perceived futility, and previous inaction – collectively accounted for 38% of
responses and reflect systemic failures to address racism adequately.

Solutions identified for combating racism included First Nations Sovereignty (self-
determination, land back, reparations) and increasing understanding of First Nations
culture and history (both 14%). In combination with stronger legal protections and
penalties for racism, these strategies comprised over a third of all responses (40%). For
reports from friends or relatives, the most common solutions were educating the
community on how to stand up against racism (16%), stronger legal protections (14%),
and increasing public awareness about the impacts of racism on First Nations people
(12%). Age-based differences were observed in preferred solutions: those aged 40-49
prioritised stronger legal protections (13%), while those aged 20-29 equally emphasised
education campaigns and sovereignty (both 16%).

The shared responsibility for combating racism was evident throughout the reports, with
some individuals reluctant to act and others actively engaging through listening, learning,
and speaking up. Calls for enhanced protections and stricter penalties were reiterated,
including more severe consequences for perpetrators. Public education and awareness
strategies accounted for 39% of responses, encompassing increased understanding of
First Nations culture (14%), community education on anti-racism (10%), public
awareness campaigns (7%), legal and ethical responsibilities (5%), and empowering
First Nations people to use the law (3%). Prioritising First Nations sovereignty and
amplifying First Nations-led responses was highlighted as a key strategy (34%).

Our Report affirms that the struggle against racism is collective and ongoing, demanding
accountability, education, and the amplification of First Nations voices. The
commitment to truth-telling, recognition of generational trauma, and the pursuit of
solutions led by First Nations people are essential for a future where equality and respect
prevail. The enduring message of the call it out report is clear: the elimination of racism
against Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people requires determined action, shared
responsibility, and First Nation led solutions and accountability in responding to racism.

Call It Out is committed to ensuring every voice and story is heard. The individual stories
shared each year not only highlight the prevalence of racism across all spaces in society,
but also addresses the importance of unity, accountability, and education. The ultimate
conclusion of our Report this year is that while the evidence of racism is overwhelming,
the pathway to elimination lies in the collective societal commitment to support those
who speak out, translating their experiences into irreversible systemic change and
effective and enforced processes for accountability. Amelia Whyman clearly and
powerfully defined at the start of this report the necessary path forward, the ideal


                                           113
environment where: ‘People look at another person and think they are just another
person, I don’t need to say anything about their cultural background, and that people
don’t offend each other – where they see each other as equals’. Amelia’s vision demands
an unwavering commitment of every individual to challenge and call out racism.
