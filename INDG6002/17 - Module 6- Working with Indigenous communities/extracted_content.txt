Module overview
-
-
Your final Module this week provides information on the strong connections between Indigenous health and wellbeing perspectives and the principles of Primary Health Care and Health Promotion. The origins and principles of Indigenous health frameworks are discussed by way of two key historical documents.  Aboriginal Health Workers' roles are described. The Aboriginal Community Controlled Health sector’s role as a world leader in early Primary Health Care is acknowledged.
Your final reading this week confirms why Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples' Country, family and culture are profoundly related to optimal health and wellbeing. Recognising and comprehending the circular nature and interconnection of the different aspects of your learning journey in this Unit is important for holding new perspectives.
Module purpose
By the end of this module you will be able to:
- define and discuss Primary Health Care and Health Promotion and principles
- understand how and why these models of health resonate so strongly with Indigenous worldviews on health and wellbeing
- describe the work and roles of Aboriginal Health Workers and Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations
- comprehend the interconnected learning and topics from Modules 1–6 of this unit.

Introduction
-
-
This chapter reframes how health professionals understand and engage with Indigenous communities by shifting the focus from deficit and disadvantage to Indigenous capacity, resilience, and cultural vitality. It provides a critical foundation for working with communities in culturally safe, strengths‑based, and self‑determining ways (Taylor & Thompson Guerin, 2019, Chapter 9).
Reflection:
As you read this chapter, reflect on how deficit‑based assumptions can shape health programs, professional roles, and community relationships.
- Where do you see strengths and capacity being overlooked in current health systems?
- How does a strengths‑based orientation challenge common ideas about “helping” Indigenous communities?
Primary Health Care is a social view of health, recognising that health is much more that the absence of disease. Primary Health Care (PHC) emerged in the 1970s as a new and somewhat radical approach aimed at addressing inequities in health globally. PHC’s core principles are: essential, accessible, acceptable and affordable healthcare that is not top-down, but rather based in and directed by community. These core principles have particular relevance for the world’s Indigenous Peoples and other marginalised populations in less developed countries. PHC’s holistic and social view of health fits well within an Indigenous worldview (Taylor & Thompson Guerin, 2019, p. 134).
Health Promotion (HP) compliments PHC, with a focus on empowering people to increase control over their health. Health Promotion identifies obstacles for the adoption of healthy public health policies, with the aim of placing health at the top of the agenda of policymakers. There is a significant overlap between the principles of Primary Health Care (PHC) and Health Promotion (HP) and the concepts of empowerment and self-determination for Indigenous Peoples. The 1978 Declaration of Alma-Ata called on all governments and the world community to protect and promote the health of all people of the world (WHO,1978).
Both PHC and HP are integrally linked to the social determinants of health (explored in Module 3), which the World Health Organization (WHO) view as the main cause of health inequities experienced by marginalised groups, including Indigenous Peoples (WHO, 2013).
Definitions of PHC & HP
Primary Health Care is a whole-of-society approach to health that aims at ensuring the highest possible level of health and wellbeing and their equitable distribution by focusing on people’s needs and as early as possible along the continuum from health promotion and disease prevention to treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care, and as close as feasible to people’s everyday environment.
— (WHO and UNICEF. A vision for primary health care in the 21st century: Towards UHC and the SDGs https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/primary-health-care)
Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health. To reach a state of complete physical, mental, and social wellbeing, an individual or group must be able to identify and to realize aspirations, to satisfy needs, and to change or cope with the environment. Health is, therefore, seen as a resource for everyday life, not the objective of living. Health is a positive concept emphasizing social and personal resources, as well as physical capacities. Therefore, health promotion is not just the responsibility of the health sector, but goes beyond healthy lifestyles to wellbeing.
— (Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, 1986)


===== FILE: 03 - Why Primary Health Care-.epub =====
Why Primary Health Care?
-
-
According to the World Health Organisation:
All people, everywhere, deserve the right care, right in their community. This is the fundamental premise of primary health care. http://www.emro.who.int/health-topics/primary-health-care/
Primary health care (PHC) addresses the majority of a person’s health needs throughout their lifetime. This includes physical, mental and social wellbeing and it is people-centred rather than disease-centred. PHC is a whole-of-society approach that includes health promotion, disease prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care.
A primary health care approach includes three components:
- meeting people’s health needs throughout their lives;
- addressing the broader determinants of health through multisectoral policy and action; and
- empowering individuals, families, and communities to take charge of their own health.
By providing care in the community as well as care through the community, PHC addresses not only individual and family health needs, but also the broader issue of public health and the needs of defined populations.
The principles of Primary Health Care (PHC) cite a set of values and attributes that are most relevant to, and resonate with Indigenous Peoples and communities across the globe. The PHC model aims to deliver healthcare that is:
- Accessible for all people no matter where they reside, with no barriers to accessing services
- Acceptable and culturally safe for, no matter what their cultural background or beliefs
- Is community driven, enabling full participation – in other words, is not managed or controlled in a ‘Top Down’ manner
- Affordable and is not based on treatments and interventions that are high-cost and highly reliant on expensive technologies.
Image source: Ampilatwatja Health Centre Aboriginal Corporation – https://www.ampilatwatja.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/primary.jpg
PHC, because it is about how best to provide health care and services to everyone, everywhere, is the most efficient and effective way to achieve health for all.
Primary health care is designed and intended as the ‘first contact’ a person has with the health care system in Australia. It should be the gateway to health care services for individuals, families and the community, where they develop a relationship with their general practitioner (GP) who can provide comprehensive and continuous care. Ideally for Indigenous Australians, PHC should enable access to GPs, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Workers and Practitioners, nurses, allied health professionals, midwives, pharmacists and dentists. The services provided as part of primary health care are broad, and include health promotion and education, prevention and screening, early intervention, treatment and management (WHO, n.d.).

===== FILE: 06 - Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations.epub =====
Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations
-
-
In this week’s tutorial we will explore how approximately 150 Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations provide PHC and HP services that are culturally safe, accessible, and acceptable healthcare services for Indigenous Peoples across Australia.
ACCHOs – pre-dated the Alma-Ata 1978 Declaration of Primary Health Care
The first Australian Aboriginal Controlled Medical Service (AMS) was opened at Redfern, NSW in 1971. The primary health care approach adopted by Redfern and other early Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs) was innovative and mirrored international trends at the time for accessible, effective, appropriate, needs-based health care with a prevention and social justice focus (National Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation, 1992-2022). The opening of Redfern AMS pre-dated and foreshadowed the World Health Organizations’ Alma-Ata 1978 Declaration on Primary Health Care, which stated:
The existing gross inequality in the health status of the people particularly between developed and developing countries as well as within countries is politically, socially and economically unacceptable and is, therefore, of common concern to all countries.
— (WHO, 1978)
In view of this, the Australian Aboriginal Community Controlled Health movement can be viewed as world leaders in addressing the healthcare needs of vulnerable and disadvantaged peoples in First World nations.

===== FILE: 09 - Summary.epub =====
Summary
-
-
In this final module we have presented the principles of Primary Health Care and Health Promotion in the context of mirroring Indigenous perspectives on health and wellbeing. Primary health care’s focus on accessible and acceptable health services that are culturally safe and target the needs of a particular community, reflect the health and social needs of Indigenous Peoples. The principles of Health Promotion such as empowerment, strengthening community action, and reorienting health services originated from the early Aboriginal Community Controlled Health sector in Redfern and beyond. The crucial role of Aboriginal Health Workers within their communities is highlighted, along with the leading role that Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations play in leading the way for PHC and HP initiatives aimed at delivering health equity for vulnerable and disadvantaged people in Australia and in other First World nations.
