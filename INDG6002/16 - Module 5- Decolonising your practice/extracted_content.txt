===== FILE: 02 - Module overview.epub =====
Module overview
-
-
What does the term decolonisation mean to you? Until recently this term was only known predominantly in academic environments. It is now becoming more familiar across western nations, with the recent emergence of the Decolonisation movement globally. Decolonisation is integrally linked to improving the health and wellbeing of Indigenous Peoples. This unit has already discussed how the violence of the colonisation of Australia continues to profoundly impact Aboriginal and Torres Strait islander peoples health and wellbeing.
In this module we define and exemplar what decolonisation is and what it may look like in the context of your professional practices. You will watch Indigenous scholar Professor Michael Yellow Bird explain how all of our brains are colonised by western culture and concepts. Your reading this week provides a social worker’s perspective on decolonising practices.
Within Australia the ongoing ‘problematising’ and ‘othering’ of Indigenous Peoples by media organisations is known as a significant barrier to decolonising our ways of doing things. These deficit-based approaches are countered by the words of Aboriginal health workers’ perspectives (Green & Bennett, 2018) on how to work in a decolonising and culturally respectful way with her peoples.
Optional reading
(Full book available as PDF)

Module purpose
By the end of this module, you will be able to:
- define and discuss what ‘Decolonising your practice’ means in the context of working with Indigenous Australian Peoples
- expand on and deepen your understanding of the relationship between colonisation and the health and wellbeing of Australia’s Indigenous Peoples
- begin to practice critical self-reflection and reflexivity for decolonising your own thinking and practice while working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander clients in all health service and support environments.
Introduction
According to Indigenous scholars Walter & Baltra-Ulloa (2019), the future of social work in Australia depends of how we address the violence of colonisation. For health and social care professionals, this requires a ‘decolonising’ of your thinking and approach while working with Indigenous Australian Peoples.
Decolonising your practice as a healthcare or social worker begins with acknowledging, counter to narratives based on a deficit lens, the ‘problem’ is not Indigenous peoples. When it comes to disadvantage and inequity the 'mainstream' or dominant cultural group (non-Indigenous Australians) must recognise Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are not the cause of 'the problem'. The ‘problem’ is colonialism and how colonial systems maintain, perpetuate, and permeate every aspect of Australian society (Green & Bennett, 2018).
The principles of cultural safety framework (discussed in Module 4) enables health and social work professionals to contribute to reversing the impacts of the dominant cultural group on both Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander clients and colleagues. Decolonising practices requires recognition that race is a feature of human relationships, and that the dominance of ‘whiteness’ across all societies has created power imbalances for all peoples rooted in Indigenous cultures.
In practice, decolonising requires us to acknowledge and negate the power and privilege that comes with embracing only one way of knowing, being and doing (western culture) over all 'other' cultures (Walter & Baltra-Ulloa, 2019).
“Learning to unlearn is about silence, deep listening, learning to let go of control, learning to let go of the familiar, comfortable position of power. Guided by the relationships we develop with the people we work with, practice becomes about being genuine, humble, learning to blend ideas, explore possibilities and venturing into the creation of new ways. Nothing will feel like a firm fit anymore and yet everything will be, because the focus turns to the relationship at the heart of the practice”.
(Walter & Baltra-Ulloa, 2019, p. 77)


===== FILE: 03 - So what is decolonisation-.epub =====
So what is decolonisation?
-
-
Decolonisation is the process by which a country that was previously a colony (controlled by another country) becomes politically independent. In the Australian context, however, decolonisation also refers to the ongoing struggle “to recognize, legitimize, and rebuild subjugated Indigenous knowledges, cultures, values, governance, and social and economic approaches, also referred to as re-Indigenization (Bennett et al., 2025, p. 2). In order to decolonise ourselves in Australia we must acknowledge that settler colonialism has created and embedded a racist and damaging culture within health and social services and that these ways of knowing and doing are at the core of health and social inequities for Indigenous Peoples.
Further Bennett et al. (2025) state that meaningful decolonial leadership requires explicit acknowledgement of history, critical reflexivity about colonial harms, and intentional processes of truth‑telling and cultural resurgence, emphasising that organisations must “foreground Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing  [knowledges]” (p. 12) and expose “colonizing and racist assumptions underpinning praxis” (p. 6) as part of building emancipatory practice.
Definition:
Decolonisation was defined for health and social workers by Indigenous social work scholar, Michael Yellow Bird, a member of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara tribes from North Dakota. He defines it as:
"… a process that begins with an understanding that one is colonised (at whatever level that may be). [Decolonisation] is creating and consciously using strategies to liberate oneself from [oppression]. It is the restoration of cultural practices, thinking, beliefs and values that were taken away or abandoned but are still relevant or necessary for survival…it is the birth and use of new ideas, thinking, technologies and lifestyles that contribute to the advancement and empowerment of all people" (Yellow Bird, 2008, p. 284).


===== FILE: 04 - How might decolonising your practice look and feel-.epub =====
How might decolonising your practice look and feel?
-
-
Obviously, in order to decolonise your practice, you need to first decolonise your own lens on working with Indigenous Peoples. In Module 4 we explored cultural safety as the appropriate and comprehensive framework to achieve this. Cultural safety is a tool to dismantle and unsettle the dominant ways of thinking about health and social care. A decolonising of your practice inevitably includes the examination of the omnipresent existence of race that exists in human relationships (Walter & Baltra-Ulloa, 2019).
Never under-estimate the power and privilege that comes with being a university educated professional and a member of the dominant culture in this country. Decolonising your practice requires an ability to acknowledge this, and how you work towards reclaiming trust and address the fear, loss of identity, loss of power and destruction of culture and families experienced by Indigenous Peoples since the arrival of the colonisers.
"Decolonisation is not a simple act, it is a journey that requires more than simple words and cannot be achieved in a short time frame.”
(Green & Bennett, 2018 p. 262)
This aligns with Rix et al. (2023, p. 6), who caution that “Positive rhetoric in policies and guidelines in no way guarantees a shift in practice”
Taken together, these perspectives highlight the gap that often exists between intention and transformation, reinforcing that decolonising practice demands long‑term, relational, and structural change rather than short‑term or performative responses.

Australian media representation of ‘the Aboriginal problem’
-
-
Mainstream media in Australia has used a negative and problematising perspective in all reporting relating to Indigenous Peoples. According to McCausland (2004):
“There is an institutional racism in the media, an entrenched culture that represents issues and people in particular ways”. (p. 84)
Those working in the media have the power to marginalise and construct Indigenous Peoples and their communities as the ‘other’, associated with criminality or conflict. Racism and stereotyping in the media has been and remains systemic and institutionalised. In the digital age, this bias and negative portrayal continues. Research has shown this negative media stereotyping extends to the origins and outcomes of poor health for Indigenous Peoples. A 2014 study found that 74% of media articles on Indigenous health in Australia were negative, and ‘othering’ with devastating effects. Racism perpetrated by influential figures in our society, including media storytellers, fuels racist attitudes and negatively impacts the self-esteem of many Indigenous Peoples, especially children and young people (Stoneham, Goodman & Daube, 2014). More damaging, however, is the potential for this to create and further embed fear and avoidance of much-needed health and social support services for Indigenous Peoples.
“the more the dominant culture reports negative stories about Aboriginal people, the more that Aboriginal children feel bad about being Aboriginal”.
(Fiona Stanley, in Sweet, 2009)
It is evident that this process of decolonisation is required across all mainstream domains in Australia, including journalism and the media. There is an obvious need to continue to challenge the cultural norms, values, and assumptions, that drive this deficit lensed approach. Journalists and media organisations need to take more responsibility for counteracting the negative impacts of this systematic negative media portrayal of Indigenous Peoples.
Even the inclusion of Cultural Safety for Indigenous People into AHPRA’s Nurses Code of Practice in 2018 was met with misinformation, ignorance, and even blatant untruths within some media sources, as evidenced in this 5.35 minute video clip.

Summary
-
-
This Module has defined decolonisation and provided rationale and context for decolonising your perspective on working with Indigenous Peoples as clients in health and social care settings. Your understanding of decolonising professional practice has been expanded by a short presentation/lecture from a North American Indigenous scholar and social worker. These settings are highly relevant to health and social care professionals, as this is where many of you are likely to encounter and work with Indigenous Peoples.
